# To Rama, Who Is Said to Be Vishnu's Incarnation!

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. The verified article heading/contents witness is `விஷ்ணு அவதாரம் எனப்படும் ராமனிடம்!`; the separate scan-63 body phrase is `விஷ்ணு அவதாரம் என்பதும் ராமனிடமே`. English preserves that distinction rather than making the Tamil witnesses identical. Source-specific or culturally marked forms such as **shraddha feast** and **rasabhasa** are kept close to the source rather than silently replaced with outside interpretation.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 61 / printed 59 -->

So far, 69 chapters of *Chakravarthi's Son*, written by Achariyar, have appeared. From among them, replies have from time to time been given to the story appearing in 41 chapters and to Achariyar's opinions. Neither Achariyar nor the Ramayana tigers—none of them—has come forward with a rebuttal. They themselves know that even if they did come, they could not win the argument, and that those who come forward in that way do not possess even the slightest trace of truth.

We are once again continuing *Chakravarthi's Son* so that the people of the country may understand the true state of affairs—and so that we may show how much crookedness there is in Achariyar's words. We shall review together, over a week or two, the articles from Chapters 42 through 69, and afterwards continue writing from time to time.

Carrying Rama's sandals, Bharata departed from Chitrakuta hill. Rama's party left the slopes of Chitrakuta to find another place in Dandakaranya. For ten years they lived comfortably in Dandakaranya without any disturbance. Before that, Rama set out to have darshan of Agastya. At this point Achariyar describes Agastya's greatness. Look:

<!-- Tamil source: scan 62 / printed 60 -->

Two demons named Vatapi and Ilvala had been greatly troubling the rishis. However much Vatapi's body was cut into pieces, it would join together again and come back to life; he had received such a boon. Ilvala would invite rishis to his house saying it was for a “shraddha feast”—if invited, they had to go. Vatapi would turn into a goat. Ilvala would slaughter the goat and serve the feast. The rishis would eat until their bellies were swollen. After they had eaten, Ilvala would call, “Vatapi!” Vatapi, who had gone into the Brahmin's stomach as goat meat, would regain his whole body, tear open the stomach and come out. The Brahmin rishi would fall dead. Such men invited Agastya and gave him a feast. Vatapi entered Agastya's stomach as goat meat. After he had eaten, Ilvala called “Vatapi” as usual. But Agastya digested Vatapi inside his stomach and also burned Ilvala.

When a Ramayana play shows sages eating meat, the little fellows who flare up in anger—how they are going to deny the truths written by this great man, I do not know. Vatapi turned into goat meat, entered their stomachs and killed them—how can it be fitting to say that men unable to detect the plot mixed into the goat meat were rishis who knew all three times? Even if one says that they were forcibly taken away and made to eat, does not one feel like asking: Agastya, powerful enough to destroy Vatapi, could have understood it the very moment they invited him and burned both of them then and there! Instead of destroying them that way—if, under the name of mesha (goat) meat, he ate human flesh (in his own terminology, Rakshasa flesh) and digested that too, does it not become clear that the rishis of those days were not people who shrank from eating human flesh!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 63 / printed 61 -->

Leave that aside—Rama had darshan of such an Agastya. He said that he was going to Dandakaranya to destroy the Rakshasas. At once Agastya gave him the bow made by Visvakarma for Mahavishnu, the quiver of arrows that would never run short, and the sword, and blessed him: “Rama! Destroy the Rakshasas! Earlier Mahavishnu gave these weapons. You too bear them and destroy them!”

(Notice the contradiction. Rama is said to be an incarnation of Mahavishnu. Achariyar himself, who said Rama was not an incarnation of God, again insisted that Rama was indeed an incarnation-man. Now he has begun muddling it all up!

<!-- Source note: the Tamil witness opens a parenthesis before `முரண்பாட்டைக் கவனியுங்கள்` and supplies no visible closing parenthesis before the article continues. English preserves the unmatched opening rather than silently repairing it. -->

And to that very Rama who is supposed to be Vishnu's incarnation, Agastya hands the bow and arrow saying, “Here; Vishnu gave this.” If even Devarishi Agastya, who knows all three times, did not know who Rama was, is that not a great wonder!

When speaking about Agastya in Chapter 44, Achariyar says, “That sage knew even the things that were going to happen.” If such a man says to Rama, “Here, the bow Vishnu gave!” is it not amusing indeed?

Surpanakha comes to the Panchavati ashram. Valmiki, it seems, introduces Surpanakha in her own form. Kambar, it seems, changed it so that Surpanakha came like a beautiful maiden and tried to deceive Rama and Lakshmana. At this point Achariyar praises Kambar greatly. Perhaps the scene as Valmiki wrote it is embarrassing even to him.

Seeing Surpanakha speaking out of the thirst of lust, Rama says, “Beautiful one! Desiring me will bring you trouble. This woman—my wife—is here with me. Two wives will give rise to business trouble. You know that, do you not? Here is my younger brother; he is alone. And in

<!-- Tamil source: scan 64 / printed 62 -->

strength too he is my equal. He is a husband suited to you. Ask him. Leave me alone.” When she goes to Lakshmana, he sends her back to Rama saying, “Go and become elder brother's second wife—do not be afraid of Sita! Rama's love will surge only towards you—he will neglect Sita. You can live happily.”

Achariyar praises Rama and Lakshmana as incarnation-men—noble sons—men who do not speak evil words—men of virtuous character. Such gems among men—and that too an elder brother and younger brother—behave towards a woman in such an improper manner. That is why Achariyar says Kambar wrote the Surpanakha scene beautifully. What does that mean? Is it not that Valmiki states without hiding that Rama and his younger brother behaved in a **rasabhasa** manner, while Kambar concealed it!

Look—Achariyar fastens a “splint” onto Valmiki too:

“Some intellectuals may ask, ‘Can a woman—a woman deeply in love—be tormented in all these ways?’ If they place a grotesque form before themselves and use their power of imagination, they will not ask so.” This is Achariyar's justification.

If a woman came in a grotesque form, men would simply move away from her; if she said that she was in love, they would simply say it was not possible and leave. No man with decent feeling would descend into degrading mockery in which the younger brother says, “Go to elder brother,” and the elder brother says, “Go to younger brother!”—each competing with the other like this, and doing so with the wife standing right beside them. Perhaps, according to Aryan culture, Achariyar's justification may be fitting.
