# Father and Son—Both Strayed from Dharma!

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. The source-visible name `ஜாம்பாலி` is retained as **Jambali** rather than silently normalised to another epic-name form. The unusual source form `நாதிகமுள்ளவர்கள்` is retained below through the source-bearing term **naathigam** rather than being assigned a confident meaning not supplied by this article. The source's opening Chitrakuta quotation uses mixed quotation-mark forms; its scope is clear, so English uses normal quotation typography while the source anomaly is recorded in the review ledger.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 55 / printed 53 -->

On the slopes of Chitrakuta hill, as Achariyar has written, “Sita! Look there at the birds playing! Look at the mountain rocks! See how the minerals in them shine blue, yellow and red! Look at the plants and creepers in the forest! Look at the flowers. What all had we imagined! How delightful this forest exile is. How much more pleasurable this forest life is than life in the city”—while Chakravarthi's Son and Janaki were conversing like this, passing their time happily and “suffering,” Bharata, the three queens and the others arrived. Unable even to find out whether Bharata was coming for war, the Lakshmana incarnation leaps up in haste. Bharata and the others who arrived were distressed on seeing Rama. Only after they told him that Dasaratha had died did Rama, the incarnation of Mahavishnu, come to know of it! After learning it, they completed the duties that had to be performed for the father.

When Kausalya Devi and Sumitra heard that Lakshmana regularly fetched the water they needed from the river Mandakini there, they supposedly wept. Why would Kausalya have wept? Perhaps because in the palace water and rosewater were available whenever one asked, while here her son was “suffering” by drinking river water!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 56 / printed 54 -->

Do you know what Kausalya says to console Sumitra as she weeps? Read it in Achariyar's own style.

“Sumitra! Look, for my son's sake your son goes down into the river at this bathing place every day, brings clean water and leaves it at the ashram. Your son will happily do any work for his elder brother!”

Kausalya says this and consoles her. As though praising Lakshmana's greatness, the elder woman tries to conceal his hardships from his mother.

When we think that Lakshmana himself did even important chores such as fetching water, we do not know what to think of Sita. Sita Devi, absorbed in Rama's embrace and enjoying the pleasures of the forest, somehow allowed her “younger brother-in-law” “Lakshmana” to take a pot and go fetch water. Even women suffering from ill health would not allow the men of their household to do kitchen work, fetch water and perform such chores. But Sita Devi is the ornament of chastity! An example, it seems, of the glory of womanhood in ancient Bharata! And she simply watched while Lakshmana did such work.

Leave that aside. Achariyar praises the scene in which Bharata and Rama meet in the forest as a scene of the greatest grandeur—let us look at that scene a little.

“Bharata must rule the kingdom; Rama must go to the forest for fourteen years”—that was the father's command. Keep these lines clearly in mind when thinking about this scene. When Bharata asks Rama to return,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 57 / printed 55 -->

do you know what answer Rama gives? “Father gave you the responsibility of the kingdom—he gave me forest exile. In this way he laid out life for both of us and died.” Look at how much anger against Dasaratha must have been in Rama's heart. The father made one rule the country and the other rule the forest, and thus “laid out life” for both of them—Rama serves up his irritation mixed with mockery.

When a man called Jambali urged Rama somehow to come to Ayodhya, Rama says:

“You speak atheism, treating truth as though it were of no importance. There is nothing in the world higher than truth.” This is what Rama says. He says that after the father ordered Bharata to be crowned, it would not be right to violate that order. There is nothing higher than truth; to violate it is atheism, he says. If so, is violating the promise—the truth—given to the king of Kekaya alone not atheism? Is there truth in praising that Dasaratha as Dharma Raja and an upright king? Will Achariyar come forward to answer this? I ask those who carry the Rama story on their heads and dance like Bharata—can they deny that Dasaratha was indeed one who broke his word of truth? There is no use gathering like a flock of sheep and shouting “Govinda!” We ask those with *naathigam* who can honestly oppose the arguments we make to come forward; they must explain whether Dasaratha was an atheist who broke the truth or not—will they come? Do they have courage? Do they have evidence?

Leave that. Let us look for a moment at the beauty of how Rama too “protected” the truth.

Bharata called him—Rama refused to come. Vasishta called him—he refused to come. Why? Can we believe that Rama had no desire to return to Ayodhya and take up the burden of rule; that he did not wish to seize the kingdom his father had given his younger brother—and therefore refused to come? No—not at all! When Rama, Dasaratha, Vasishta and the others conducted a conspiracy and secretly arranged to crown Rama, Kaikeyi broke it and sent him to the forest—there lies the catch! Had she, like Bharata, pleaded with Rama to come back, Rama would certainly have come. Bharata said he would commit suicide—said he would enter the fire—cried and said all manner of things; but those who had to call him did not call him, and Rama did not return to Ayodhya. That is the truth!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 58 / printed 56 -->

In truth, no one can say that Rama had no desire to sit as king in Ayodhya. Had he had no such desire, he would not have crowned himself king after fourteen years. Here Rama becomes an atheist who violated his father's command—the truth. The father had given the kingdom to Bharata, had he not? Who said that after fourteen years Rama himself should rule the country again? Even Kaikeyi, who asked for the boon, did not say so! How can Rama's taking the country that belonged to Bharata, in violation of the truth, be justified? Look—Achariyar himself writes how much conspiratorial intent lay in what Vasishta and Rama said in order to get the country back.

Rama says: “Younger brother, regard it as a kingdom given by me; accept it and act according to father's word!”

Did you see the right Rama indirectly claims over the kingdom? By the promise Dasaratha made, Bharata is the one entitled to the country! But Rama, very deftly—very gracefully—tells Bharata that it is “the country given by me”! The country that the father had declared to be Bharata's, he calls his own—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 59 / printed 57 -->

what courage, what capacity to forget the truth, Rama of Ayodhya—the incarnation—must have had! Look at Vasishta speaking!

“Bharata! With Rama's authority, administer the kingdom on behalf of Chakravarthi's Son! If you do that, there will be no blame—and the truth too will be protected.”

The sage has spoken like this! Think of how wonderfully he has instructed him! What a great conspiracy this is! Vasishta was one of those who first conspired to crown Rama; now that same Vasishta shows him a way to establish Rama's right over Ayodhya. Even if Rama himself is absent, let his authority remain—let it be made clear that it is Rama's kingdom that is being conducted in Ayodhya: that is the sage's plan! Accordingly, Bharata placed Rama's sandals on the throne and administered the kingdom from Nandigrama. We ask: what does this mean? Did Rama violate his father's word—the truth—or did he not?

Rama did not sit on the throne; his sandals sat there.

“O Dasaratha Raja! Wretch who happened to become my father! You said I must not sit on the throne—here I am, pretending to protect that truth while placing my own sandals on the throne. Your truth—your command—all of it is equal to this sandal.”

Was it not as though Rama issued such a challenge when he said that his sandals should rule in his place? Is this not to be called Rama's rule? Does it not mean that he violated his father's word? Today, is Nehru sitting in Chennai and ruling? He is in Delhi; here his authority is exercised through Governor Prakasa! In the same way Rama

<!-- Tamil source: scan 60 / printed 58 -->

was in the forest—and in Ayodhya his “holy” sandals ruled as his official representative. We say it a thousand times: Rama violated his father's word—violated it! Those with honesty in their hearts—the crores of devotees eager to protect Rama—Achariyar's disciples—can they prove that Rama did not violate his father's word? The conspirator Rama triumphed in the manner of: “What? You say I must not rule? I shall make my sandals rule!” And the sage Vasishta, who shows special affection at the very words “Kausalya's son,” planted Rama's authority firmly in Ayodhya and planted the flag of victory through audacious, conspiracy-laden speech. Achariyar cannot deny this.
