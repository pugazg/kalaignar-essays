# Chakravarthi's Son

> **Translator identification note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** In this publication, Kalaignar's recurring `ஆச்சாரியார்` refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji). The translated body preserves Kalaignar's chosen label as **Acharya**. The Tamil `சக்கரவர்த்தி` also means “emperor” and, in this article, participates in Kalaignar's wordplay around Rajagopalachari/Chakravarthi; the English therefore retains **Chakravarthi** in the title and where that wordplay is active.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 9 / printed 7 -->

Readers already know that Acharya has been writing serially in *Kalki* under the title *Chakravarthi's Son*. Ten articles have appeared so far. Just like Vishnu's ten mutually contradictory avatars, Acharya's ten articles stand contradicting one another. In this issue I shall give only an overall criticism of those Dasavatara articles—and then, week after week, on Acharya's—that is, Chakravarthi's—son, *Murasoli* will follow Acharya, see how he takes one crooked path after another to keep his race alive, and from time to time tell you what it sees.

Dasaratha's Ashvamedha sacrifice was conducted and completed with every splendour. Then divine payasam was granted for Dasaratha's wives to drink. Dasaratha's three consorts divided the divine payasam among themselves, drank it, and conceived. Acharya describes it. Read!

“According to the proportion of payasam each drank, a share of Vishnu is assigned to these four princes. Rama is said to be one-half of Vishnu; Lakshmana, one-quarter of Vishnu; and Bharata and Shatrughna, one-eighth each.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 10 / printed 8 -->

Those are Acharya's words in the *Chakravarthi's Son* article in the 6 June 1954 issue of *Kalki*. The same Acharya had already answered the June 6 article on May 23. On June 6 he says Rama is an incarnation of Vishnu. Fifteen days earlier he had answered the question whether Rama was an incarnation. Read that too.

“The Rama seen in Valmiki's *Ramayana* is an excellent prince, a heroic man, a man of rare qualities. That is all; he is not God.”

“In the epic of the sage Valmiki, Rama is a great man; he was not written as an incarnation of Ishvara.”

**(Acharya in *Kalki*, May 23.)**

*Chakravarthi's Son* is one serial article. One man writes it—and he too is a Chakravarthi—and that very man contradicts himself to this extent between May 23 and June 6. In his article in *Kalki* on July 18 he states:-

“There is nowhere in old Tamil literature any evidence for giving Tamils the new label, ‘You are the very people who were called demons in the Puranas.’ It is pitiable that some people today, out of ignorance, think and speak of them as Tamils and as their own forefathers.”

To think and say that those called demons were one's own forefathers is ignorance, it seems! Ignorance, comrades, ignorance! The pride-filled Acharya, who imagines himself an ocean of knowledge, declares it ignorance! I wish to give him Pandit Nehru's own words as the answer.

“To tell the truth, it may be said that the story of the *Ramayana* is about the war that took place between the Aryans and the Dravidians when the Aryans invaded the South—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 11 / printed 9 -->

The *Mahabharata* does not speak of a war between Aryans and Dravidians; it speaks of a war between Aryans and Aryans. Even today, it is the Dravidians who continue to live in South India.”

Pandit Nehru wrote the above in 1928 in a letter to his daughter Indira while he was in Mussoorie in the Himalayas. These truths can be found in the book *Letters of Jawaharlal Nehru*. What does Acharya say? Will he come forward and say that Pandit Nehru—the so-called Light of Asia, his own leader—also belongs to the ranks of the ignorant? Or will he himself cut apart the web of falsehood that he has spread?

Look further at the ways Acharya contradicts himself!

“Rama and Ravana are characters given entirely by Valmiki! Was there some other story or history about Ravana? No. Do we find any Purana, song, history, or stone inscription written before Valmiki in which matters were written differently? No;”

That is Acharya's writing of July 18. Now look closely at what this same Acharya wrote on May 23.

“It appears that even before the sage Valmiki sang the *Ramayana*—that is, from ancient times—the history of Sita and Rama had circulated among the people for many centuries as an oral tale without taking written form. It appears that Bhagavan Valmiki took the Rama story that had already existed in inherited tradition and gave it the form of a book.”

Acharya, who wrote the above on May 23, says in the same *Kalki* on July 18—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 12 / printed 10 -->

“Valmiki's book alone is where the matters concerning the Rama story were obtained; from nowhere else!” he declares with certainty!—Look again at Chakravarthi's contradiction!

“If, before Valmiki, someone had written a Purana, a song, a history, or a stone inscription, and if we were to find matters written differently there, then perhaps one could argue that sage Valmiki, out of partiality towards Rama and the Devas, alters things, covers them up and conceals them, offers explanations and deceives us.

<!-- The Tamil source opens the quotation above but has no visible closing quotation mark before the following paragraph. -->

In the July 18 issue he says that if one takes something from an earlier story, covers it up and tells it in denial, that may be called “deception.” Valmiki was the first to write the story of Rama. Valmiki portrayed Rama as a great hero. Kambar turned that hero into an incarnation. Acharya himself said this in his first article. Will Acharya admit that this is “deception”? Or will he not? Now!—And that is not all. Valmiki is the author of the *Ramayana*. Kambar altered and revised the character Ahalya created by that Valmiki. Will Acharya, in his own language, come forward and declare, “Kambar deceives us”? Acharya himself writes about Ahalya. Look!

## The Lord Who Entered the Hut!

“One day Indra watched for his opportunity and, when the sage was away from the ashram, assumed Gautama's appearance and the garb of a sage and entered the ashram. Thinking of Ahalya's world-famous beauty, he came with the evil desire to possess her. The king of the Devas, who had thus come stealthily, looked at Ahalya Devi and said, as though the sage himself were speaking, ‘Beautiful one, desire has overcome me. In such a state, this is no time to consider whether the moment is proper. Let us unite.’

<!-- Tamil source: scan 13 / printed 11 -->

The sage's wife immediately understood the matter. She knew that this was not her husband, but Indra. Yet she became proud of her beauty because the king of the Devas himself had come seeking her, lost her balance of mind, and consented to the desire of the king of the Devas.

Then she warned him: ‘O king of the Devas, get away quickly. Somehow save yourself from the great danger.’”

What stands above is what Acharya wrote about Ahalya in the July 18 issue.

“This is the Ahalya story in Valmiki's *Ramayana*. Some matters are spoken of and circulated differently in other Puranas and stories. There need be no confusion on that account.”

So Acharya preaches. The very Acharya who said that if the truth in the original story were made different, one could argue that the writer who created that difference intended to deceive us—now also says there need be no confusion on that account. That is the wonder! He creates enough confusion to give one a headache and then advises us that there need be no confusion.

“On the whole, the Devas are good-natured people. Among them too, some will commit evil acts and suffer for them.

The Rakshasas are wicked people who do not fear adharma in the least. Even among the Rakshasa clan, there were a few who were intelligent and good.”

This is how Acharya writes in the July 18 issue! In that very issue he has also drawn the story of Indra, lord of the Devas, and Ahalya, wife of a divine sage, indulging in illicit pleasure. In Valmiki's *Ramayana*, Rama is supposedly the hero—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 14 / printed 12 -->

and Ravana the villain! Rama, the hero, falls at the feet of Ahalya—who had lost her chastity—when she receives release from her curse; Acharya does not fail to write that either. Society refuses even to touch women whose chastity was lost through coercion. Chakravarthi, who lacks the courage to condemn that society, makes Rama himself bow to the Ahalya who surrendered her chastity thinking, “When such a big man himself grabs one's hand and pulls, how can one refuse?” Shame! Shame! The Devas, it seems, are good—only a few are bad. When the conduct of Indra, king of the Devas, is itself like this, need one even ask what his subjects must be like?—Ravana, it seems, is evil—the Devas, it seems, are good—and the tale of those scoundrels does not end with sexual mischief alone! Without even realising it himself, Acharya writes on. Read!

## The Lord Who Stole the Horse!...

“Prince Amsuman was a man of very fine character, endowed with virtue and wisdom. Prince Amsuman guarded the horse intended for Sagara's Ashvamedha sacrifice. But Indra came in the guise of a Rakshasa, took that horse away, and hid it near Kapila, who was Mahavishnu. The sixty thousand sons of Sagara who went in search of the horse suspected that Kapila had stolen it and rushed at him. Kapila reduced all sixty thousand of them to ashes.”

This is the story Acharya gave in the July 4 issue! Because Indra stole the horse from a good man and hid it near Kapila, sixty thousand people were reduced to ashes all at once as though struck by an atom bomb. These Devas, it seems, are good; contradicting himself, contradicting himself, Acharya groans on! Through *Chakravarthi's Son* he fastens a splint onto sagging Aryanism—poor man.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 15 / printed 13 -->

Acharya says that Viswamitra brought Rama and Lakshmana with him precisely to destroy the demons who destroy sacrifices. But in the July 4 issue he speaks a truth. Look—he who wrote in one issue that the Devas are good and that only a few of them may do evil—here, in *Kalki*, he makes all the Devas wicked!

“It is the custom of the Devas to obstruct Ashvamedha sacrifices.”

And in this very state of kicking one way and then the other, Acharya's—yes, Chakravarthi's—cherished son keeps growing. His dream that he can lift up and hold erect the collapsing Aryan Empire will not come true—it certainly will not come true—his own contradictory articles themselves bear witness to that. We shall see which directions the “son” walks in next!
