# Why Did Rama Go to the Forest? A Rishi's Curse? Kaikeyi's Anger?

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's recurring `ஆச்சாரியார்` refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji); the translated body preserves his chosen label as **Achariyar**. The source-specific disparaging expression `மூலி அலங்காரி` is retained as **Mooli-Alangari** because this article does not explain it. The coinage `பிராமணவம்பர்` is rendered **Brahmin Vambar** to preserve Kalaignar's immediate Kambar/Vambar verbal play rather than replacing it with a neutral paraphrase.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 38 / printed 36 -->

Kaikeyi declares firmly: Bharata must rule the country—Rama must go to the forest.

It is in fulfilment of Dasaratha's promise to the King of Kekaya that Kaikeyi hurls such words. By the promise given to Kekaya, it was only just that Bharata should receive the kingdom.

But one may feel like asking: was it not unjust that Rama should have to go to the forest? If one stands on Kaikeyi's side too and argues the case, one can clearly see justice even in her condition. When she hears that Rama had been ready to have Bharata sent away to his grandfather's house and, without anyone knowing, join the conspiracy and have himself crowned, would Kaikeyi's anger not boil over? Kaikeyi was not jealous of Rama from the beginning! She loved him with affection. Achariyar too has written that. Why then did such a Kaikeyi grow angry with Rama and tell him to go to the forest? What mother would not seethe on hearing that Rama too had agreed to deceive her son! We have not dug out these proofs in order to establish that Kaikeyi was a good woman. We point them out only to ask why the character of Kaikeyi, as constructed in the *Ramayana* story, should contradict itself from beginning to end—and why she should be added to the Mooli-Alangari list. What should Kaikeyi have spoken to Rama about?

<!-- Tamil source: scan 39 / printed 37 -->

Has not Achariyar written that Rama was not an incarnation of God—only a great hero? In a *Ramayana* story whose character is such a Rama, how should the character Kaikeyi have spoken to Rama? Did they make her speak that way—Valmiki? Or Kambar? Or this Brahmin Vambar? No!

“Rama! The promise your father gave my father was that my son would receive the crown! He is breaking it now—not merely breaking it; he has cunningly sent my son away to his grandfather's house and is trying to crown you without his knowledge! Is this just? Thinking it just, did you not also agree to this plot? Dasaratha is breaking the oath he gave my father, the King of Kekaya. Therefore, using the two boons Dasaratha gave me, I shall now successfully accomplish my purpose! I shall show that intrigue will not triumph!”

Is this not how Kaikeyi should have spoken to Rama! Is this not how Kaikeyi's character has been set up in the story itself? After mentioning the promise Dasaratha gave Kekaya, after mentioning Bharata's being cunningly sent to his grandfather's house, after mentioning the attempt to perform Rama's coronation without Bharata's knowledge—why, at the end, turn Kaikeyi's conversation alone in a different direction? To show Dasaratha as a good man? Or to say that Kaikeyi was cruel? Or does Achariyar admit that the author of the original story had no skill in constructing a character?

Rama decides to go to the forest. Achariyar pours out the special qualities of this sorrowful scene; in the rush of pouring them out, he also gives a brief account of the greatness of the *Ramayana*:

“Wherever, in whatever place, whoever tells the story of the *Ramayana*, there, with eyes brimming full of tears,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 40 / printed 38 -->

Hanuman will stand with folded hands and listen, tears streaming from his eyes—this is the meaning of what the elders say.”

Achariyar writes so—and, somehow, it is true! Whether Hanuman listens from hiding or not, no one can deny that there are many real Hanumans—many Hanumans who betray their own people to the enemy—people like Achariyar, who stand with tears flowing and hands folded whenever the *Ramayana* story is told!

Father and son thought to seize the kingdom by conspiracy! A woman fought against it. She had agreed to marry the aged old king with the single hope that her son would receive the crown. That woman had been ready to place the very bloom of her youth upon the old man's sacrificial altar. When the very thing for which she accepted that sacrifice of youth failed to happen, what was wrong in her becoming enraged? It was in that anger that she commanded: Ayodhya for Bharata—the forest for Rama. To us this does not look like a scene of sorrow; it looks like a scene of joy in which the schemers were brought down.

Rama, on his way to the forest, takes leave of his mother and of Sita. Lakshmana too sets out with him. Sita too leaves with Rama.

“How can I remain separated from you?” she weeps. Sita had great love for Rama. We cannot simply say that she wept because of that love and move on. Even in Sita's love a blemish can be found. Read it! Achariyar himself has written it—in the 19 September issue!

Lord Rama! To live with you in the forest is happiness to me—that alone is life to me—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 41 / printed 39 -->

Sita says all this—let her say it—but merely thinking of the last thing she says to her husband brings laughter.

“When I was in Mithila, Brahmins and astrologers told my mother about me: ‘She is destined for forest exile.’ Can I undergo forest exile alone? By joining you and undergoing it with you, this is the very time to comfortably get over that event which must somehow come to pass.”

Sita is said to have spoken like this! If a wife says, ‘I shall join you now and experience the destined thing that must somehow happen anyway,’ what else has raised its head in her except self-interest? Achariyar alone must tell us.

Let Sita's self-interest stand on one side. Will Achariyar agree, then, that Kaikeyi was not the cause of Rama's forest exile—that the real cause was the special feature of Sita's horoscope? Achariyar has said that, because of a rishi's curse, Dasaratha suffered separation from his son. Therefore—did Rama go to the forest because of Sita's horoscope? Because of the rishi's curse? Because of Kaikeyi's anger? Or because of the power of fate?

If it was the power of fate, then abusing Kaikeyi is not upright—it is not honourable!

In one place Achariyar says Sita is an incarnation of Lakshmi!

In Ayodhya, where Lakshmi set foot, calamities flourish in abundance.

When a bride enters a house and some suffering follows, people mutter, “So this was the moment Moodevi set foot!”—everyone, including Achariyar!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 42 / printed 40 -->

But in the palace where this Sita set foot, Dasaratha, who had lived for sixty thousand years, comes to die—Rama comes to go to the forest—Ayodhya itself turns into a forest of sorrow. Yet she is supposedly the incarnation of Mahalakshmi! If one says that she too is Moodevi, they come charging at you.

Quite apart from the tales Achariyar spins while fastening a splint onto the old order through *Chakravarthi's Son*—there is his beauty of writing! Ah! Only that Hanuman can shed light on it!

Here is an example. In the 19 September issue of *Kalki* he writes:

“People stood in groups and watched Rama, Sita and Lakshmana walk on their feet.”

What do you think! In those days, apparently, people used to walk on their heads and hands. That poor Kaikeyi must have ordered Rama and Lakshmana to walk on their feet—is that it! They walked on their feet, it seems! On their feet! The genius Achariyar writes like this! And a Hanuman gang waits to read even that with devotion! Aryan guard forces such as *Swadesamitran* lie waiting to take up the brief and sing his praises. Kakapattar will write anything—say anything—this is the genius's literature, ayya, literature!
