# To Rama Who Went in Pursuit of Maricha

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. Article 12's controlling body witness is scans **71–73 / printed pp.69–71**. Scan 83 is a separate back-cover promotional excerpt of part of printed p.70; its visibly different wording and sentence boundaries are preserved independently and are **not** used to rewrite this article body. The source-specific closing label `ஆஷாடபூதிகள்` is retained as **Ashadabhoothis** without an outside identification.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 71 / printed p.69 -->

After sending Lakshmana too to Rama, who had gone in pursuit of Maricha, Sita is alone when Ravana comes there in the guise of a sannyasi. Sita, the incarnation of Lakshmi, believes him to be an ascetic and receives him with hospitality. He reveals his desire. Sita trembles and grows angry. What happens next?—Achariyar writes. Read.

“He cast off his disguise and assumed his true form. Seizing her hair with one hand and taking hold of her with the other, he lifted her up, hurried into the forest, mounted with Sita in his arms the chariot that stood ready, and set off into the sky.”

(30.1.1955 — *Kalki*)

As they went, the eagle Jatayu opposed him and fought, and Ravana's chariot broke apart. Poor thing; if only that Jatayu had not fought, Ravana would at least have carried Sita away in the chariot itself. Because it fought and the chariot broke, the occasion arose for Ravana to clasp Sita against his own body, grip her tightly, carry her forcibly and travel through the sky. A woman of a respectable family—an incarnate divine lady, as Achariyar calls her—is embraced close and carried away by a man. At that moment, to mark the path along which she was being taken, Sita is said to have devised a stratagem. That stratagem—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 72 / printed p.70 -->

how degrading it was, the readers themselves must judge. I give Achariyar's words exactly. Read.

“As they crossed many mountains and rivers, Sita saw someone standing on a mountain along the way. Then she took off her upper cloth, tied her ornaments in it, and threw it down.”

(6.2.1955 — *Kalki*)

She is caught in another man's grip; Achariyar also says that she is desperate to preserve her chastity. In such a condition, would any woman take off her upper garment and throw it down?—did she do so?—if she did, what is that a sign of?—is that the mark of a woman of a respectable family?

Ordinarily, when a man looks at a woman, without even her realising it her hand goes to arrange her upper garment properly. Even when she sees her own husband, when such a sense of modesty rises, women do not fail to adjust their upper garment. But Sita has fallen into the hands of another man and is being carried away. If at such a moment she quite casually takes off her upper garment and throws it down, what are we to think of her? What would Ravana himself have thought?

She asks Lakshmana, “Are you waiting for elder brother to die so that you may have elder brother's wife?” Now, while in another man's embrace, she herself drops her upper garment below. Let the theist worthies consider how far these two acts diminish Sita's stature. Let the mothers who say Sita is the ideal of womanhood examine these two scenes as well.

But Achariyar, instead of letting Sita's mistakes remain with Sita, himself commits a great mistake. Here it is:

All the suffering women in our country are aspects of Sita Devi.

(6.2.1955 — *Kalki*)

<!-- Tamil source: scan 73 / printed p.71 -->

That is how he describes them. All suffering women are supposedly aspects of Sita! What a cruel disgrace! What a base interpretation!!

Are all the women weeping in this country women who, while in another man's grip, threw down their upper garments? Are they women who asked their younger brothers-in-law, “Are you plotting that if elder brother is gone, younger brother may have a chance?” All suffering women are supposedly aspects of Sita; supposedly women carried away by others; supposedly aspects of Sita, who became the object of suspicion to her husband Rama—does this fit? O priestly leader! Does your new research fit the womenfolk of this country? Let me try arguing from within your own defence. If all suffering women are aspects of Sita, what of those women who mortgage their youth and beauty to the kings of the night, live a life of prostitution, wander the streets as volcanoes of anguish with tears flooding their eyes—are those sisters who slipped and fell also aspects of Sita? Achariyar! Why do you insult Sita without even realising it yourself? Do you write under the compulsion that something or other must be written in *Kalki* to take up their brief? Or do you write with the confidence that people will forgive the contradictions between the beginning and end of your writings as disorders of old age? Pitiful—most pitiful! To write that Sita, wife of Kakuthan, and the woman who stands at the market-street corner, makes eyes and feeds her stomach are of the same aspect—you must have great audacity indeed!

To depict Sita, who has no concern for her upper garment, and the flowering vines born into noble families as of one and the same aspect—you must have immense nerve indeed!

However many contradictions you may pour out in writing—as long as you have the confidence that Ashadabhoothis roam about ready to heap that rubbish onto their heads and dance, would you write only that all the women of this country are incarnations of Sita? You would even write that they are incarnations of Draupadi!
