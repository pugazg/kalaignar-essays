# The King Who Ruled the Land Died with No One to Tend Him

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. The source form `சயந்தன்` is preserved as **Sayantan** rather than silently normalised to another epic-name form. The source-specific body-part word `பாக்கை` is retained as **paakkai** because this article does not gloss it.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 50 / printed 48 -->

Rama, Lakshmana and Sita—the three of them—have begun their life in the forest. Here in Ayodhya, Dasaratha writhes like a worm in grief for his son. Achariyar notes that Kausalya is beside him at that moment.

“When the king, not knowing what to do, was floundering and lay half alive, Kausalya spoke out all the justice as it appeared to her and, in a very severe manner, kept stabbing at Dasaratha's guilt again and again.”

Look at the purity of Kausalya's devotion to her husband. The king, it seems, lay half alive, pleading for his very life. And Kausalya Devi—Rama's mother—spoke to him with stabbing severity! Is that all? The queen of chastity(?) who has seen her son sent to the forest and her husband on his deathbed—what does she lament to that husband? Achariyar himself has written it. Read!

“Who is my refuge? For a woman, her husband alone is her refuge. The husband I got has joined another wife and abandoned me. My son has gone to the forest. My relatives from my natal home are in a distant land. While my husband is alive, how can I go there and live depending on my relatives? I stand without refuge. What concern have you for any of this?”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 51 / printed 49 -->

Taking the Ramayana story itself as evidence, I ask: in what way is this devoted wife Kausalya Devi, who worries only about herself, superior to Kaikeyi? If she could go to her relatives' home and live under their protection, but a royal wife laments that even for that her husband is still alive—need one even describe the measure of her character?

Dasaratha, for his part, firmly believes that the reason he suffers this grief for his son is a curse he received in his youth.

He was supposedly an expert at shooting an arrow by sound. One day in his youth, in a wilderness, he mistook the sound of a man drawing water into a pot for the sound of an elephant drinking water and loosed an arrow. When that arrow killed the man, the man's parents, it seems, cursed the Dasaratha who had shot him: may he too suffer grief for a son—and then they died. Thus it becomes clear that the cause of Dasaratha's torment was not Kaikeyi; it was the curse he received in his youth.

For Dasaratha, a warrior so accomplished that he could hear a sound and shoot an arrow to victory, Kaikeyi's help nevertheless became indispensable on a battlefield! Poor man; perhaps he could hear the sound, but could not discover whether it was the sound of an elephant—or the sound of a pot drawing water! While he is telling Kausalya this story—that this distress is the shadow of the curse he received in his youth—

“My chest is closing up! Nothing is left now, Kausalya! I cannot see you with my eyes. Death is drawing near—come close! The oil in the lamp has run dry—Kausalya! Sumitra!” he cried. His speech grew quieter and quieter, and that very night the king gave up his life.

Achariyar has written this too in detail. And he has written another painful absurdity as well. Look.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 52 / printed 50 -->

Even Kausalya and Sumitra, who were nearby, did not know that he had died.

What was the reason? He tells us. Read.

“From sorrow and the exhaustion of keeping awake, they lay worn out in a corner of the inner apartments.

The husband had cried, “Death has come near—everyone come close—Kausalya! Sumitra! The oil in the lamp has run out!” His speech too is described as gradually dying away—the final stage of his deathbed. During that scene of anguish, Kausalya Devi and Sumitra Devi, it seems, became exhausted from keeping awake and fell asleep! They did not know that Dasaratha had died until daybreak! And even after dawn, how do they come to know? Achariyar himself has written that too. Look!

“At daybreak, the Suta-Magadhas gathered as usual at the entrance to the inner apartments and, each according to his order, sang Suprabhatam and played instruments to awaken the king. There was no sign of the king rising. The attendants waited a long time and then, wondering, ‘Why has the king still not risen today?’ entered inside. They saw the king lying lifeless. Then the matter became known throughout the palace, and the whole palace became one sea of grief.”

It is indeed a pitiable thing that the lord of Ayodhya—Dasaratha Maharaja, with three crowned queens and sixty thousand beloved consorts—lay as a corpse until late in the night without anyone noticing. This is precisely the danger in marrying so many wives!

The younger one slept thinking the elder would attend to him—the next one slept thinking the younger would attend to him—and so all the loving wives

<!-- Tamil source: scan 53 / printed 51 -->

competed with one another in sleeping beside the king's deathbed; was that not why no one noticed the long sleep he had begun!

As soon as life left him—there was no one beside that ruler even to close his open eyes, poor man! Before the last breath left him, even to perform the rite of pouring milk, the monarch who had ruled the world was left with no one to tend him! Wives in their thousands—yet as he breathed his last, the king could not find room even in the lap of one among them—they had all fallen so deeply asleep. Perhaps that was the Aryan culture of those days! Perhaps the women of Ayodhya were extraordinarily accomplished at sleeping! Why only Ayodhya? Even Sita Devi, the lightning-creeper of Mithila, was so accomplished that when Sayantan, son of Indra, in the form of a crow, pecked at her *paakkai* and blood flowed, she went on sleeping without waking! The very embodiment of chastity, indeed! Somehow or other, Dasaratha Maharaja died. Valmiki is said to have praised Dasaratha as one who possessed the ability to master his senses. Right at the beginning of the Ramayana, the sage is said to have written such a description of Dasaratha. Achariyar says so. It was precisely the man who had mastered his senses who needed “sixty thousand”!

The younger wife Kaikeyi existed only for sensual amusement. Achariyar himself has written that on the night before the day fixed for Rama's coronation, Dasaratha babbled to Kaikeyi in an intoxication of lust; perhaps even that intoxication of lust came from his mastery of the senses! He died unable to bear grief for his son; perhaps Achariyar will say that even that event arose from his mastery of the senses! “Having lost his heart to the beautiful young Kaikeyi, the woman who caught his eye, unable even to keep his tail under control as he spoke, he said that he would give the kingdom to the son born to her,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 54 / printed 52 -->

and enjoyed bodily pleasure with her day after day; the moment the words ‘Rama has gone to the forest’ entered his ears, his mind broke and he fell dead—can it be fitting to call that Dasaratha a man who mastered his senses?” I ask the sacred-thread king Achariyar.
