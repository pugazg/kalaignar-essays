# Is Everything That Happens Narayana's Doing?

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. The source-specific name **Kaga Pattar** in the closing passage is retained without outside identification. The verified source also contains an outer unmatched quotation opening in the scan-69 Janaki passage; English documents that anomaly rather than silently treating the Tamil punctuation as regular.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 65 / printed 63 -->

Seeing Surpanakha dishonoured, Khara and the others try to take revenge on Rama and in the end meet their deaths—Rama stands alone and kills the army of demons and its leaders in heaps.

Look at what Achariyar writes about Rama's power:

“If God himself came in human form, gave refuge to the helpless and set out to keep that pledge, one can see through the power of thought how much that man could accomplish.”

### He does not stop with this—read further:

“Kambar and Valmiki both take Rama as an incarnation-man and portray the events to us in different ways—in some places as a hero who has received divine grace, and in some places as God himself.”

What did this same Achariyar write in the earlier articles? Read that too:

“In the epic of Rishi Valmiki, Rama is a great man—he was not written as an incarnation of Ishvara.”

It is one and the same serial article—appearing in one and the same magazine. In May 1954 he says Valmiki did not create Rama as an incarnation;

<!-- Tamil source: scan 66 / printed 64 -->

in May 1955 he says Kambar and Valmiki both wrote Rama as an incarnation-man. What answer will Achariyar's backing orchestra give for this contradiction? A disorder of the brain?—confusion?—would not even a man capable of a little thought be compelled to ask this question? Poor Achariyar; I do not know why he unnecessarily puts his foot into this mire and lands himself in trouble—why say I do not know? For the permanent welfare of his community, that Chanakya is ready to embrace thousands upon thousands of contradictions. But one thing—I remind that pitiable Brahmin leader that this is no longer the old age that raised no question mark of “Why? How?”

### Look at another piece of filtered, dry confusion:

“Narayana knows that he himself is Rama. But Rama does not think that he himself is Narayana.”

He himself wrote this too in the issue of 9.1.1955. There is no need to explain it or debate it. Try and discover whether there is any clear meaning at all in these lines written by Achariyar—those who find one and tell us—or those who explain that what he wrote is indeed correct—will receive a proper prize, and I am prepared publicly to admit my defeat as well.

Leave that. Ravana, hearing of the dishonouring of Surpanakha and the destruction of Khara and the others, plans to abduct Sita and seeks the help of Maricha, a Rakshasa ascetic living the life of an ashram. Maricha advises Ravana and sends him back to Lanka. Ravana approaches Maricha again and asks for help, asking him to take the form of a deer and beguile Sita. Maricha refuses and gives him a long sermon. Maricha says that Rama will destroy both himself and Ravana,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 67 / printed 65 -->

and that he will not consent to commit a sinful act. Achariyar himself has written away two or three pages of Maricha's objections mixed with “dharma and justice.” Seeing Maricha refuse, Ravana says, “If you do not do as I wish, I shall kill you right here.”

### Maricha speaks—listen:

“Ravana! I tried to tell you what was good for you—you did not listen. If I do as you say, it is certain that my life will go. Rather than die at your hands, it is better that I give my life to the enemy.” (23.1.1955 — *Kalki*)

Maricha knows that Rama will kill him—he also knows that if he refuses Ravana he will die—death is there either way. In such a situation, what should he have done?

If Ravana killed him, it would all have ended without causing trouble to Sita and Rama. If he prepared to die at Rama's hands, he would have to cause trouble to Sita and her husband and, in addition, pave the way for Ravana to enter upon an evil path. Therefore Maricha, the ashram-dweller, could have died at Ravana's own hands—he need not have become the target of the cruel words of Rama, Sita and the others—and, by his own reasoning, he could have been freed from the “sinful act” as well. For some reason he did not do that. Only Achariyar can shed light on it!

Maricha went in the form of a deer. Opening her honeyed lips, Devi Sita asked Rama, “Catch that deer and bring it to me.” Leaving Lakshmana to guard Sita, Rama chases the deer a very long way. When the deer keeps running without being caught, he shoots an arrow at it. Maricha in deer form changes his voice to sound like Rama's and cries out loudly, “Ayyo, Lakshmana!” Seeing this, Rama says,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 68 / printed 66 -->

“Oh-ho! This is a great deception; if Lakshmana were deceived by this trick, left Sita alone in the forest and came looking for me, it would be a great danger! It seems the demons have contrived this deception in order to carry Sita away—or to kill and eat her. Thinking she heard my voice crying ‘Ha, Sita,’ Sita will at once be frightened and send Lakshmana to me. The jackals. They howl ominously. Birds and animals show signs of misfortune. In my heart, instead of courage, there is trembling. Everything I see is an ill omen. I think I am going to meet with great danger.” (13.2.1955 — *Kalki*)

When I see Rama grieving, I can only laugh—do not abuse me, Achariyar, saying, “It is his wife he is worried about; of course you will only laugh!” Think a little! Maricha cried “Lakshmana!” in a voice like Rama's and died. Rama fears that on hearing this Sita will be frightened, Lakshmana will panic, and he may leave her alone and come to him. At the very moment Maricha shouted, why could Rama not have shouted too?

“Younger brother, Lakshmana! This is no deer; Maricha, that great sinner, is dead! It was not I who cried ‘Ayyo, Lakshmana’—it was he! Do not leave Sita and come!” Rama could have shouted this, could he not? Will you say that if he shouted they would not hear it? When Maricha's shout could reach their ears and cause such alarm, would the shout of Rama, who had the power to destroy thousands upon thousands of demons like Maricha, alone fail to reach them? The immensely powerful Rama could even have prevented Sita or Lakshmana from hearing Maricha's voice—why did he not do it? Why did he not shout? O Chakravarthi who writes *Chakravarthi's Son*, give us a proper answer and let us see.

Hearing the cry “Ayyo, Lakshmana!”, Sita trembles, thinking danger has come to Rama. She tells Lakshmana to run at once.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 69 / printed 67 -->

He gives many explanations, saying, “This is a demon's magic—therefore there is no need for me to go.” Achariyar himself describes this scene. Read.

“Janaki's anger—what anger it was! Beating her belly with both hands, “Son of Sumitra! Have you become an enemy? Have you been putting on an act all these days? Have you been waiting all this time for Rama's death? Have you deceived me all these days thinking that once he dies you can have me? Wicked man, you hear Rama crying out in anguish and still stand here without going? Was all the affection you showed elder brother one great plot? Man of evil ways!” she said.

“Giving the excuse of elder brother's command, even when elder brother cries out in anguish will you refuse to go and let him die? Ah, what a deception Rama and I have suffered by trusting you! Vile man! Wicked man! Deceiver who came as Rama's enemy! Are you happy that danger has come to Rama? Are you trying to deceive me? Now I understand the purpose for which you came to the forest with Rama. You sinner! Is this at Bharata's instigation? Have all of you become enemies of my lord? What are all of you together planning to do? Having lived with Rama, would I even cast my eyes on any of you? After Rama dies I will not remain alive for even a minute—know that!” she said.

(30.1.1955 — *Kalki*)

Is this the speech of a woman of a respectable family to her younger brother-in-law? And is she an ordinary woman? An incarnation of Mahalakshmi. Wife of the great man Rama—that is who is speaking. In anger: “Do you think that if elder brother is gone, you can have elder brother's wife?”

Even a fourth-rate woman whose trade is quarrelling at street corners would shrink from speaking such words to her younger brother-in-law; if the wife of the bearer of the conch and discus speaks them,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 70 / printed 68 -->

in what list are we to place them?—Achariyar, who sings the Mother's grace, must answer. He will say fate made them speak that way. If fate is to be given as the reason, then Ravana too must become blameless! Achariyar and those like him may simply say that Ravana's taking Sita captive was also the play of fate and refrain from reviling him.

The man who lamented “Fate, oh fate!” in Chapter 49—the man who cried in that same chapter, “God has arranged disaster in every way”—in Chapter 53 says:

“Through desire and delusion, by failing to do at the proper time what they ought to do—and by doing what they ought not to do—they lost Sita.” (20.2.1955 — *Kalki*)

Did you see how disgracefully Achariyar speaks of Rama and Lakshmana without even realising it himself?—even if Rama did not know that he was the incarnation of Narayana, did Achariyar too fail to know it? Why does he abuse that Narayana incarnation with a tongue that seems to have no nerve in it?

Desire—

Delusion—

Doing what ought not to be done—

With such pungent words, Achariyar “worships” the incarnation of God. If we say the very same thing, bans—and forces—come down on us. At a time when we console ourselves, “It is time to close shop—time for Kaga Pattar to settle the account—we are bound—let us endure,” whatever falls into the hands of the Achariyars in the time that remains is profit, is it not!

Let the livelihood go on—we shall see for how long;
