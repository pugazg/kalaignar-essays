# Bharadvaja's Ashram—or a Paris 'Bar'?

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's recurring `ஆச்சாரியார்` refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji); the translated body preserves his chosen label as **Achariyar**. The article-heading witness reads `பரத்துவாஜா ஆஸ்ரமமா - பாரிஸ் நகரத்து ‘பாரா’?`, while the contents-page witness reads `பரத்துவாஜர் ஆஸ்ரமமா - பாரீஸ் நகரத்து ‘பாரா’?`. The English title follows the article heading's sense without altering either Tamil witness. `‘பாரா’?` plays on the English loanword **bar** with the Tamil interrogative ending; the English title therefore preserves **'Bar'?**. The two Kambar verses below are translated closely from the Tamil witness together with Kalaignar's own accompanying explanation, not substituted from an external English version.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 30 / printed 28 -->

An ashram—and Apsaras there?

A sage's camp—and amorous revelry there?—

A camp seeking the path to liberation—and there,

faces swollen from kisses?—

A house of sorrow—and there an unbroken

journey through the world of pleasure?

A task filled with trouble—and then,

pleasure beyond all bounds?

Bharata returned from his grandfather's house. He heard of his father's death and his elder brother's forest exile. Refusing to accept the crown, he wept. Separation from his brother and the happenings in the palace turned him into a worm in fire. With his retinue he sets out to bring Rama back. On the way he meets Guha. Seeing Bharata's brotherly affection while he is sunk in immense grief, Guha marvels.

**Look at how Achariyar describes Bharata's condition.**

“After meeting Guha, Bharata's grief increased beyond what it had been before. Bharata, who had received purity of nature as an inborn possession, had come to such a state; thinking of Rama, he

<!-- Tamil source: scan 31 / printed 29 -->

sighed deeply, sweated all over his body, and tossed about without sleep.”

Bharata, thus become the very summit of anguish, looked at Guha and, it seems, cried while asking: What did Rama eat? Where did he sit? He even asked where Lakshmana slept.

“Seeing Rama and Janaka's daughter lying there on the ground, could sleep come to me? Shedding tears and weeping, Lakshmana stood guard the whole night, bow in hand, without lying down—just as I did.”

Even when Guha said this, Bharata, unable to bear his grief, was convulsed with sorrow, it seems!

“When they rose in the morning, they twisted their hair into matted locks and went on foot.”

Guha tells Bharata all these sorrowful tidings. Bharata, who had already rolled about weeping in the palace, comes on, it seems, struck and exhausted by painful news even in the forest.

“The scene in which Bharata and Guha meet on the bank of the Ganga and share their grief is one deeply relished by the Alvars and devotees.”

Achariyar himself has written that. Such a vast flood of sorrow it was! Achariyar, who sees the beauties of the *Ramayana* through Valmiki and through Kambar, for some reason does not explain how far Kambar's poetic force rises to its peak in this scene of sorrow.

In the *Chakravarthi's Son* articles, verses from the Kamba Ramayanam are quoted in many places. In the same way, why should Achariyar hesitate to tell the people of the scene Kambar gives when Bharata goes in search of Rama? With tears filling his eyes, Bharata travels with his retinue,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 32 / printed 30 -->

and through Guha's efforts a great line of boats is arranged to cross the Ganga. Bharata, Vasishta, and the men and women all board them and go on, longing to see Rama.

Look at how beautifully Kambar, so dear to Achariyar, describes this sorrowful scene.

As the oars move, spray from the Ganga rises and wets the garments of the women in the boats; through the wet portions, what they had kept concealed becomes visible outside—and the men in the boats, it seems, delight in the sight.

Here is the verse—

*Ayodhya Kandam, Guha Padalam, verse 56:*

> “As the oars swept and the boats moved,
> the spray rose and fell upon the women;
> through the wetted cloth
> their hidden parts showed outside;
> and to the strong men whose hearts had grown faint,
> that sight—ah!—gave life again.”

Palace women and queens are travelling in the boat! The whole scene is a scene of grief! But Kambar's eyes—what scene do they choose to photograph!

Women who do not think to cover the parts made visible by the water that splashed and soaked their clothes!

Retainers rejoicing at what remained uncovered!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 33 / printed 31 -->

What civilization Ayodhya had—civilization indeed! Where in this world is there an equal to the display of genius of Achariyar, who has said that Kambar wrote the *Ramayana* with devotion as his chief concern?

Very well. The entire army has crossed the river. Bharata goes to Bharadvaja's ashram on the other bank. Bharadvaja the sage, it seems, suspected Bharata just as Guha had suspected him: “Perhaps Bharata has come leading an army against Rama.” Guha was an ordinary man; so he suspected Bharata. But Bharadvaja was a sage—may a sage entertain such suspicion? Fearing that someone might ask this, Achariyar has written an explanation for that too. Here it is!

“I am writing *Chakravarthi's Son* according to Valmiki's *Ramayana*! Just as Guha suspected Bharata on seeing his retinue, Valmiki has sung that the sage Bharadvaja too suspected him. It is not like this in the Tulsi Ramayana. The Tulsi Ramayana is entirely steeped in devotion. There is nothing the rishis do not know. Therefore, in the Tulsi Ramayana, how could Bharadvaja have any doubt about Bharata? But what Kambar has sung follows Valmiki.

<!-- The Tamil source opens the quotation above but has no visible closing quotation mark before Kalaignar resumes his own prose. -->

Look at how great a confusion Achariyar himself has fallen into over Valmiki—and how he confuses the people too.

Achariyar says that Valmiki portrays Rama as a heroic man, Tulsidas sees Rama through the eyes of devotion, and Kambar sees him as God himself. If everyone sees this motley Rama in a different way, why carry him around and conduct religious discourses?

Valmiki says Bharadvaja had no knowledge of Bharata! Tulsidas says he did have knowledge! Achariyar asks, “Can a rishi be without such knowledge?” Yet what he is writing is supposedly Valmiki's *Ramayana*!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 34 / printed 32 -->

So far he has written 36 articles on the *Ramayana*. In all 36 articles, 36 contradictions can be found. In the first issue he wrote that Rama was not an incarnation of God; in the articles that followed he declared Rama an incarnation of Vishnu.

Do you know what he says in this 36th article?

“Though Rama is indeed the sacred incarnation of Bhagavan himself, in Valmiki's *Ramayana* he is portrayed only as a heroic man.”

Look at the contradictions! Look at the evasions!

Whoever tries to hide the truth—however talented they may be—will end up stuck in the mud and floundering just like this! Achariyar is a fine example. Then Bharadvaja held a feast in the ashram for Bharata and his retinue.

What sort of feast do you imagine would take place in an ashram? You would think: varieties of fruit, vegetables, cow's milk, things like that. And Bharata's group too is sunk in a melody of grief, with tear-filled eyes. At such a moment the feast ought to be even more ordinary, should it not? Even if the one serving serves, is this not a time when neither the diner's stomach nor his mind would make room for it? But look at the feast Bharadvaja provided.

Bharadvaja summoned the Devas Yama, Varuna, Agni and Kubera and ordered the feast—rather like placing an order for a dinner party at an Arya Bhavan Hotel. They too carried out the order to perfection. Achariyar himself writes about the feast. Read. But while reading, do not forget how grief-stricken Bharata's party is supposed to be.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 35 / printed 33 -->

“Suitable lodgings for everyone, facilities for bathing and the like, sandal paste, flowers, food and drink, varieties of delicacies, the dance of Apsaras, Gandharva music—everything was arranged in an unimaginably divine fashion, and everyone lost themselves in the experience. The soldiers in Bharata's army, intoxicated with joy, thought, ‘Now we need not go to Dandakaranya; nor shall we return to Ayodhya. Let us stay right here.’”

“Like a great feast held in his capital by a Maharaja of boundless wealth for another Maharaja and his retinue, in the ashram-grove where Bharadvaja lived, lodgings, vehicles, attendants and everything else sprang into existence in an instant; everyone indulged beyond all bounds, lost themselves, and fell asleep.”

What an ashram feast! And a feast held at a time of grief!

I have shown exactly what Achariyar wrote—did you see how delicious the feast is? And that too in the ashram of sages who had renounced everything! A feast so vast that even the American government could not conduct it! A dance so marvellous that it could not be staged even in the Kothavalai Kozhumandapam! Ah! Ah! Ah! Read again what Achariyar has written.

(1) Apsaras—(divine women such as Rambha, Urvashi and others)

(2) food and drink—

(3) indulged beyond all bounds—

(4) lost themselves—

Put all these words together when you think of the feast conducted by the sage! Will the thought, “Alas! Why are we not Bharata too!” arise in some young men—or will it not?

<!-- Tamil source: scan 36 / printed 34 -->

—Achariyar.

At this point Achariyar has not written out Valmiki's and Kambar's descriptions in full. If he had written those too, every letter would be intoxicating. The feast is over.

Look at how Kambar explains the way the Ayodhya warriors, whose stomachs had received food, and dear younger brother Bharata, who had come in search of his elder brother, received food for the senses as well.

> “On a bed layered with fivefold softness,
> beside tender, ankleted feet stained red,
> beside maidens with poison-like eyes
> lying close like does,
> all those young men too fell asleep.”

That is: on cotton beds made of five kinds of soft materials, divine maidens with tender, ankleted feet stained with red dye and eyes like poison lay close beside them, like does; and all those young men too fell asleep.

The place is a forest; the hour is midnight; the one describing scenes of lust is Kambar—even Achariyar might have lost his composure.

Perhaps it was the wicked thought that *Kalki* readers should not receive the pleasure he himself received—who knows?—that made that grand old Vedic man pass over Kambar's curious descriptions without explanation.

Achariyar writes of the splendour of such a feast—and devotees find it sweet! We gave this very explanation of the feast and said, “Look at the indulgence in the ashram”—and then the devotees sprang at us in fury!

Why does what tastes sweet now appear bitter then? Will the devotees answer? Or will Achariyar himself, lord of the Brahmin clan, who comments upon Bharadvaja's feast, answer?
