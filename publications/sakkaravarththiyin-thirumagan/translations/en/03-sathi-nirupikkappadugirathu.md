# The Conspiracy Is Proven

> **Translator identification note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's recurring `ஆச்சாரியார்` refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji). The translated body preserves Kalaignar's chosen label as **Achariyar**.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 22 / printed 20 -->

In the previous issue I had concluded by saying that Achariyar had revealed the truth that all three—Dasaratha, Vasishta and Rama—joined in a conspiracy and decided to tie onto Rama's head the burden of the kingdom that, by the agreed condition, rightfully belonged to Bharata. I had also pointed out that Dasaratha was breaking the promise he had given King Kekaya that Bharata would be crowned. Here, in this issue, Achariyar writes along the very same line of thought. Look.

“When Dasaratha had not yet been blessed with children, he married Kaikeyi in accordance with royal-family custom. At that time Kaikeyi's father, King Kekaya, obtained a promise from Dasaratha: the crown would go to the son born of her womb. There could have been nothing surprising or unusual in saying so then. At that time none of them had children. The other wives had not borne children. It was precisely out of the desire for offspring that he took a third wife. Even that desire was not fulfilled. Much later, after performing a sacrifice, all three wives had children. Rama, the son of the chief queen, was the eldest among the four sons, possessed every noble quality, had the fitness to bear the burden of rule, and had earned the affection of ministers, townspeople, villagers and chieftains alike as one fit for the office of Crown Prince—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 23 / printed 21 -->

with all this being so, how could it be just to disregard his right and royal-family custom and crown Bharata?”

Achariyar admits that Dasaratha had indeed given that promise, yet argues that breaking the promise and setting Bharata aside was not wrong. What reasons does he give? Rama, it seems, was the eldest of the four. All three wives ate the divine payasam obtained from the sacrifice at the same time—yet Achariyar says Rama was the eldest. Perhaps he calls him the eldest because he was the son of the senior wife. If so, would not the senior wife's last-born son also be the eldest? He also says Rama possessed every noble quality. What does that mean? If the elder brother is intelligent, does it not mean the younger brother is a little foolish? Achariyar, who wrote in his earlier articles that all four children were said to contain a share of Vishnu, now says that among the four Rama possessed every noble quality. Does that not mean he is finding fault with the other three, who were also divisions of Vishnu's share! Perhaps, as he wrote in the earlier article, Rama was one-half of Vishnu's share, Lakshmana one-quarter, and Bharata and Shatrughna one-eighth each—and so he says Rama possessed all noble qualities. But that will not fit either! For he himself, further down in that very article, wrote the view that there is no need to calculate shares in a divine portion; every share is complete in itself. Therefore all four princes are complete indeed. Think: is Achariyar's argument defeated by Achariyar himself or not?

Rama alone, it seems, was fit to bear the burden of rule—that is what he says! Was Bharata unfit? After Rama went to the forest, Bharata, with Lakshmana gone as Rama's companion and his father no longer there to console him, governed the kingdom with Shatrughna for fourteen years. What happened then—was Ayodhya thrown into confusion?

<!-- Tamil source: scan 24 / printed 22 -->

Did enemies invade? Did anarchy break out? Was repression needed? Bharata ruled peacefully, did he not? If one wants turmoil, it came fourteen years later, after Rama took the throne—and that too was a civil war: a battle arising from the Ashvamedha sacrifice between the father and those said to be his sons, Lava and Kusha. Bharata, who ruled peacefully for fourteen years, is apparently unfit to bear the burden of rule—but Rama, who waged battles even during his forest years, is the fit one. Achariyar argues so.

Rama, it seems, possessed every noble quality; therefore, it seems, he could be crowned. Was the Rama who desired to be crowned when his dear brother Bharata was absent the man of noble qualities? I do not know in what way Rama was of nobler character than Bharata, who withered in grief the moment he heard that his elder brother had gone to the forest.

Dasaratha first broke the promise he had given and, without Bharata's knowledge, tried secretly to crown Rama. That was right. Entirely just—that is what Achariyar argues.

Was not Achariyar himself one who came into the Council of Ministers through the back door! There is therefore nothing surprising in his pleading Dasaratha's case.

Look further—the stage has arrived where Achariyar contradicts himself.

“Besides, Bharata and Kaikeyi had no desire or even thought whatsoever concerning the crown. There was not even any talk of it. Therefore the king thought there would be no difficulty in installing Rama, according to custom, in the office of Crown Prince. Likewise, there was not the slightest guile in Kaikeyi's heart. This is plainly evident from Kaikeyi's actions. As for Bharata's heart, there is no need even to say anything.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 25 / printed 23 -->

This is what he has written in this issue. Do you know how he ended the previous issue? In this week's *Kalki*, King Dasaratha thinks there will be no difficulty at all in installing Rama, according to custom, in the office of Crown Prince. But the previous week, afraid that Bharata might return, he hurriedly summoned Rama a second time, spoke to him, explained the situation to him, and began attending to the arrangements for the Crown Prince's coronation the very next day.

“The streets had been sprinkled with water and decorated. People had flown many kinds of beautiful flags over their houses. Dressed in fine clothes and ornaments, smeared with sandal paste and adorned with flowers, people stood everywhere in groups, talking about something. In the temples there was the resounding music of instruments.”

Ayodhya is thus full of joy—but Manthara, who was Kaikeyi's kinswoman, companion and confidential servant, knew nothing whatever about any of these happenings. She asks another woman nearby and learns that all this celebration is for Rama's coronation. Not Manthara alone—even Kaikeyi, one of the queens, learns the news only through Manthara. Achariyar has written all these details in the 15 August 1954 issue of *Kalki*.

Ordinary people, it seems, heard the news of Rama's coronation, put up flags and celebrated in crowds upon crowds. But the “guileless” Kaikeyi did not know the news, it seems—and neither did her confidential servant Manthara!

Then what does that mean? Are we not compelled to conclude that a “conspiracy” was carried out to deceive Bharata and Kaikeyi? Not merely we—everyone with a brain will come to exactly that conclusion.
