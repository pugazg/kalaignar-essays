# Traitors Meet!

> **Translator identification/source note — not part of Kalaignar's text:** As established for this publication, Kalaignar's `ஆச்சாரியார்` is rendered **Achariyar** and refers to C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji) only in translator/editorial identification. The source quotation punctuation across scans 75–76 is irregular: successive paragraphs begin with fresh quotation marks without corresponding visible closures, and scan 78 prints the final citation as `(6.3.1955 - ‘கல்கி)` with no source-visible closing single quotation before the parenthesis. The English keeps the quoted speech readable while recording those source irregularities here rather than inventing missing Tamil punctuation. The strict-reviewed scan-76 form `துராசைப் பட்டுக்` is also left untouched in the Tamil layer and translated contextually as being driven by greed.

When Sita went missing, Rama lamented. Looking at trees, plants and creepers and asking, “Have you seen my wife?”, he beat his chest and wailed an oppari.

“If Sita, out of ignorance, spoke improper words to you, could you not have borne even that and stayed there on guard?” he scolded his younger brother Lakshmana. Thus he cried and lamented, and at last, through Jatayu, they learned that Sita was in Ravana's possession.

As they wandered through the forest thinking about how to rescue her, a demon named Kabandha caught them. Kabandha had no head—his mouth and eyes were in his belly itself. He had no legs either; his habit was to reach out, drag in the animals and human beings who passed through the forest, and put them into his stomach. Rama and Lakshmana killed him. The moment he was killed, Kabandha's demonic form changed and he became, in a divine form, a dweller of heaven.

After telling his former story—that he had become like this because of Indra's curse—he told Rama and Lakshmana that if they wanted to recover Sita, they must obtain the help of Sugriva, who had been driven out by Vali and was hiding on Rishyamukha mountain; then he set off for heaven in a vimana.

From this story of Kabandha, it becomes clear that the Devas have incarnated not only as Rama and Lakshmana; they have also incarnated as terrifying demons like Kabandha!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 75 / printed 73 -->

Then Rama and Lakshmana set out in search of Sugriva. Do you know who Sugriva is? Do you know what his condition is? Like Rama, he too had lost his kingdom and his wife and was wandering along the banks of the Pampa river. Rama, who conspired to deprive Bharata of his right to the kingdom—and Sugriva, who had been driven out after plotting to deceive Vali and seize the kingdom—became allies. “You recover my wife from Vali! I will recover your wife from Ravana,” Sugriva assured Rama. Why was Sugriva driven out? Why did enmity grow between Vali and him? Achariyar himself writes that story in the 6.3.1955 issue of *Kalki*. Read—the passage is as though Sugriva himself tells the story—

“My elder brother Vali was crowned king of the Vanara kingdom and was ruling at Kishkindha, a warrior of immense prowess. I remained Crown Prince, giving him love and devotion without stint. Vali and an asura named Mayavi had an old enmity. One night that asura came to Kishkindha and challenged Vali to battle.

“Heard the roar he let out in the night, Vali rose from his sleep in anger and went out at once to confront him. I too went along to help him. Seeing the two of us coming in the moonlight, Mayavi ran and entered a cave. Vali pursued him into the cave. I too was about to enter. Vali stopped me, saying no. He said he alone must kill that wicked one and told me to stand outside at the mouth of the cave. Saying so, he went inside.

“The one who went inside did not return even after many days. While I was worrying about what might have happened to my elder brother, a great clamour of the asuras was heard inside the cave. Along with it, from the cave, ‘a flood of blood

came surging out.’ Seeing this, I concluded that Mayavi and many asuras had surrounded Vali and killed him. Thinking that the asuras would come and attack me too, I placed a great boulder at the mouth of the cave and sealed it.

“I returned to Kishkindha in grief. Without telling anyone that Vali had died, I went on attending to the affairs of state. Then the Vanara subjects pressed me. ‘If there is no king, the kingdom will fall to ruin; Vali has not returned even after so long; have yourself crowned and rule,’ they insisted. I too agreed.

“After some time, having killed and destroyed Mayavi and the asuras who were his accomplices, Vali returned. Seeing the cave blocked, he shouted from inside, ‘Sugriva! Sugriva!’ I was not there, so I knew nothing of this. He became greatly enraged with me, kicked the rock aside and entered Kishkindha.

“When he saw me ruling after having myself crowned king, uncontrollable rage rose in him and he abused me in many ways. I told him what had happened exactly as it had happened. ‘I did this thinking the demon had killed you. I accepted the coronation because the subjects pressed me. This kingdom is yours; take it back. I shall be your servant as before,’ I said, and fell at his feet. But he paid no heed at all to what I said. He thought that, driven by greed, I had sealed the mouth of the cave and unlawfully had myself crowned, and he drove me out of the city. ‘If you come back this way, I will kill you,’ he said, and drove me away. I ran out in the garment I was wearing. From then on I have been wandering in hiding through forests and mountains,

keeping myself alive. I have lost everything and am in this state. These four Vanaras alone have remained my companions. Unable, because of anger, to know the truth, my elder brother is committing such injustice. Slay that Vali, that warrior of immense prowess, and save me,” said Sugriva.

## This Is the Story Achariyar Has Drawn Up!

Look closely: is there even a drop of truth in Sugriva's words here? Vali goes alone to fight the asura Mayavi. A flood of blood has come surging out of the cave; Sugriva assumes that it is Vali's blood and concludes that he is dead. Both sides are fighting inside the cave. And Sugriva himself knows that Vali is a warrior whom no one can defeat. Achariyar has noted in the 13.3.1955 issue that Sugriva himself tells Rama that Vali was the warrior who, with one blow, felled Dundubhi—the buffalo-shaped asura with the strength of a thousand elephants, whom neither Himavan nor Samudra Raja could defeat—and hurled him beyond a distance of one yojana. Could Sugriva have thought that the blood flowing out belonged to a Vali of such strength?—Very well! Even if he wrongly thought that his elder brother had died, what should the younger brother have done? Should he not have said, ‘What life is there for me after my elder brother is gone?’, gathered his forces, entered the cave with minister Hanuman and confronted Mayavi?—Leaving that aside, what justice was there in placing a huge rock at the mouth of the cave, sealing it and coming away?—Is it not the truth that Sugriva had been waiting for the day Vali would be gone so that he could usurp the kingdom!

If Rama takes as his friend a Sugriva of such deceitful character and with such a heart devoid of affection, what are we to think of Rama's heart? Rama is praised as an elder brother blessed with Lakshmana, a younger brother of undying affection—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 78 / printed 76 -->

then how is it fitting that Rama should seek the aid of the traitor Sugriva, who betrayed his elder brother?—Achariyar alone must explain that. The elder brother and the demon fought, they say. A flood of blood rolled through the cave, they say. Instead of thinking the demon had died, he thought his elder brother had died, they say; to save his own life he sealed the cave, they say. The subjects wanted him to wear the crown, they say! And he wore it, they say! When his elder brother returned, he told him the truth, they say; he would not listen, they say! After he was caught—after the conspiracy came to light—the traitor staged a sham drama to escape! Rama becomes his ally! Here Achariyar dodges.

“There is no great fault in Vali's conduct! Nor is there any great fault to be found in Sugriva's actions.” (6.3.1955 — *Kalki*)

This is Achariyar's verdict!—If so, should not Rama's course of action have been to make peace between the two brothers and obtain the help of both! But that was not Rama's action! Why? Why, Achariyar; why?
