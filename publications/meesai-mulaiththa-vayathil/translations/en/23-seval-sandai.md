# Cockfight

<!-- Tamil source: scan 115 / printed 114 -->

When Iniyan returned home after going to Iyalur, Enthizhai flew toward him asking, “What have you bought and brought, Aththaan?” Iniyan and Enthizhai—young treasure-chests of love still savouring without weariness the new flavour of household life—were both of Tamil stock, both figures of beauty!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 116 / printed 115 -->

“I bought and brought a rooster with a tail that curves in rainbow beauty,” he said. “Look at this rooster perched upon my lap, displaying the bearing of another Tamil! Have you seen its comb like a bitter-gourd fruit, woman? Look, my dear, at the beauty of the wattle hanging like a tongue struck loose! It shows the gait of the Chera! It shows the form of a warrior unshaken at the sight of an army!” he praised it.

“I agree, certainly. But if you had bought a hen, it would at least lay eggs... What use is there in keeping this?” At Enthizhai's words he first burst out laughing, then continued: “Great beauty with a mouth brightly red! In raising a cow, a she-goat, a hen, there is only one aim—to gain the use of milk or eggs!... If I put into your ear the purpose for which I bought this rooster, you will approve my act!” Leaning upon Aththaan's shoulder, she laughed: “I understand your heart. To praise a woman people call upon the peacock; from now on, your thought is that the rooster may be shown to praise a man too!”

“You are wrong there as well, my love. A rooster may be compared to a man—no one will object! But one thing: even to praise womankind, it is the male peacock that is needed. Understand that too!” Before anger could settle upon her face, he reddened it with a kiss and finished off her anger!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 117 / printed 116 -->

“Enthizhai! I cannot keep from telling you something that happened this morning! You know my uncle Sengannan? Every day the fat growing upon his body seems to spill through his tongue—I experienced the sight directly. Because fame has gathered around him as a great hero of cockfighting, he twists his sickle-moustache as though he himself had strained his body, fought and won, and walks imagining himself Ravana who once ruled Lanka. Let that pass; I do not care! On my way back carrying this rooster he stopped me and asked, ‘What, young fellow! Have you bought a chicken to train for fighting?’ I said, ‘No.’ He cried out from the mountain of self-importance, his head made heavy by the pride his fighting cock gives him: ‘However much you train it, there is no one in all the fourteen worlds who can defeat me! Raise your rooster and see! If the breath of my bird touches yours, your bird will run a kadam away.’ I too began to speak:

<!-- Tamil source: scan 118 / printed 117 -->

“‘Do not take the fame won in cockfighting for great valour and speak to me, uncle, with the fire-sparking words Cheran Senguttuvan hurled at Kanakavijayar! I bought this bird to raise it fondly. But your speech, which makes even cowardice reveal itself, has changed that thought. I give you my vow—accept it. Within two months I shall make you lose the title “Dur Neethan” you have adorned yourself with and keep thinking of. From today my rooster is a fighting rooster! Know this. I shall come.’ Saying that, I returned. The news has spread through the whole village, woman!”

“What new quarrel is this, Aththaan! Sengannan has made cockfighting his trade. Is it easy to defeat him? Do not boast! If you lose, there will be shame—shame even to step outside and walk the street! Do not be stubborn, my love. Give it up!” she said.

“If a Tamil gives a vow and then retreats, will he not have to bow his head, my gold? Do not fear! Here is one proof that our rooster is a warrior-bird. They say that one day, when its mother hen went searching for food with her brood, she fought off and drove away a hawk that came to seize them. Another day, they say, she fought and escaped when she was on the brink of becoming a feast for a treacherous fox! This rooster is her son!”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 119 / printed 118 -->

“All right, Aththaan—let the rooster-talk end... Come and relieve the sorrow of the hen!” she said, calling him to the soft bed. Even the fruit-sweet nectar offered by the queen of youth had no taste for him; the flower-bed gave no pleasure. Even when they had become veena and music together, one look at the red-eyed Enthizhai brought Sengannan to mind and confused him. Driven by the urge to win at any cost, he went into action. The rooster's war-training began.

On one side stood an unwavering faith that the child would take its mother's name; on the other, praises declaring Sengannan a master of cockfighting. As they struck at her heart, Enthizhai began to tremble more and more as the day drew near.

The day of battle came. The villagers gathered. The village elders set the condition that each side must stake two hundred gold pieces. The hall, where a purse containing four hundred gold pieces hung, filled with the people of the village.

Iniyan entered the arena with his rooster. Some old men said, “A young bird! It does not look as strong as Sengannan's.” Enthizhai heard it. Her whole body became wet with sweat!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 120 / printed 119 -->

Sengannan's rooster came with a royal gait toward the place where Iniyan's stood, and the children of the village welcomed it with clapping hands. Enthizhai could not bear it. The sight of Sengannan's bird standing taller than Iniyan's shook her heart.

The fight began. They collided, trying to peck each other, claw each other and strike fiercely with their legs! Feathers flew in the air! Bristling combs stood upright! Like whirlwinds the two roosters spun and spun in battle, and the villagers marvelled at their speed!

A peck on the head of Iniyan's bird! A flood of blood! Yet it did not tire. Another peck on its back! It could not bear the pain. A little weariness!

The moment it saw that weakness, Sengannan's bird flew at it again and again in rapid bursts and pecked! It hissed! Iniyan's bird cried out! Enthizhai could no longer stand there. She ran away.

Thinking disgrace was about to surround him, Iniyan too was shaken. Seeing Enthizhai run wounded his heart. Sengannan's laughter grew louder. The villagers clapped and roared.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 121 / printed 120 -->

Before an old man could finish saying that Iniyan's rooster would now fall and die, it launched itself at Sengannan's bird. Another leap—a wound in Sengannan's rooster's belly! Then another clash—a leap!... Great joy for Iniyan!

Suddenly Sengannan's rooster drew away, made as though to spring upon Iniyan's, and then ran out of the arena! Sengannan's face drooped in defeat.

The same villagers garlanded Iniyan. He placed the garland upon the rooster and returned home with the four hundred gold pieces. When the news of victory reached Enthizhai, who lay face down, she came to meet Aththaan and embraced him. She refused to let go. A rain of kisses—the great flood of love broke its banks and ran; a torrent of delight.

After doing what was needed to let the rooster recover from its exhaustion, she said, “Buy a cage for the rooster even if it costs all four hundred gold pieces!... From now on an iron-barred cage will not do for our rooster; it needs a cage with silver bars.” Saying “All right,” he set out to buy one.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 122 / printed 121 -->

The next morning, with the silver cage, he called Enthizhai of spear-like eyes: “Have you seen the beauty of the cage?” She came. Together they went to the iron cage and opened it to move their beloved rooster into its new home. Perhaps the rooster had no wish to live in a silver cage—for it lay dead inside the iron one!
