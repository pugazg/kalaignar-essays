# The Sculptor

<!-- Tamil source: scan 103 / printed 102 -->

A sari; in the grove, jasmine. In the jasmine, a bee. The breeze shook the jasmine... The bee thought it was fleeing in shyness... humming, it drew near... Then another jasmine flower laughed and scattered fragrance; thinking wrongly that it was calling him, the bee flew there... Before this delightful scene ended, she came as though a rose blossom were running toward it.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 104 / printed 103 -->

The woman who had come looked around the grove... and gave a deep sigh. “Nature—nature that creates poets, nature that gives birth to epics!”

She said it aloud. “O Nature that made me a poet! A poem as an offering of gratitude! Accept it.”

“O jasmine that leaps upon the mango tree—
you have none of the ‘caste’ spoken of by common fools!”

Having sung this, that cuckoo-woman skipped away. A carelessness that worried nothing about the world joined the swagger of youth. After all, was she not a fawn? She danced and sang all manner of things. She ran as she danced. She stroked the vines spread across the ground as though a statue made by a sculptor whose name had been engraved at a world-wide award ceremony as “Kalaivaanan” had come alive and wandered there. A peacock displayed its joyous dance; she competed with it and showed her own grace. Had the plants and vines of that grove possessed mouths to speak, her ears would have been deafened by their mocking words: “You mad girl!”

Her dance and song ended... She sprang onto the swing that had been waiting for her arrival; the swing flew!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 105 / printed 104 -->

The branch of the mango tree bent down to yield. Such was the speed!

“Swing as soldiers spring upon their enemies!
Swing as though flinging the drawn sword and dying!
O swing, swing!”

She instantly made a poem for the swing too! Yes—the ravishing beauty! The coral mouth that had been singing suddenly closed. Her eyes, which had been pouring coolness, became lumps of fire. Without her knowing it, her teeth bit her lips. She turned away the face whose cheeks had reddened as though pierced by spears and streaming blood, and swung again. Her eyes shot like arrows only toward one particular place; like sharp arrows striking a strong iron shield, her gaze fell back again. Yet she could not keep from looking there. “Shameful... a mere... sculptor... such arrogance?” her mouth muttered. A house stood at the edge of the grove. That house—an old house! In places its walls had collapsed. The lattice window on the upper storey increased her fury. The eyes of a man wrapped in black cloth kept looking at her through that lattice.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 106 / printed 105 -->

“Seeing a deer struggling, even a hunter sometimes changes his aim. Seeing an enemy rendered helpless, even a soldier sometimes withdraws. But this sinner... with his eyes fixed on me, he keeps pursuing me! I had only one place where I could enjoy nature. Must even this be ruined? Hm... I must not leave him alone... Who am I? Who is he? A mere sculptor! Such arrogance?... Here, I shall find destruction for that pride!”

She boiled. She imagined that her chastity would be destroyed by the gaze of an ordinary sculptor—by a violent lustful look. She looked again at the lattice. Those eyes had not left her. “Ponnazhagan... look at the name. Sculptor! May those lustful eyes go blind. Let the sculptor become sightless...” She did not continue speaking! Suddenly she ran. A good sharp black stone! She hurled it with great force! The instant she threw the stone... “Ah... my art is gone!” cried a man as he ran. The stone struck the lattice with a crack.

All her nerves trembling, she stood frozen. The running man stopped and began: “Princess!... What have you done?” Anger blocked and shut his words.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 107 / printed 106 -->

He ran again. She too ran behind him.

The man who kept running entered the house. She gave the house one sweeping look.

‘The sculptor's house’—though this murmur placed a barrier before her speed, an eagerness had been swelling in her heart strongly enough to break it at once. She ran inside. A sobbing sound from the upper floor! She moved step by step toward that sorrowful sound. “Cheran Senguttuvan!... the art I created...” The words tangled again and again with broken sobs and snapped apart.

She stood without moving. Only her nerves trembled like plucked veena strings. The statue of Cheran Senguttuvan lay shattered. The sculptor Ponnazhagan lay face down.

“Ayyo... the princess has split open and thrown away my heart! She has broken the Chera as though slicing the moon into pieces! Senguttuva... how hard I laboured to kindle light in your eyes. Those eyes lie rolled away as though they had rolled the sun itself aside!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 108 / printed 107 -->

I spent a whole day shaping your budding moustache! It lies powdered as though exclamation marks had been hacked to pieces! I set a pulse upon your lips... I set within you the thought that, even if enemies came in ranks upon ranks, you would regard them as nothing... and I took pride in it. That pride now lies destroyed here!”

Ponnazhagan lamented. He raised an oppari over the death of the memorial he had created.

Her eyes became breached reservoirs of flame. A sob violent enough to defeat the shock of an earthquake shook her. That grieving hiccup stirred feeling even in the sculptor's numbed heart and made it leap. Ponnazhagan turned. She stood there—like an uncarved statue! Water gathered in Ponnazhagan's eyes. Fire rose.

“Princess!... There is no place for Chera domination in the Pandya country. That is your law, is it not? Does a sculptor not even have the right to make a statue of the heroic Chera king Senguttuvan? If you wished, you might have beheaded me for the crime of making this statue. Without destroying the statue,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 109 / printed 108 -->

if you had planted it at least in Chera soil... I would have embraced death in fullness, saying, ‘My art lives.’ Did even this mercy fail to raise its head in your woman's heart?”

All he could receive as an answer was the princess's tears. He too became calm. “If she had meant to break the statue, would she not have been enraged? Instead she is crying!”

This doubt in the sculptor's heart allowed peace to take residence there.

When mustard is dropped into boiling oil it bursts and scatters. After the sculptor's boiling anger subsided, the princess began to speak.

“I have neither the narrow mind nor the crooked intelligence to break a statue. I know that if I break this one statue, statues of crores of Pandya kings will be broken in the Chera land. Not only that; I know too that the fame of the Pandya lineage would fall under the criticism that, because a statue was broken, one petty-minded woman had acted so. And beyond that, I know the truth that the Chera too is one of our people, artist... If the world knows why I broke this statue, it will praise you with a full mouth as ‘king of kings among masters of art’!”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 110 / printed 109 -->

A new light in the sculptor's eyes! Lines of doubt on his forehead! Pulses of eagerness on his lips! The princess continued:

“Unable to determine whether it was a man or a statue, I thought it was a man staring at me without taking his eyes away... and in anger I threw the stone. But what broke was a statue! There will be no poets enough to praise your artistic skill that had given life to a statue and made it stand!”

“What! Did the statue look at you?” The tremor on the sculptor's lips did not cease.

“Yes. That look pulled me. It pursued me without leaving me until fire rose in my heart. Only then did I throw the stone!”

“You call it a look of love!... Does my Chera not have a heroic gaze, amma?”

“Perhaps... From so far away I could not know the nature of the look.”

“Hm, a fine lesson! A beautiful woman—a tasty fruit—a fragrant flower—just as these face danger, living art faces danger too!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 111 / printed 110 -->

But only today have I learned that such danger can come in this form as well.”

“A fragrant flower, a beauty and a tasty fruit melt beneath the waves of desire that strike the mind. Was not your work of art shattered by the dagger cast by my stone-heart?”

“Stone-heart?... Did you not call it my artistic skill, Princess!”

“What I thought was a man trying to enchant my mind was your artistic skill! The mind that dared oppose the man I imagined was mine.”

“I do not understand, Princess!”

Ponnazhagan had even forgotten that the statue lay shattered. He waited for the movement of the princess's lips. She stood unable to speak. Drops of sweat on her forehead! Many flashes of lightning in her eyes! She lost the strength to stand. Without the sculptor saying a word, she sat on the pedestal lying there. She did not change her gaze. He alone kept turning the broken Chera's hand over in his own hands.

There was a man; he was no prince. In his lap, a great beauty; she was indeed a princess.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 112 / printed 111 -->

A statue like this! She looked at that statue, fixing the sculptor with her eyes...

Before she could finish asking, “This statue...?” he answered, “That is Ambikapathi and Amaravathi.” “Ambikapathi, the poet; Amaravathi, the princess! Ponnazhagan, the artist; Velvizhi, the princess.”

She could not say this aloud. In the sculptor's gaze alone, it seemed as though a spear had struck! Lines flashed across his face at lightning speed and vanished. She too understood the explanation those lines gave. The hardship of having to speak disappeared for them.

Do the red sun-ray and the red lotus flower sit talking to each other? “Princess!” he said, longing to enjoy the fruit-slice of her reply; his words too had now become sugar-candy to her.

The very Ponnazhagan at whose offence of looking at her she had raged—the very Ponnazhagan whom she had muttered about as “a mere sculptor”—she now trembled to hear that Ponnazhagan's sweet speech. Her tongue alone would not rise to speak.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 113 / printed 112 -->

Ponnazhagan continued.

“That poem you sang in the grove!... May I hear it once?”

“Poem?... Did you hear it?”

“Yes. I hear it every day.”

“Truly?”

“O jasmine that leaps upon the mango tree—
you have none of the caste spoken of by common fools!

“You set the mango tree and the jasmine in the poem and rebuked the human race... Do you remember?”

Red spread across the princess's cheeks.

The sculptor continued: “I never fail to receive the pleasure of your poetry. In the evenings it is my habit to hide behind the jasmine grove that becomes the mark of your imagination and savour your poems...”

“Why?” She was no longer in this world.

“Princess......” Ponnazhagan drew out the word.

The princess interrupted: “I am Velvizhi!”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 114 / printed 113 -->

She bent down in shyness.

“Velvizhi!”

“My love!”

The statue of Ambikapathi and Amaravathi seemed to bless them!
