# Yaazh

<!-- Tamil source: scan 96 / printed 95 -->

“While bees at your side hum splendidly,
wearing the jewelled garment of flowers,
opening your black carp-eyes and swaying,
you walked—hail, Kaveri!”

Azhagurumbi sat bewildered, wondering whether the yaazh was the instrument in the hand of the woman who poured out the sweet taste of the Kaanal Vari song—or whether the yaazh was the sound between her lips. His eyes judged that the two ends of the beautiful yaazh laid across the lofty maapakam had no equal, and then gazed in wonder at the bodily form of Thenmozhi, the lady of fortune. She is the yaazh! Her form is yaazh! Her speech is yaazh!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 97 / printed 96 -->

Azhagurumbi sat motionless before her, his mind intoxicated. The music hall delighted in saying that until now no one had been born who could, like her, sing honeyed Tamil to all eight directions and pluck the strings of the yaazh.

“If those fingers that awaken so many feelings in the strings of the yaazh were to touch the strings of my heart, could I not hear crores and crores of tales of delight? Lord! By your grace, may this sweet-spoken woman become mine!” Azhagurumbi yearned.

When the sound of the yaazh ceased, the great musicians of the hall remembered that they belonged to this world and went home. Like an ant that had swarmed over jaggery, Azhagurumbi began to walk behind her.

Was she a painting come alive—or a great fortune gained by the world—walking away after stealing life with incomparable beauty? He kept sighing. “If the beauty from behind alone brings me to this state, what will become of me if I see the beauty in front, the beauty of that jasmine smile, and the beauty of the lips that gather as though to string a pearl garland?” he asked himself as he followed.

She had enchanted the hall. She had blended her beauty with cultivated Tamil music and offered it. She took up the yaazh. She became the yaazh.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 98 / printed 97 -->

She gave forth a sound declaring that none, whoever they might be, were her equal! All the ideas given by the Vedas would burn away in the light given by her eyes. She walked toward home!

The maiden did not know that behind her a mountain of unending desire was staggering along in thirst!

She entered the house—a dove gone into its nest, my dove who plays the yaazh. Standing at the doorway, senseless Azhagurumbi thought: “Who but me, the prince of this land, can possess her?”

As he stood looking upward, the moon-disc hung in the sky to shake the very foundation of his heart—and at its centre was a figure: her figure. She had come to the moonlit courtyard to rest. The prince, son of the crowned king, saw her whole form; the last of the three divisions rose to his head, and he knocked on the house door! A companion ran and opened it. “Let my beautiful queen know that the prince has come,” he said.

She ran to the beauty and said, “Honey-tongued one! The prince of the land has knocked at the street door and stands there with his whole body a pool of sweat. With my eyes I saw shyness hanging upon shoulders famed for courage—and embarrassment dancing in the moustache that sings of valour.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 99 / printed 98 -->

Shall I send him away—or invite him in?” she asked.

“Bring him in,” she said, and entered her chamber of adornment. He came; she too came, with the yaazh in her hand!...

“Thenmozhi,” he said. “Greetings,” she replied, and, finding no path to open her mouth further, began playing the yaazh and singing.

“I did not come to hear the yaazh, my dear,” he said.

“Then what did you come to ask for?” she asked.

“To ask for you!”

“To ask for me?”

“Yes, beautiful one. On this earth I know no thought but you. Take up residence in my heart this very instant,” he said.

“Prince! You have calculated wrongly, thinking me merely a courtesan; you have also forgotten that I have a husband. Do not destroy Tamilakam, which gives honour to chastity. Go!” she said angrily.

“Only today have I heard that there is a man who married you, maiden. Show me who he is;

<!-- Tamil source: scan 100 / printed 99 -->

I shall separate his legs from his arms, and we shall keep the bedchamber for ourselves,” he said.

“O shoot of tyranny born in a dynasty of sceptre-bearing kings, know this: the three things I will not dare to lose are my husband, my yaazh and my rare chastity!” she said, rising.

Like fire leaping upon oil—like molten matter flowing in the great breath of a volcano—he rushed upon her with speed. Crying “Ayyo!” she ran into the next room and fell weeping upon her sleeping husband. Her husband opened his eyes and, seeing the prince standing before him, boiled with rage.

“Chastity for a courtesan? The world will laugh! Man who stands here as her husband!... Give me the lump of jaggery to taste!... Otherwise you must become prey to my sword,” the prince roared.

Thenmozhi's husband was a man with no way even to stand—a cripple without legs. Seeing this, the prince grew bolder and touched and kissed the peahen. Unable to bear the cruelty happening before his eyes, the cripple lifted an earthen tub there and hurled it at the prince's head. Seeing it, the unjust man thrust out his sword and sprang at him. To stop it,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 101 / printed 100 -->

Thenmozhi seized the yaazh and flung it against the sword. The sword broke; the yaazh shattered; and the jewel-voiced woman fell to the ground!

“Once the yaazh is gone, what does it matter what else is lost? Come, let us live, woman!” said Nambi, gathering her into his arms.

“If the yaazh is gone I can buy another. But if my chastity and my husband are gone, there is no market where I can buy them—know that!” she said. Taking the broken sword by its hilt, she told the prince, “Run while your life remains.”

Seeing her take up the sword, the prince stood against her holding the broken yaazh.

“I will not cut my yaazh with your sword; nor will you leave us alive! Of the three things I had to protect I have lost one. I was not born to lose my chastity and make my husband your slave. Look!” she said, and sprang toward her husband.

“My precious Aththaan! I will not send you alive to a chamber of torture. Let both of us leave this world together,” she said. “All right,” said her husband.

She drove the broken sword into her husband's chest—and raised that same sword into her own heart.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 102 / printed 101 -->

Their two bodies fell upon the broken yaazh!

The villain could not seize the three—chastity, husband, yaazh. Saying that her resolve had won, the king's son stood ashamed. His eyes became waterfalls as he left the house!... In every direction he went, the sound of the yaazh pursued him.
