# Letter

<!-- Tamil source: scan 123 / printed 122 -->

My love, I am writing the ninth letter. You took my hand saying that there would always be a flood of delight. Instead—a rain of sorrow! A serial tale of grief! If I merely say, “Was it for this that I was born a woman?” what else is there for me to write? If I ask, “When will you come, with the face that is medicine for the pain of my heart?”—must you display this useless dignity precisely when you are being invited to a feast!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 124 / printed 123 -->

A warrior who returned with a broken leg told me that the war had ended, that many days had passed since all the blood soaking the battlefield had dried, and that neither crow nor eagle circles there now. He himself told me: “Woman, the fame your lover won on the field is great fame!” I heard that too.

For me, who had been waiting for the time when a call of “My dear!” followed by a handsome young bull would enter the house and hunt me once again, that news was sweetness indeed! But then why this? You slipped away saying, “After destroying the enemies, I shall have your lips press their herbal cure upon my scars!”

“When I throw the spear, it is your eyes that will stand in my memory! When I whirl the sword, it will be your flash of light—your parrot-speech!” Only today do I learn the truth: those were lies you poured over me in abundance simply to take leave and go! You stood yearning over how the night hours would pass in the camp; I leaned against your swelling shoulder! Doubt now makes me ask whether you were truly that man! Kovalan sent, through his own mouth—through Vasanthamalai—a reply that broke the heart of Madhavi, who had written her letter on a white screw-pine petal, dipping the rich champak bud in red cotton-dye. You, Aththaan, are elder brother even to him!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 125 / printed 124 -->

He reproached her: “I know Madhavi's kankoodu-vari—the woman who deceived me with bubble-flower nose, blue-lily eyes, and a mouth whose speech is like the kovvai fruit!” Your silence shows a cruelty greater even than that! He insulted the jewel of chastity Madhavi, saying that he had seen in that courtesan the theatrical kaan-vari pose that comes when told “come” and goes when told “go”! I say you are worse than he, because you remain without thought of me!

“I know Madhavi's ul-vari performance: when I wither in the intoxication of love at evening, she hides her peacock grace and swan gait and comes before me in the guise of a serving woman,” Kovalan growled. I say you stand one step above him in deception, because you have forgotten me for so many days!

“I know the puravari Madhavi learned: she comes before me with girdle ringing, anklets cooing and love swelling, curls up before me, and leaves without embracing me!” You are a greater deceiver than that merchant, because you have not written even one line to this woman!

He took a word spoken in lovers' sulking as a word of true anger and described Madhavi as performing the kilar-vari. After seeing her letter...

<!-- Tamil source: scan 126 / printed 125 -->

Aththaan, you are even more unjust than he, because you do not wish to bring relief to my suffering! Kovalan said he knew well Madhavi, who had learned the therchi-vari that grieves in separation, the kaatchi-vari that complains of love in the place of separation, and the eduththukkol-vari that displays unbearable sorrow while seeking consolation from kin because her man has not returned. Yet even he cannot be used as a comparison for you, Aththaan; for he did not leave as soon as he had joined Madhavi... Their place was a school where every delight of the world was practised—there Madhavi and Kovalan took turns being teachers! Even Kovalan, who reproached Madhavi as one learned in all eight forms of vari-koothu in dramatic arrangement, did not fail to praise her beauty. They remained together all those days; unlike you, he did not run off after a single day to drive away an advancing enemy.

I am sending this ninth letter as the last one, trusting that you will return before I begin to think you have become Kovalan. In the Tamil of the Silambu, in the grove of sensual delight, you would scatter words such as, “See the moon-face on which the fish is drawn, the bow is drawn, the cloud is drawn, and the deeds of Kama are drawn!” Have you found no time to write even one letter, Aththaan? Go then, Aththaan—wicked Aththaan!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 127 / printed 126 -->

Madhavi at least had the little child Manimekalai for comfort when she made a garland of champak, green leaves, dark buds, white flowers, jasmine and red water-lilies, placed her letter within it and sent it to her lover! Who do I have? I do not even have that!

“The young prince
of sweet summer,
who joins all living beings
with their beloved mates;

even the wealthy moon
that rises at evening
through the budding darkness—
he too is not just;

though those united
let a little time come between them,
though those separated
forget their own companions,

to take good life
with arrows of fragrant flowers
is no wonder—
know this.”

The thought of the letter Madhavi wrote to Kovalan applies to me as well!

Even if lovers who joined in joy forget each other for a little while, even if separated lovers forget their companions, it is no new thing that summer and the moon kill life with soft flower-arrows—this is what Madhavi told Kovalan through her letter. I too remind you of the same.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 128 / printed 127 -->

—Your loving beloved
