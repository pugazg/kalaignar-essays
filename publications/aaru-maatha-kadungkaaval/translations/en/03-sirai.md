# Prison

---

<!-- Tamil source: scan 108 / illustrated intertitle: சிறை -->

**PRISON**

<!-- Tamil source: scan 109 / blank-reverse; no independent printed text -->

<!-- Tamil source: scan 110 / printed folio not visible -->

...the sun, seeing upon the earth a red that could conquer even the twilight sky it creates, lowered its head in shame and disappeared. Inside the police security cordon—at Kallakkudi—we lay curled up, hearing these sorrowful tidings!

It must have been past midnight. Mani, Muthu and the other friends brought us half a masala-vadai and a quarter-cup of tea! That was the excellent supper that reached us at the proper hour. The thirty-six of us slept with one man's leg serving as another man's pillow. The doors were locked. Reserve Police stood guard with rifles. The Sub-Inspector who was with us suddenly began counting us in the middle of the night. What a wonder: all thirty-six were correctly there. As usual, after counting two or three times, his arithmetic came to an end.

The next day dawned with the calm that follows a storm!

We received word that Kannadasan and other friends had been admitted to hospital.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 111 / printed 108 -->

Comrade Radhakrishnan of Tiruchi Kalaimagal Press, a man of love for the country and love for the Dravidian people, was arrested. Comrade J. M. Sheriff of the Tiruchi Muslim Youth Front, who went to see what had happened to him, was also detained.

More than eighty comrades—including Thangapazham, Sivasamy, K. V. K. Sami, Natarasan and Rathinam—men of action from Thoothukudi, lion-like bulls with hearts that could bear anything, beloved jewels of the movement who would not deviate even by a mustard-seed's measure from dignity, discipline and the sense of duty—were arrested under a plan to implicate them in a conspiracy case.

More than fifty comrades who went to picket before Achariyar's house in Madras, along with the heroic woman Comrade Sathyavani Muthu Ammaiyar who led that march, and Kanchi Rajagopal, were arrested on July 14.

On the 15th, N. S. Ilango—the fearless heart given by Thanjavur soil, a man whose force could drive arrogance away, commander of a joyous battle—who led the picketing struggle, and more than fifty comrades including Vedachalam, were arrested in Madras!

On the 16th, under the leadership of Perur Subramaniam—a man with a firmness of mind unmoved even if a thousand hardships pile one upon another, and in North Arcot district a force unto the party—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 112 / printed 109 -->

more than fifty friends were arrested in the street of Achariyar's house.

With our ears filled by news that the prisons were filling—overflowing—and our thoughts filled with throbbing, we sat under guard.

At ten in the morning on the 16th, the D.S.P. came. He ordered that all of us be sent at once to Ariyalur and left.

At noon, food was served in the police cell. That was the last meal we ate on a leaf!

The meal ended—the police van came and stopped. We all climbed in and sat down. The van set off toward Ariyalur.

Yes—our prison pilgrimage had begun.

A motorcar followed our van. It came behind us for a short distance. Two or three people inside saluted us. My brother Arangannal was in that car too. Stretching out his loving hand, he bade me, “Go and return.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 113 / printed 110 -->

The memory of him carried me somewhere far away—into a long train of thought. The police van went on throwing up dust. The motorcar behind us disappeared into the dust and turned another way. But “Arangu” did not disappear from my mind.

The year was 1940—I was studying at the school in Tiruvarur. Even then, more of my attention went to the movement than to my studies. Inside the school itself I threw myself intensely into movement propaganda, circulating movement journals, producing handwritten copies, and such work. At that time V. Ko. Shanmugam, known as “Maavengko,” worked with me as both companion and equal in movement service. He is now in poor health. In my school days I had two sworn enemies. One was Venkatachalam, a green-khadi-shirt man. He has now—indeed, even then—come over to the Dravidian movement. The other was Rangasamy. The very sight of me was bitter neem to him! That young friend, wearing a stiff khadi shirt with the Congress emblem on it, needed only to see me walking along the school corridor: he would join his group and pour out mocking words in noisy merriment. Sometimes he would even throw a small stone.

I would ask myself why this Rangasamy had such a fever against me—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 114 / printed 111 -->

I would wonder—I would feel hurt—but I would not grow angry. That was why Rangasamy could so quickly become Arangannal. In the first play we staged for the Tamil Students' Association fund, *Pazhaniyappan*, Comrade Rangasamy—no, Brother Arangannal—played the role of Doctor Vedanthangi. Rangasamy became Arangannal! Arangannal became a stage doctor—and now he has become one of the doctors curing the country's decline—through writing! Through speech! Through incomparable action! To him I am—and to me he is—*udanpirappu*.

My deep stream of thought about Arangu was stopped by a voice: “We have reached Ariyalur.” There was a huge crowd at the entrance to the Ariyalur Sub-Jail. Knowing that we were coming, people had gathered in large numbers. We got down from the vehicle. We saluted the crowd. They greeted us. We were taken inside.

The Ariyalur Sub-Jail could hold fewer than twenty prisoners. Many prisoners of other kinds were already there before us. After we arrived, the total rose above sixty. Four or five cages were allotted to our thirty-six men. Only two people could stay comfortably in one cage. Seven—eight—were shut into each.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 115 / printed 112 -->

**Our cage:**

I—Sakthi—Kasthuri—Ramasubbiah—Venugopal—Malaikottai Rathinam—Ariyalur Ethiraj—and one more friend: eight of us were confined. Eight men in that cell, about ten feet long and eight feet wide!

Even there, more than half the space could not be used. It was a cage in which women had been confined. When we arrived they moved those women elsewhere and gave the place to us. It stank of urine so badly that we had to thrust all our noses outside the bars just to breathe.

At five in the evening they opened the cage. Those who needed to relieve themselves were told they could go, and a place called the “kakkoos” was shown to us. Only those who knew the “long jump” could get inside and sit.

The reason was that the stones laid for placing one's feet were set so far apart from one another.

Then they called us to the eating place. On a floor mixed with rubbish and refuse—yes, on dusty earth—they told us to sit. We sat. The food came. No—no... a pot came for each man—a clay pot! Only after that did the food come.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 116 / printed 113 -->

You have heard of a six-flavoured meal—I have heard of it too. We have been told that a six-flavoured meal means delicious, excellent food—food rich in six kinds of taste.

The six-flavoured meal given to us at Ariyalur had a new meaning.

**Six-flavoured—flavour severed.**

**Food—even calling it a meal is cruelty.**

They poured that flavourless cruelty into clay pots. Yellow-coloured rice! Cotton-coloured curry! In salt—in sourness—in heat—the piece of pumpkin that had independently fallen in without tiring itself in any of them: we picked it up and put it in our mouths! Somehow, swimming against the current, it went down into the stomach. All the comrades sat without touching the rice, looking only at me. “Come, eat,” I said, pinched some rice and put it in my mouth. A drunkard will sing, “The whole cosmos is spinning, brother!” We did not sing, “From the pit of the stomach it is heaving, brother!” Yet we could not keep the grief within from showing on our faces.

We threw the balls of rice onto the ground—washed the pots and put them away—and all thirty-six of us formed ranks, four by four!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 117 / printed 114 -->

Then, as before, we were shut into the cages. Eight men in our “Black Hole of Calcutta”! Night came—after two days without proper food, did sleep at least come to us? Not that either! Even if it came, where was the space to lie down? Insects came—mosquitoes came—the most important thing that did not come was air! Eight men—the sweat poured—and if we took off our shirts, the mosquitoes drilled into us! Three slept sitting in the corner—Venu, Rathinam and Ethiraj. In the filthy part, Sakthi stretched only his legs and curled his body like a worm. Kasthuri lay bent with his head near Sakthi's feet. With troubled eyes Ramasubbiah looked at me and asked me to rest on his lap. I tried—it was impossible. Then I found a way. A little air would come; I would not have to curl my body. With that thought I held the prison bars and slept for a while standing up. It may have been two in the morning—there was no way to know the exact time—the Deputy Superintendent came. “Why are you standing? Haven't you slept?” he asked. “It is all right,” I said. He went away.

Somehow, that night in Ariyalur jail we experienced a hardship that until then we had never touched in life. With enormous difficulty we pushed the night along.

In the morning they gave us rice gruel. We took care not to let it go cold before drinking it. They locked us up again.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 118 / printed 115 -->

That day was July 17. At the picketing before Achariyar's house in Madras, more than thirty people were arrested under the leadership of Comrade M. S. Ramasamy, the warrior given by Aruppukkottai. Kannadasan, who had been injured at Kallakkudi and was in hospital, was also arrested.

We remained shut in the cage, consumed by the desire to know what was happening outside!

In the afternoon they opened the cage for food. We could tell well in advance when the warder was coming to open us. The moment the key-ring in his hand sounded, Ramasubbiah would say, “They are going to open it!” That afternoon we got a chance to meet the other prisoners there.

There were four or five elderly mothers in the jail. They had been arrested in liquor cases. We saw a young man of about twenty-five. He was accused of killing his lover's husband because of an illicit affair. A sturdy young man. His lover too was confined there, in another cage. The case was that the affection he bore her and the love she bore him had carried them into the crime of killing the husband.
