# The Drum

---

<!-- Tamil source: scan 10 / printed folio not visible -->

On the morning of June 15, the aeroplane that left Madras for Tiruchi lifted Comrade Mullai Sakthi and me and flew off with us. Though the plane was travelling only at its necessary, ordinary speed, we felt as though it were tearing through the wind and clouds at an extraordinary pace. In truth, the flying machine itself was not moving so fast. It was the speed of our minds that was like that. A freshness we had never known before. An itch in the shoulders. A dance of delight filling the heart. Why?

The field of struggle was at Kallakkudi. I was its commander. Sakthi, hearing that happy news, came along with me. What more was needed to bring both our hearts to such a state? Our Purananuru tells us that, street after street, tiger-like Tamils once lived in many a home, bows in hand. They would be lying on flower-strewn beds; the war drum would sound—and, saying joyfully that this might even be the last kiss, they would bury their love in the cheeks of tear-filled sweethearts, slap their shoulders, and run to battle.

The heroic poems of the Tamils that remain to us have indeed kept telling us those sweet tidings

<!-- Tamil source: scan 11 / printed 10 -->

to this day.

If descendants of that line hear that an order has come from their leader—beat the drum against the wicked who refuse to come to reason by any sacred means except struggle—if they kiss that order and march toward the field beating the drum, why should delight not rise! Why should enthusiasm not surge! Why should a joyous melody not spring up! Was it not the “boon” we had won by asking Anna: “Do you not have faith in the blood that races hot through us—in the taut nerves—in the swelling mountain-like shoulders—in the battle methods filled with the strength of your younger brothers?” We received the boon. We received new vigour! We flew toward the battlefront.

We could not tell whether the wings belonged to the aircraft or to us. We felt such tremendous speed. A district-wide tour had been arranged to explain to the people of Tiruchi district why the agitation had become necessary. On June 14 I had a responsibility in Madras to act in a benefit play for a college. It was for Thiagarayar College, named after the jewel-like lamp, Thiagaraya Periyar, who sowed the seeds of awakening in Dravidian society and of the rising that sought a new path. The play was “Parabrahmam.” A tremendous collection. Sivaji Ganesan, the distinguished actor who had written a new epic in the dramatic art of the South, and other friends acted in it. After finishing the play at night, because it was important to begin the Tiruchi tour the next day,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 12 / printed 11 -->

we chose the air route.

I do not know what thoughts occupied the other passengers on the plane; Sakthi and I, from above, looked down upon the earth. Mother Dravida, draped in green silk, seemed to be caressing and praising the great farmers with her river-hands. A majestic form—eyes full of tenderness—a face pouring compassion—these were ornaments upon our beautiful mother. Yet the lofty temple towers looked like little blemishes rising upon her face. We thought of our mother as one enslaved. Pools gathered in our eyes! Seeing the mother's condition, we turned to look whether we two alone, up there in the sky, were weeping.

Beside us, a young woman too showed on her face that her heart was crying. At first we could not understand why that young woman, employed in the responsible duty of attending to air passengers, looked so stricken. Her office was to keep travellers from feeling fatigue—to keep fear from arising—to keep thoughts of accidents away—to offer a dignified smile—to hand out newspapers with courtesy—to serve fruit juice—to perform little services and keep them at ease. Why had this young woman in such a post fallen into a well of sorrow? Those questioning eyes pierced my friend Sakthi even more sharply than they pierced me.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 13 / printed 12 -->

“What is the matter, sir?” he asked me, in words full of the quick concern natural to him. He asked. He kept asking. Then he himself became clear enough to explain the reason to me. There were a few letters in her reddish hands. I call them letters; whether she called them a gold mine, or treasure newly unearthed, I do not know. For they were letters from her lover. Somewhere far away must have been the man yearning to win that slender-waisted woman as his companion. It was he who was now speaking to her through those old leaves.

Memory—what a delightful pain it can give. Was she alone to be exempt from it? Old letters in her hands. New storms in her heart.

So too—

in our memory, the old histories of the Tamils. But our present condition? Earthquake—volcano—deluge! In the lover's letter she may have been described as, “Spotless gold! Right-spiralled pearl! Flawless fragrance! Sugarcane! Honey!”

Yet the former glory of the Tamils—the tale of gold and pearls and sandalwood from the Podhigai hills being shipped to Egypt and Athens—has not even had the fortune of becoming a letter.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 14 / printed 13 -->

Her lover who wrote that letter may have sung a love-song in the spirit of:

“What gem shall I call you, not born in a mountain?
What music shall I call you, not born in a lute?”

We, with weary pride, sang another hymn:

“What land shall I call you, where Akbar never entered?
What earth shall I call you, where Kanishka never entered?”

That love letter would have said: “We parted—we shall unite.”

“We joined those with whom we should not have joined—we shall part,” we told ourselves with a deep sigh—we who were travelling as soldiers of the movement that demanded a separate Dravida.

Ah—how much pride there is now in saying “we”! “We too have given our names to the ranks that will guard the nation—we too”—do you know with what feeling that is said? An indescribable feeling—a feeling only freedom fighters can experience and know. Even in her sorrow, that woman carried out her office—her duty—properly. So too we flew like wing-beating pigeons to do our duty.

The plane flew on, frightening the clouds into flight—and suddenly—do not be alarmed, there was no accident—it touched the boundary of Tiruchi

<!-- Tamil source: scan 15 / printed 14 -->

itself. The airport at Tiruchi lies close to the Central Prison. As we descended, we joked that if we happened to be arrested in the agitation, the place we would have to come to was conveniently nearby. Two men welcomed us with smiling faces: Dravida Pannai Muthukrishnan and Tiruchi secretary Mani—both of them! It is essential that I introduce those two to you.

If I were to describe every comrade who appears in this heap of writing, no volume would contain it. Yet I consider it a necessary and useful effort, and whenever their names occur here and there I wish at least to touch the first line of the proud poem of praise that belongs to their pure service.

Dravida Pannai Muthukrishnan is short in stature, able to charm others with a small smile, possessed of a tongue that speaks anything firmly, and a heart that melts if even an enemy speaks with a little smile. Such a tender-hearted man. Through his Pannai he shattered to pieces the notion that only the people of the agraharam knew how to publish books beautifully. Through his publishing house he gave polish to Anna's works of beauty! Such

a man once had a time when he concentrated only on his own business. Very soon he became a movement worker—when the North Indian minister Mehtab came, he showed the black flag, was imprisoned, was beaten by the police, and then grew to the point of becoming one of the foremost men of the movement in Tiruchi—no, it was the dignified principles of the movement that drew him in. Even without accepting prominent posts, he has never failed to give the Tiruchi organisation as much service as he could, small financial help, and assistance as an adviser to the constructive-work committee concerned with the movement's growth.

The other man who came to receive us was M. S. Mani. In earlier days I did not like him very much. Why? Whenever I went to Tiruchi, he would sit in the movement office upstairs at Dravida Pannai, keeping accounts and writing letters with the briskness peculiar to him, but he would barely even speak properly with me. Later, as my connection with the Tiruchi organisation grew stronger and stronger, Comrade Mani became very dear to me. His devotion to duty, and the troubles he willingly undertook for it, won my sympathy without my even knowing it and went and placed itself around Mani's neck like a garland. For ten days—or twenty—he would carry on party work without worrying about his family. Upstairs at Dravida Pannai

he would curl up and sleep without even a pillow. His son—I have forgotten the boy's name; I used to call him “Mangini”—would come and stand there saying, “Mother asked you to come.” Mani would send him away saying he would come in the evening. Evening would arrive. But Mani would have left on movement work for another town! The Tiruchi organisation has acquired such a young machine. Eyes behind spectacles! Dark complexion! An attractive figure! That is Mani! He has much desire and enthusiasm to speak at meetings—and he is meeting with success in that effort.

With Muthu and Mani welcoming us, we left the airport. The car moved on. We decided to stop on the way to see Parangusam. “See Parangusam? What! Did he not come to the airport?”—those comrades who knew him will suddenly feel like asking that! Yes; there was good reason why we went to see that friend who, before our plane could even land, would ordinarily have come flying some distance through the sky itself, stretching out the long “ē” in an affectionate voice—“Come! Come!”—with all his warmth and love. Endless labour for the district conference had worn down his body; a severe illness had seized him; amid doubts whether he would survive, Parangusam lay in hospital. Merely thinking of “Para” gives me new enthusiasm. A

<!-- Tamil source: scan 20 / printed 17 -->

guileless young breeze stands before me, smiles from the corner of its lips, strokes my thoughts, and sings a living epic. A small salary; the burden of supporting a family full of children; and, unable to cool the fever of principle within him, the desire to work without rest—the way he sacrificed his youth between those two, and the hardships he undertook, had made him—yes, “Para”—pure gold of the highest touch.

When he saw me in the hospital, tears flowed from his eyes. Not from worry over his health! Because he could not come beat the war drum with us! “A war without a sword? A field without ‘Para’?” I too asked myself. The chapter of consolation ended. Then we took leave and began carrying out our travel plan.

**First day:**

From July 15, the movement's daily newspaper *Nam Nadu* began publication under the administration of Ponnambalanar, the embodiment of integrity. From that very day, explanatory meetings for the agitation to remove the name “Dalmiyapuram” began. On the first day, the drum thundered at Manalmedu. Manalmedu is a beautiful little village. Eye-catching groves. Cultured youngsters there. It lay along the Salem–Tiruchi road, where it had the chance to touch

<!-- Tamil source: scan 21 / printed 18 -->

and witness political ferment. By six in the evening, Mani, Muthu, Sakthi, Sivaprakasam and I had all reached the village. Sivaprakasam, whom I introduce to you here, has been known to me since his youth. He took so active a part in the movement that even I found myself thinking, “Would he too come this far!” He was joint editor of the paper *Theeppori*. A good young man. A student of Salem College. A B.A. graduate. He too spoke that day. The meeting was a fine success. Those movement comrades of Manalmedu—good heavens, what powerful young men! Symbols of enthusiasm—golden rays of the movement. The way they ran about—the songs of enthusiasm they raised—these are scenes that will not leave the heart! The first meeting announcing the righteous struggle ended in complete success. And, as though crowning all that success, Dravidar Kazhagam comrades from Chittoor and other places around Manalmedu—taking their leader's command that they must not even stand in our shadow as a kind of ironic praise—happily garlanded us on behalf of their organisation!

People gathered in their thousands, so many that one might have feared little Manalmedu would collapse beneath them. They saw—they listened—they benefited—they gave us strength as well. After finishing our night meal, we came by way of Musiri, met and spoke with the comrades there, and returned to Tiruchi at exactly 1:30.

**The next day:**

There was a meeting at Manapparai. We were getting ready to leave for it when the district secretary, Anbil Dharmalingam, arrived. Anbil is a small village in Tiruchi district. A minor panchayat. It has a high school too. But when you say “Anbil,” our movement people do not remember the village; the name of Dharmalingam itself comes and stands before them. Everyone says only, “Anbil came—Anbil went.” And when I say “came—went,” even that has meaning. It is difficult for him to remain in one place for even a few hours. He has that much work. Everyone says, “He came—he went.” I will be looking for him; people will come to me and ask whether he came to see me. Not only movement work: he has a special taste for helping others in ways that benefit them, and for running about and striving to make such help happen. Does someone have to win an election? Anbil will be there—without pay, and without compromising principle! He trusts anyone easily; as a result he is sometimes betrayed, and then the sight of him grieving over himself is pitiable even to us. At the Lalgudi conference, nearly seven thousand rupees were left as surplus, and the district earned the verdict that the conference was “a record of awakening”; the credit belongs to the reception committee that had Comrade Anbil as its head.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 23 / printed 20 -->

As far as I am concerned, Anbil is a plaything for me—a spear that brings down those who do mischief—an army for the movement! A camp for Tiruchi district! Short in stature—bulging eyes—a guileless heart—that is Anbil: the home of affection!

He, I, Mani, Sakthi, Muthu, Sivaprakasam and Manickam set out for the Manapparai meeting.

We entered Manapparai. Red-flag comrades went in a small procession shouting “Zindabad!” They were congratulating the Communist comrade who had won that day's panchayat election in Manapparai. We went into the town. News reached us that Dhanapal, whom our movement had supported, had also won as panchayat chairman. Not only on the Manapparai Panchayat Board; throughout the Madras State, in a greater number of places, frontline comrades of the movement, movement sympathisers and movement functionaries had gained the opportunity to sit on panchayat boards. After the general election, that was the good verdict the country had delivered against the wicked. Ilamuruganar, Porselvi Ammaiyar and movement comrades welcomed us as we arrived with the news of victory.

It is known throughout the country that Porselvi and Ilamurugu entered into a love marriage across social divisions, and that they are living grammar for the verse: “In love—in poetry, speech enough for the field; for husband and wife, Dravida itself is breath.” Flag-hoisting ceremonies had been arranged at many places in the town.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 24 / printed 21 -->

At one upstairs house—right at the top of the upper floor—they asked me to hoist the movement flag. Somewhat surprised, I looked at Comrade Ilamurugu. He explained: the owner of the house was a young man newly come to the party; the well-known Congressman Ponnambala Gounder was his paternal uncle; his attachment to the party had begun only after the Lalgudi conference—and so on.

Then came flag-hoistings—a procession—a huge public meeting under the chairmanship of a Muslim friend; everything went off happily. Afterwards we ate at the house of that newcomer to the party. The newcomer was shy even to speak with us. When Sakthi and I looked at him we were mutually surprised—but he later gave us a still greater surprise. That young comrade, who had joined the party only after the Lalgudi conference, came to the volunteer camp at two-thirty in the morning on the eve of the Kallakkudi agitation and desperately begged me to include him in the battle ranks—and in the very first contingent! An aged mother—a young wife—a household with no support but himself—without worrying about any of it, he roared, “Write my name too

<!-- Tamil source: scan 25 / printed 22 -->

in the ranks!” That newcomer to the party—a dark-complexioned man with a smiling face—possessed both the eyes of our movement: dignity and discipline. He won a place in the ranks; in prison he also won the prize of six months. One of our five—Kasthuri Raj, the new bull of the party! We thanked him for his hospitality and reached Tiruchi at two in the morning, pleading even with sleep to come to us.

**Third day:**

A play was staged at Anbil. Besides presiding over it, I gave an explanatory speech on the Kallakkudi struggle, praised the public who pledged their support to the agitation, and returned to Tiruchi that very night. The next day, June 18, there was a meeting at Kulithalai. Not merely a meeting—the Dalmiyapuram Agitation Action Committee was to meet there as well. Not only Tiruchi district; all Dravida waited for the committee's decision. Word had spread that it was there the date of the agitation would be fixed. At four in the evening, the seven action-committee members—I, Anbil, Mani, Ilamurugu, Thambusamy, Manickam and Kulithalai Raja—met and discussed the circumstances of the agitation for more than two hours. Outside, a great many comrades waited eagerly for the committee's decision. The thousands who had gathered like a flood for the public meeting kept their eyes fixed on the road, waiting to hear when the committee would come out and announce its decision

<!-- Tamil source: scan 26 / printed 23 -->

When I told them that the committee had resolved to begin the agitation on July 15 if our demand was ignored, and to seek the permission of General Secretary Anna for it—the way the assembled people danced and sang—ah! it had to be seen in person; what words can describe it! “War,” I said—“We are ready to march,” their eyes replied!

“July 15,” I said—“Must we wait that long?” their sighs replied!

Kallakkudi won the honour of becoming the siege-field of the great army of Dravidian racial awakening; Kulithalai won the honour of being the place where the day was fixed. Kulithalai—a town where delight itself seems to play.

On the banks of the singing Kaveri, banana groves bend under the weight of their abundance, like Kanaka and Vijaya bowing their heads, unable to bear the stone carried by the Chera. Fertile lands; streets where warriors with sword-like eyes and unfailing valour move about.

People speak of coolness as delight. It is such a fine town that one wonders whether a place lush with coolness became *Kulirthalai* and then *Kulithalai*. There are so many Kulithalais in Dravida

<!-- Tamil source: scan 27 / printed 24 -->

but there are some people with mere sparrow-heads who cannot realise it! When I think of that, I also remember that among those Kulithalais there are Raja “Cholagars” and Muthukrishnans strengthened by principle. The thought gives sweetness too. Raja will never say something cannot be done. He believes that saying so would hurt another. He has the courage to ignore opposition and shake it off like dust. Comrade Raja is a son of the family of heroes renowned as the Cholagar family.

Comrade Muthukrishnan is possessed of the resolve always, at every moment, to do something fitting for the movement's growth. Kulithalai has countless comrades as warriors, not these two alone. There are many towns like Kulithalai. There are many hamlets and villages overflowing with noble workers and hearts full of strength. All must become our camps. In some camps there will be many weapons but few warriors to wield them. In others there will be many warriors but few weapons. The frontline fighters in each town must come forward both to increase the weapons and to enlarge the number of warriors to match them. The moment I say “weapons of war,” this Government of astonishing heroism may take fright—and even if it does not, parties that openly practise violence will go about beating the drum and awarding us the title “advocates of violence”! By “weapons of war” I mean members of the movement, and by “warriors” I mean executive-committee members. I say this not out of fear, but out of the dignity that must be observed in politics!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 28 / printed 25 -->

Even when no fierce battle is upon us, warriors have work in the camps! Keeping the spear-point from growing blunt—testing the strength of the sword—training—enlarging the army—and many more such tasks! Our movement camps too must engage in duties of this kind.

The rolls of warriors need not grow merely so they may take part in righteous struggles—there is much constructive work for them. Nor must they take part only in constructive work conducted in the movement's name or announced from its headquarters; movement warriors must acquire the habit of doing public service even when townspeople face troubles that have nothing to do with politics. Such forces must multiply in cities and in the smallest villages. So long as no harm is done to movement principles, and so long as it increases the people's affection for the movement and its warriors, the habit of such service must continue.

Nor must we forget that this service must not become a net cast merely “to attract the people.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 29 / printed 26 -->

Such eyewash is the drama staged by khadi-shirt men under names like the Harijan movement. We labour to obtain Dravida Nadu. We ask for Dravida Nadu for the Dravidian people. We must show concern, as far as we can, in the everyday lives of the Dravidian people too. Even though we say Dravida must separate precisely so that all the miseries of daily life may be removed—suppose, for example, a house catches fire. Can we sit still saying that such daily struggles will not occur once Dravida separates? We must run to put out the fire. In every town there must be a force ready to run like that. It must be a band of brave men surpassing even the fire brigade.

A man lies dead with no one to claim him—one man? Under the rule of these noble souls (?) a thousand may die like that! Before the panchayat board or municipal council has time to put to a vote a resolution on whether the corpse may be lifted or not, our force must set out at once and quietly attend, within the bounds of law, to what has to be done.

Cholera will come to the town! Now and then Congress ministers will come too! If cholera comes, our force must be ready to prevent it—and if there is no movement order to show black flags to Congress ministers, it must be ready to meet them and explain the condition of the villages.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 30 / printed 27 -->

Some will set out to perform puja to evil deities in order to drive away contagious disease—we must not merely abuse them and sit down! With the help of health officers, our force must go from village to village and protect the people from disease and deficiency.

Through the efforts of movement comrades—and the work of the health department—the disease will disappear. Like the tale in which the crow sits and the palmyra fruit falls, they will say, “We performed puja to Kali and cholera vanished.” You need not fall over laughing—just smile. You need not speak. The man who survived after begging for his life and taking an injection will speak to them himself—after untying and throwing away the yellow thread bound to his hand!

Warriors who work on so many fronts must march through village streets. Only then will free Dravida gain the strength to protect itself.

Kulithalai has taken me—and you—somewhere far away. All right, let us eat. The meal is over. We left for Tiruchi. As usual, it was two o'clock before we fell into bed!

**June 19**

In 1946, friends of our movement were implicated in a major conspiracy case and thrown into prison. Arignar Anna made great efforts to conduct a case to secure their release. In those days we were still the Dravidar Kazhagam. Even though there was no official announcement from the Dravidar Kazhagam calling for support for that case, Anna and a few others made efforts on their own, staged the play *Neethidevan Mayakkam* in Tiruchi—with Anna himself acting in it—and contributed more than five thousand rupees to the case fund. People spoke then of the “Ayya–Anna dispute”; this case was one reason for it. That famous case was what came to be called the Karur riot case.

At that Karur, an “explanatory meeting on Dalmiyapuram” had been arranged for June 19. Our group reached Karur. That day another person joined us. Do not these names ring out at our conferences, public meetings and musical programmes—Isai Murasu Hanifa, Innisai Selvar Chellamuthu? Of the two, Chellamuthu joined the tour from that day onward. Whether at the opening of meetings or during our journeys, he kept a breeze of music blowing. When Chellamuthu is with us on tour there is no lack of flavour—and no lack of briskness either. He attends to matters with great responsibility and also becomes an instrument that plunges us into a sea of delight. At the Karur meeting he poured down a rain of music. Then we spoke. A huge gathering of more than roughly thirty thousand people. Just as Karur immediately brings the riot case to mind, friend Karikalan too comes and stands before us—activist Murugesan appears before our eyes—and the friends of Periyar Unavu Viduthi come forward with affection.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 32 / printed 29 -->

Looking at Karikalan, one cannot help wondering: with such a thin frame—with a body like a splinter—how does he spin like a top and perform rationalist work like this?

Friend Murugesan is almost an example of modesty—selflessness—boundless enthusiasm—all three.

He too was with us in Tiruchi prison. One evening someone came hurriedly to me and said, “Sir, Kamaraj Nadar is coming, sir.” I was surprised. Perhaps he was coming as a jail visitor, I thought. The answer—how could great men like him ever develop such concern?—immediately arose in my mind as well. Still, let us see, I thought, and came out of the room. There was no Kamaraj. Karur Murugesan was coming. Seen suddenly, Murugesan

<!-- Tamil source: scan 33 / printed 30 -->

resembles Kamaraj in appearance! Comrades—remember that word “appearance.” There may even be similarity in age. But a great difference has grown between them—Karur Murugesan has racial consciousness—he is a soldier in the liberation war of the Dravidian nation—he is no lackey of foreign imperialism! So my friend had been teasing me, I realised, and laughed. I believe I have introduced Karur Murugesan to you to some extent. The Karur region must make good use of him—and of blades like Karikalan.

The meeting at Karur ended at ten at night. Then I had to get into the car! I myself do not know how I got from the stage to the car. I could see the car. A crowd surrounded it. All the comrades who had come to escort me were flung far away. With great difficulty I was brought up to the car. Comrades desperate to see the Karunanidhi inside the car grabbed me, pulled me aside, thrust their heads into the car, and shouted, “Long live Karunanidhi!” How were they to know that Karunanidhi was swimming in the sea of people, unable even to reach the car? This condition was not confined to Karur; it was the same in every town! Not for me alone, but for all our frontline fighters!

Enthusiastic comrades themselves should recognise and take up the task of rescuing a speaker from this delightful torment.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 34 / printed 31 -->

At last I was put into the car. With a mighty noise, the car frightened the people and then moved. With no time to eat in Karur—and without meeting anyone—we set off toward Tiruchi.

We had come perhaps half a mile. We got down beside a wall and sat to rest. Saying, “At least let us smoke a cigarette,” Mani put his hand into his shirt pocket. “Sir—the purse is gone, sir,” he said. We joked that some sovereign of this land who had married the goddess Poverty could run a month's life on the thirty rupees that had been in Mani's purse. Comrade Muthu then finally said what he had been too embarrassed to say: one sandal was missing from his foot. Before he had finished saying it, Anbil cried out, “Sir, the pen is gone too, sir!” I could only laugh. He often says, “I am a Kallar; nobody can do anything to me”—not out of caste pride, but as a joke! “A thief has shown a Kallar his handiwork,” we said, and all laughed. There was no time to keep laughing. That night I had to preside over a play at Tirukkattuppalli, about sixty miles from Karur. So we set off straight there. It is worth noting that we still had not eaten. We reached Tirukkattuppalli at one in the morning. I presided over and spoke at the play staged by poet Murugaiya's troupe,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 35 / printed 32 -->

chatted with our old friend, Comrade Pulavar Kuzhandhaiya, swallowed two idlis each at three in the morning, and went straight to the great city of Thanjavur—to sleep!

**Dawn:**

Within a few hours, dawn had come. We too set out. Around ten in the morning we reached Salem. After finishing some work there, we left at two in the afternoon and reached a place called Velayuthampalayam near Karur. A large procession set out from the Pugalur bridge. A young man unable to bear the splendour of that procession stood with a red flag in his hand shouting, “Victory to the Communist Party!” Later he removed—himself—from the scene. The Velayuthampalayam meeting was conducted in the manner of a conference. A comrade who had been in the Socialist Party joined the DMK that day and spoke in support of the policy of Dravidian separation. A college-student comrade who, when I had previously visited that region, had issued a leaflet titled “Welcome, Principle!” also mounted the stage that day and beat the Dravidian drum. The warriors of Velayuthampalayam conducted the meeting with the same discipline observed at conferences. Before I began speaking, the chairman of the meeting conducted a widow remarriage before thousands of people! My speech that day was shaped around that very event.

“Here on this stage we have conducted a marriage for a widow; we have held a ceremony giving life again to a woman who had lost her life. Our Dravida too is a widow! A woman who has lost her life! We have gathered here to devise the methods of action for conducting that remarriage—to give renaissance—to arrange the marriage of the Dravidian widow—to restore life to her who had gone out to live. Our faith is growing that our effort will succeed.

“All those who once cursed us, ‘May you be ruined,’ now bless us: ‘Long live the good younger brothers who guard the nation.’ The man who called me ‘Kolaignar’—killer—at the last meeting calls me ‘Kalaignar’—artist—with tenderness at this meeting. He even sheds tears thinking of what he did before. Those who were in other ranks are now seen in ours. In short, to string a garland for Dravida's remarriage—to pluck flowers for it—all those who had been in the opposing camp are clearing their minds and running toward us.”

I used this as the prelude to my speech, then explained the movement's programmes and the Kallakkudi agitation. Just as every town meeting on the district tour had its own distinction, Velayuthampalayam too had a distinction of its own. Though many activists were responsible for that distinction, the credit for shaping them into lion-like bulls, and the honour of producing a fine blossoming in that region, belong to Comrades Rathinavelu and Moorthy. Rathinavelu was already a bull of the movement while still a college student; bringing together the experience gained in the camp of knowledge and the thoughts born of enthusiasm, he has been carrying on movement work vigorously. Comrade Moorthy deserves the reputation of acting with organisation and method. The Pugalur region must become a fortress of the movement through their work. Just as the great bridge joining the banks of the Kaveri has brought fame to the Pugalur region, I believe—and wish—that Moorthy and Rathinavelu will become lamps of the movement's fame in that area.

After the meeting, comrades of the region surrounded us. Comrade Rathinavelu said he would donate a plot for a movement building and asked me to stage a play to raise money for the building fund. I told him: let the agitation finish. I do intend to stage one.

Our comrades wish to raise movement buildings. But what can we do—we are not relatives of the Birlas! At least in the shape of a hut, we need a place of our own in every town. We must build little, yet live greatly. Anna is said to have spoken at the Mayavaram conference—I was on my deathbed at the time:

“I should hold in my hand the keys to a thousand buildings. I should tour with that bunch of keys. If I go to a town with a branch of the movement, however late the hour, without troubling the secretary or anyone else, I should have that bunch of keys with which I can open our building myself and rest.”

Anna expressed that desire at Mayuram. Desires can be expressed. Must plans for fulfilling them also be handed down? Rathinavels must donate plots. Activists must set out. As I said earlier, a house-building force must arise. A movement mansion must be built. “A mansion?” you ask. Yes, a mansion! To a crow, its chick is golden! A little hut for which we ourselves mix the mud—we ourselves thatch the roof—we ourselves finish the building—is a mansion to us!

Why do we stage plays—why do we tremble lest they run at a loss—why do we look at the sky and sigh, “Please, let it not rain”?

A Rathinavel in every town—to give the land! A Velu for every man—and the mansion will rise! Even the stones thrown at us in elections, at Tiruttani, and at our meetings can be used for one side wall of the movement mansion. The huge boulder lifted at Theni to hurl at Nedunchezhiyan can be used as the threshold of the movement mansion.

Have we not already built a structure that makes the world marvel—with the bones of Thalamuthu and Natarasan—with the sinews of Velayutham and Pandian—with the corpses of Lalgudi Natarasan and Nellikuppam Majeed—with the blood-mud given by those who lost hands and those who lost legs—a great mansion of sacrifice, tall enough to strike the sky, able to proclaim Tamil honour to the earth, engraved with the golden letters “DMK,” sending light in all eight directions, setting the sacred names of Thiagarayar, Nair, Panagal, Selvam and Azhagiri afloat on the breeze and making them resound—an incomparable mansion—a mansion of feeling—a mansion surpassing the glory of Tirumalai Nayakkar Mahal and the Taj Mahal—a ceremonial hall for the sweet Tamil family—a bell-hall that explains our principle.

We who have raised that magnificent hall of sacrifice need only build an ordinary hut in which to sit, converse, read and, when leaders come, sleep. A movement in every town—a place for every movement unit—is absolutely necessary; and, as said before, both strength and method are certainly necessary to build it.

Hmm; all right—set out at once to attend to that work—we must sleep; we are going to Tiruchi. Only if we leave now can we, as usual, get to sleep by two o'clock at least.

They saw us off—we left.

**In Tiruchi:**

July 21 was a day of condemnation throughout Dravida. It was the day the warning cry of those who stood against the Achariyar Government's attempt to destroy the intelligence of the Dravidian people was heard across the land. Saying that the existing education system was no use to anyone, the Premier of Madras—forgive me—the man whom Governor Prakasa had made Premier—brought in a new scheme. It was the story of Perumal becoming Great Perumal: nothing more. It was not a plan that would lead children along a proper path. Three hours of study for children was enough.
