# Ingersoll

<!-- Tamil source: scan 109 / unit marker only -->

Ingersoll—a flame of knowledge, a fountain of energy, a mature atheist—was born on 11 August 1833 in a village called Tristan in the state of New York in America.

Ingersoll’s father worked very hard to raise his son into a great scholar and a distinguished religious man. At night, it is said, he would light a long candle and insist that his son study by that light until the candle had completely burned down and gone out. Following his father’s command, young Ingersoll would routinely keep reading the religious books his father had given him even after the candle had burned out, without worrying about the light.

Yet religious ideas did not become engraved in the mind of the young man who studied in this way; on the contrary, revolutionary rationalist ideas began to take command.

History says that the father, who at first suffered mental anguish over his son Ingersoll, underwent a renewal in his final days and came to see his son’s path as one agreeable to his own mind as well!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 110 / printed 109 -->

Even while still at that age, Ingersoll drew many people to his side through rationalist arguments while continuing his studies. He first took up work as a teacher. Because his progressive thoughts and campaign for knowledge made some people his enemies, he was dismissed from teaching. Without losing heart, he began to study law. He became a skilful lawyer and won praise as one among the few major lawyers worthy of particular mention.

There was a case against a Negro, accusing him of stealing a cow. Hearing that there was no one to argue on behalf of that Negro, Ingersoll himself took up his defence. Arguments and counter-arguments ended. Judgment was delivered. The Negro was acquitted. Ingersoll embraced him and secretly asked:

“Now that you have been acquitted, at least now tell me the truth! You did steal the cow, didn’t you?”

“Yes—I really did steal it! But there is one thing. At the beginning of this case I certainly was guilty. Later, as you heaped up evidence and argued—and the argument went on—even I began to doubt whether I had stolen it.”

Does not that single cow-theft case itself stand as testimony to Ingersoll’s ability!

Not only in court: without caring about the illness that caused pain in his chest, he travelled tirelessly and engaged in rationalist propaganda until he closed his eyes at the age of sixty-six. People would surge in crowds, paying a fee to hear his speeches. He was strict about receiving a sum for speaking. Yet he also believed that if his ideas spread among the people, the size of the sum did not matter; there are some incidents in his public life that stand as fitting evidence of how he acted on that belief.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 111 / printed 110 -->

For example, some young people devoted to the rationalist movement from a town called Klarimont invited him to a meeting. He said he would come if they paid three hundred dollars. They agreed and brought him. Because many people in the town were afraid to come to that meeting, only twenty-eight dollars was collected as admission fees. Without telling Ingersoll this, the young men paid him the agreed three hundred dollars. Ingersoll questioned them closely and repeatedly until he learned the true amount collected. At once he called the young men near and said: “Thank you for your affection—praise for your enthusiasm—congratulations for your honest conduct. Here, of the 300 dollars you gave me, keep 272 dollars yourselves. The 28 dollars paid by those who came to hear my speech is enough for me.” And with that he took leave and went away.

“Good heavens! What tremendous humaneness
this man possesses—not a speaker who talks merely to earn money,
but one who labours to spread a principle; that is why
he understands the trouble
we took to arrange this meeting for him!”

Would not those young people have garlanded Ingersoll with such praise?
