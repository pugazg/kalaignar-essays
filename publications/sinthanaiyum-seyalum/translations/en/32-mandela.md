# Mandela

<!-- Tamil source: scan 139 / unit marker only -->

On the morning of 11 February 1990, while a Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam conference was under way in Tiruchi, I came bounding with overflowing joy and stood before the microphone on the stage. Looking at the Tamil people gathered there like a surging sea, I said:

“Great people of Tamil!

Kazhaga Udanpirappugalē!

Here is news bearing boundless joy:

Nelson Mandela, leader of the Black people
of South Africa, who had been imprisoned
for twenty-seven years,
has been released—
to celebrate this joy,
let everyone gathered in this conference pavilion
rise to your feet and keep applauding.”

Hearing my appeal with this meaning, those immersed in joy at Mandela's release made, in landlocked Tiruchi, the roar of sea waves until I signalled with my hand, “Enough, enough.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 140 / printed 139 -->

At that time the whole world was speaking about, and shedding tears over, the imprisonment of that one man. South Africa is the soil on which our Annal Gandhi raised his voice for the rights of the Indian people and introduced the method of righteous struggle! History records that in the struggle led by Annal, the young men Nagappan and Narayanasamy, belonging to the Tamil land of India, and the woman Thillaiyadi Valliammai went to prison, their health declined, and they gave their lives. Rare memorials created by the hand of the Kazhagam government so that that history may forever be remembered still stand as imperishable monuments. In South Africa too, on 7 October 1997, Mr. Gujral, who had served as our Indian Prime Minister and as the leader of our alliance, went to the memorial of those martyrs and paid tribute by laying flowers. That was an event filled with emotion.

Nelson Mandela spent twenty-seven years in prison in that South Africa for opposing white domination and fighting for the liberation of the Black people. There is no need here to explain his later assumption of office as President after his release, the support his wives gave his sacrificial struggle, or the ups and downs narrated in his life story. We marvel instead that the diamond-like heart of that great warrior, the surroundings in which he grew, the repression he accepted, the sense of self-respect he guarded, and his public life without slipping—all these were qualities he had already acquired in youth. We have a duty to bless every young person who grows along such a path.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 141 / printed 140 -->

18 July 1918 was Mandela's date of birth. Among the Black people, Nelson Mandela's family belonged to the Xhosa ethnic group. Mandela's father was the head of that clan. His name was Henry Kaaniyis va Gadla. He had four wives and twelve children. Nelson Mandela was the son of the third wife.

He was born in a hut and grew up in a hut. The family had only three little huts in all—one for cooking, one for storing belongings, and one for sleeping! Mats were the beds for all of them!

In his early years Mandela's work was grazing goats and cattle. His mother did not know how to read or write, yet she sent her son to school.

By 1928 Mandela's father had died, and a relative of his father named “Jongintaba” took responsibility for Mandela's education.

In Mandela's birthplace, called Kayunu, during his spare time he would listen to elders recount old stories about their people, tell how the history of their race had been obscured by whites, and describe the arts, culture and ways of life that had existed there before white entry. Mandela drew new energy from listening to these things. He did not give up his studies; nor did he stop grazing goats and cattle.

At the same time, even at that young age Mandela formed a firm resolve that the sorrows of his people must be relieved and their rights established.

In 1938, through the efforts of his relative Jongintaba, Mandela was admitted first to “Held Town” College and later to “Fort Hare” College.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 142 / printed 141 -->

It was then that the first shoot of his struggle began to emerge. When a strike was declared under young Mandela's leadership against the cruelty of racial discrimination displayed there, the college administration expelled him. Yes—that was the first punishment Mandela received in the struggle for rights he conducted!

He was told that he could return to college if he apologised. Mandela refused, saying it was a question of honour.

Later, together with his cousin Justice, he went to Johannesburg and worked in a gold mine. After he was driven out of there too at the urging of a relative, Mandela set out to study law. He trained in law under a white lawyer called “Lasa Sidingski” and became accomplished.

Biographical accounts say that Mandela's life was disciplined; that he habitually rose early; that without fail he undertook walking exercise every day; that he ate simple food and always worked energetically; and that he would even take part in kitchen work to help women.

Although there was bitter smoke and conflict between the Black people and whites, Mandela dealt with students on both sides with breadth of mind, civility and affection. He wished to conduct a peaceful righteous struggle in the Gandhian way and remove the cruelty of racial discrimination!

In 1948, at the stage when Mandela was elected general secretary of the youth wing, the restrictions imposed on Black people—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 143 / printed 142 -->

pricked his eyes. He could not bear that Black people were called by the degrading word “Kafir,” or that they were prevented from moving about in the streets. Was that all? Whites seized the lands of Black people. Love and mixed marriages between whites and Blacks became unlawful. Racial hostility became a great hostility. Jobs were denied to Black people. They were not permitted into places of entertainment. If they violated these rules, severe punishment followed.

Opposing these cruelties, Mandela, who had become secretary of the youth wing in 1948, secured a resolution at a provincial conference held in 1949 to conduct a non-cooperation movement against white rule.

Following that, in 1951 he was elected president of the youth wing.

Mandela's righteous struggle, which had sprouted peacefully against white fanaticism, did not fail to bloom into a flower of revolution.

The student struggle of the Black people also began.

As a result of revolutions and uprisings, the white South African government told the imprisoned Mandela that he would be released if he agreed to leave the country.

Mandela refused and said: “If we do not have the right to freedom, the prison in which I am confined is all that can be returned to me.” As a continuation of the struggles he waged, he had to accept a long life in prison.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 144 / printed 143 -->

In the early period in prison there was degrading food, not even enough to satisfy hunger; a mat on a stone floor for sleeping; punishment under prison rules if newspapers were kept; work throughout the day; and, at the end of every day's work, clothes being removed for inspection. Mandela endured all these cruelties. As an echo of the resolve he had carried from youth, his words about prison cruelty were as follows:

“The cruelty of prison has not
made me suffer.
It is when I think of the pain and sorrow
my people endure outside the prison
that I find myself unable
to bear the anguish.”

Mandela's life of sacrifice is not a song of the heart only for the youth of South Africa; it is a song of the heart for countless young people across this globe who yearn to see a new world.
