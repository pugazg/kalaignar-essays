# To Clear a Greater Height

Udanpirappē,

A young man named Jayakumar came to Anna Arivalayam and met me. He handed me a sheet of paper and asked me to read what he had written.

Though his sentences were formed in such a way that a few errors poked their heads out here and there, I could see that the young man had an eagerness to express some idea.

“Leader, you toil without rest—  
you keep on toiling—  
you toil without sleeping—  
I too toil—  
but not without sleeping.  
I sleep.  
Only if we sleep,  
according to the advice  
of our President Abdul Kalam,  
can young people dream.”

The Professor, who was beside me, read it, smiled in appreciation and handed the sheet back to me.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 25 / printed 24 -->

Until I reached home from Anna Arivalayam, my attention in the car remained fixed on that sheet. Like a face appearing in a film scene, the young man’s face too seemed to flicker across the page.

Wonder! Wonder! And the wonder kept expanding! Ah! Not only is he sleeping in order to follow the President’s advice; is he not also, indirectly, preaching to me that I should sleep?

When I work without sleeping, is it not mostly while I am writing—or travelling to meet people—and doing work of that kind?

I have seen Anna sitting and writing while the town and the world were asleep. I grew accustomed to doing the same. Until dawn peeps in and snatches the pen away, saying, “Put the pen down,” the peace and enthusiasm I find in writing are unlike anything at any other time. Apart from the soft sound of the gentle breeze gliding in, there is no other noise: do you know what a tremendous encouragement that natural silence is for writing?

I learnt that too through Anna—but I also learnt another way: amid bustle and commotion, the great roar of vehicles on the road, people moving about in the printing press and crowds of visitors, Ayya Periyar would sit unmoving and pile up page after page of writing. I learnt to work that way too, watching it from close by, day and night, at the `Kudiyarasu` office in Erode!

What do these two very different experiences make clear? That one must be ready not only for a journey along a park path, but also for a journey through a bush-choked forest where storms rage!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 26 / printed 25 -->

Honourable Abdul Kalam said, “Young people must dream,” and this little brother has taken those words absolutely literally!

If someone says, “You scolded him; take it back,” it does not mean that the words must be picked up by hand, placed in the hands of the person who was scolded, and then physically received back by the one who spoke them. A verbal statement that the words are being withdrawn is enough for all those acts to be treated as accomplished.

When we say, “Awake! Rise! Become aware!” no one imagines that these are merely words for tapping a sleeping person and waking him up; they are understood as a command to instil awareness and courage in society and to rise roaring in order to break the bonds of slavery.

In the same way, Abdul Kalam did not say:

“Young people! I shall sing you a lullaby; sleep! Then, while you sleep listening to my lullaby, dream about the future!”

He did not say that.

To say “dream” means: think—think, and act!

The dreams dreamed by young people will differ—and that is how they should be. Only then will the benefits of those dreams fully answer the many different needs of this nation.

Young people may dream of becoming intellectual giants—  
brilliant engineers—  
doctors who build a healthy society—  
makers of history—  
explorers of the boundless sky and the inexhaustible sea—  
people of equality without divisions of caste or religion—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 27 / printed 26 -->

poets—  
painters—  
sculptors who carve figures in stone—  
politicians concerned with constructive work—  
scholars—  
patrons—  
and may dream of shining in such roles.

My first dream—a dream I did not wish to dream in my youth—was to study through college. It ended up as a shattered dream. But the advancement of my language, my people, my country and its people became my dream that never shattered. Those who dream in this way can only receive society’s “stamp of acceptance” as exceptions.

The dream every young person should dream is this: let us learn well, gain clarity and mastery, and in the field to which we are committed, stand as central figures whom the nation honours. There is no need to sleep in order to dream that dream. On the contrary: wake up to dream it! Only when we awaken will dreams come true. It is easy to dream of anything; it is difficult for that dream to come true without hard work!

One of the sayings  
I like very much is:

“Only by running a long distance  
can one clear a greater height.”

Affectionately,

M.K.

29-4-2005
