# Aruthakutti Nadar Who Sang an Anthathi

<!-- Tamil source: scan 214 / unit marker only -->

Yesterday’s written portrait under “Thought and Action” was, as usual, also fashioned as a portrait of ideas. Using Nattarayya’s commentary on a `Nalvazhi` poem, and taking the dictionary as evidence, I explained how Tamil words can take different meanings according to the place in which they occur.

Today’s “Thought and Action” concerns the `Chidambara Anthathi Kathaip Paadal` composed by the poet Aruthakutti Nadar.

First, thanks are due to Mr. T. Rajarathinam, who laboured successfully to add this rare story-song collection, `Chidambara Anthathi`, composed by Aruthakutti Nadar, to the literary form called `anthathi`, one among the genres of minor literature, and published it on behalf of the Chennai `Naam Tamilar` publishing house.

Professor V. I. Subramaniam, former Vice-Chancellor of Thanjavur Tamil University and former head of the Tamil Department at Kerala University, writes in his congratulatory note to the book as follows:

<!-- Tamil source: scan 215 / printed 214 -->

“Through its metre and its combinations of simple words, this anthathi has been composed in a form that attracts the mind. Since members of the Nadar community live in large numbers in Kanyakumari, Tirunelveli and Thoothukudi districts, words in local usage in those regions appear in it. Because literacy was low among the Nadar community in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the poet created this work so that these religious messages and the divine deeds performed by the deity might produce religious attachment among that community.”

Having said this, Mr. V. I. Subramaniam also expresses his heartfelt wish:

“I wish that the author-editor, T. Rajarathinam, will produce many more works with an innovative outlook.”

Under the subheading `History of Kavirayar Aruthakutti Nadar` in that book, the following details are given:

“Four hundred years ago in the southern country, spirit worship in the Nellai and Kumari districts was uncultivated. There was no proper history for the village deities. People who worshipped village deities beat a single drum, offered goats, kids and chickens, worshipped village deities such as Madan, and conducted festivals.

“The man who changed this condition, created a new awakening by narrating the histories of village deities through `villisai` in temples, was Aruthan Kutti Nadar. He lived at Thangaiyur near Udangudi in Nellai district—now Thoothukudi district.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 216 / printed 215 -->

“He selected stories for villisai and wrote songs.

“He also wrote many villisai songs for the deities of villages. He taught all these to his disciple, a poet called Chintamani Pulavar, and formed a musical group using instruments such as the bow, pot, `udukku`, `jalra` and `kattai`. For the first time, on Friday, the fourth day of the month of Masi in Kollam year 798 (A.D. 1623), he staged a villisai performance at the temple of his clan deity Shenbaga Nachchiyar in Thangaiyur.”

Among the many pieces of information in the research introduction, one of the most important explains how much effort was undertaken to publish `Chidambara Anthathi`, how many manuscripts were found, and from whom they were obtained:

“When I selected `Chidambara Anthathi` for publication and searched for manuscripts, my friend and researcher S. Thamaraipandian took me to his elder paternal uncle, the villisai artist Mr. A. Appavu Nadar of Sivasubramaniapuram. He had a handwritten copy. I stayed there and made a copy of it. Searching further, I went to Udayar Pulavar. He had a paper copy. Then I went to the villisai poet Mr. Durairaj Pulavar of Vijayaramapuram, Sathankulam. He had a palm-leaf manuscript. I copied it while keeping it in his house. These three manuscripts belonged to the same lineage. I went to Nalvazhi A. Karuppasamy. There was a handwritten copy there. It was an incomplete manuscript. He said that he had given the complete palm-leaf manuscript to a friend of his.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 217 / printed 216 -->

“He gave many pieces of information. He said that Aruthakutti Adigal was his ancestor, and that Shenbaga Nachchiyar Amman, the clan deity of Aruthakutti Adigal, was also their clan deity. He possesses several manuscripts said to have been composed by Aruthakutti Adigal.”

Should we not praise those who gave us this `Chidambara Anthathi` story-song book after contributing so much labour without the slightest weariness? Though the story and the songs that explain it portray their own period, even in that setting they reveal the ability of kavirayars such as Aruthakutti Nadar.

What is an `anthathi`?

It is formed from the two words `antham` and `aathi`.

A composition in which the last line of one poem becomes the first line of the next is called an `anthathi`. `Antham` means “end” or “boundary”; `aathi` means “first” or “beginning.” The expression `anthathi` is formed through this joining.

Here is an example, from this very `Chidambara Anthathi`:

“As Chidambaranathar, praised across the earth,
was to come in procession,
they raised the sacred festival flag and garland
on the Ani Uthiram day.”

The next poem continues from it—that is, the beginning follows the ending, the `aathi` follows the `antham`:

“On that Ani Uthiram day,
Chidambara Ananda Thandavanar—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 218 / printed 217 -->

with sacred white ash shining over his whole body,
wore the fierce tiger skin.”

Thus the section `The Lord Comes in Procession` continues through many poems. In it:

“Chidambara Thandavanar crossed
the street of Thillai Govindar,
and graciously rose to enter
the monastery of good Muthaliyar.”

At that very time, it seems, an exchange of taunts begins between a mother and daughter! Why? The daughter, seeing the form of Chidambara Thandavan in procession, is captivated, overwhelmed with admiration, nearly driven mad, and tries to run into the procession. The mother restrains her:

“My daughter, do not go into the street-junction
where everyone can see you,” she said.

The mother tells her daughter further:

“Can women properly desire
an ascetic, my daughter?”

The daughter replies to that mother:

“O Mother! If you saw that Thillai Chidambara
ascetic whom you say one must not desire,
if you saw his beauty,
is there anyone whose clothes would not loosen
and who would not be seized by longing?”

Hearing the daughter’s answer, the mother grows angry. She says:

“To test Siruthondan’s devotion,
did he not tell him to cut up Seerala Devan

<!-- Tamil source: scan 219 / printed 218 -->

and cook him as meat?
Is he not a man of injustice?”

The daughter answers and manages her mother:

“It was not injustice, Mother—listen!
To that very Seeralan
he granted the state of liberation,
making him one among the sages.”

The daughter, entranced, praises Mahesan’s dance of bliss. The mother, refusing even that, sheds tears:

“Seeing that dance of bliss and beauty,
you foolish girl, have desired him a little;
you have made yourself a target
for all who mock and speak disgrace.”

The daughter tells her mother firmly that her frenzy of desire will not subside:

“Even if I become a target for all who mock,
today I shall take up the `madal`; if I do not
join the Lord of Thillai, why did these breasts
rise and change at all?”

Hearing this, the mother speaks like a raging tigress:

“Becoming one form, Nedumal and he,
then becoming two forms,
in the Daruka forest he went
to rape its women—”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 220 / printed 219 -->

At once the daughter explains to her mother:

“When the women there, overcome by desire,
fell for him in the Daruka forest,
the greatness our Chidambaran displayed
cannot be exhausted by thought.”

In this way `Chidambara Anthathi`, while gesturing towards the flavours of sensual pleasure, also insists upon the path by which supreme bliss is attained.

“All the sages of that earth,
as though they were women, worshipped;
in Indra’s world a rain of flowers fell,
and the celestial ones bore them aloft;
Chidambara Thandavar sat in splendour,
his mind rejoicing;
Indra, the gods and the immortals all
sat there in delight.”

Thus the story-song ends in devotional ecstasy. Just as learned people praise Kambar when speaking today of the poetic beauty of Kambar, should we not also praise the kavirayar Aruthakutti Nadar, who sang `Chidambara Anthathi` with a learning worthy of such appreciation?
