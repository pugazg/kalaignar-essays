# The Small-Screen “Selvi”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 126 / unit marker only -->

(1) “A woman is one who guards herself, cares for the man who has taken her as his own, guards words worthy of honour, and does not falter.”

(2) “What can the protection of confinement do? The foremost protection is the fullness that women guard for themselves.”

(3) “She worships no god; she rises worshipping her husband. If she says ‘Rain,’ the rain will fall.”

(4) “If women obtain what is worthy to be obtained, they will gain the great distinction of the world in which the celestial beings live.”

In the chapter called “The Excellence of a Life-Partner,” Ayya Valluvar gives the above four Kurals. I draw today's picture under “Thought and Action” as one who wrote the following explanations for them respectively.

First let us see the explanations to the four Kurals.

(1) A woman is one who, in the path of chastity, protects herself and her husband and remains unwavering in protecting the good name that brings honour to them.

(2) To think of treating as slaves women who protect themselves and live with excellent character is ignorance.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 127 / printed 126 -->

(3) A wife who regards her husband's word as the word of God and worships him will be one who thinks herself a slave, like rain that falls the moment it is commanded, “Fall.”

(4) If a woman obtains a man of good character as her husband, the new world called domestic life will become one of great distinction for her.

There has been an accusation that Valluvar fashioned women as slaves through the Kural. It was precisely as an explanatory answer to that accusation that this commentary was written, and the book `Thirukkural — Kalaignar Commentary` was released at a function in the presence of the great Tamil scholar A. Sa. Gnanasambandanar, along with his words of appreciation.

Therefore, love may melt and flow between a man and a woman—friendship may blossom—the fragrance of married life may spread—but there is no place there, and there must be no place there, for the patriarchal urge that says “woman is man's slave.” Because I think so, and because from my youth I entrusted myself to a movement that thinks so, I am qualified to write this.

The concern and doubt I carry must be placed before women for them to think about. Women who speak and write with utter hatred of patriarchy—why do they become turtles retreating into their shells and submit to their husbands? Why? How? For what reason?

There is a scene on the small screen—a family portrait called `Selvi` by Kalaiyarasi Radhika. In it, Selvi seeks to forgive a woman who had tried to destroy her and to free her from punishment. Selvi's husband rebukes her and tries to stop her. Between husband and wife—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 128 / printed 127 -->

the heat of duty rises. When the husband threatens his wife as though even their family relationship might break apart, the shocked Selvi stands still, regains her composure, measures her words and gives an answer carrying this meaning: “No one can stop me from acting compassionately according to my conscience.” I was amazed to see how a woman seeking the path of righteousness issues a challenge and douses the fiery words that patriarchy had hastily scooped up and flung at her.

Not only in drama, epic and screen portrait, but in the country, in the home and in every family, the rights of both sexes must be established within the pure relationship between man and woman.

What is strange is that women themselves stand in the way of women's rights receiving the opportunities they need—women themselves show hesitation!

Apart from bodily structure, what powerful argument is there that can make us forget the truth that man and woman are equal?

Among animals, the male lion is lazy—it will not even rise to seek prey; its pride is merely its mane!

It is the lioness that is strong enough to hunt animals for food! She has no mane!

Among birds, it is the male peacock that has the beautiful train.

The peahen has no such train; nor does she have beauty of appearance!

Here is the joke: we describe a woman as a “peahen”; in the same way we welcome and praise a man as—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 129 / printed 128 -->

“O male lion!” What a contradiction lies in both comparisons!

There may be differences of form there,

but rights are equal—in the animal kind! In the bird kind!

But

in the human kind, there are differences of organs,

and there is also the distress of unequal rights!

That is why

women raise a loud voice
for their rights—

not they alone; for them, we too,
who come in Anna's path,
raise our voice in the Assembly!

*

Should it come to this—that animals and birds are above us?

Should women's hearts burn and ache in vain?
