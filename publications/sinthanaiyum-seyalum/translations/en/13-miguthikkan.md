# When the Limit Is Crossed...

<!-- Tamil source: scan 62 / unit marker only -->

Talking freely—
sharing intimacies—
exchanging affection—
sharing it—
taking part in joy and in pain and sorrow,
giving comfort,
receiving comfort—
engaging in study and in play—

these, and things like them, take place on the basis of the acts called “forming a friendship” and “being friends”.

Ayya Valluvar, who speaks so gracefully of friendship—saying that nothing is as rare as friendship, that it is not easy to find anything as suited to protection as friendship, that it gives pleasure the more one associates with another, and that it can arise from a shared feeling of mind—

also says that friendship is not for friends merely to laugh and be happy together; if one of them strays from the good path and proceeds along a wrong one, friendship must be useful for rebuking and correcting that person. Many old commentaries explain that this is the foremost grammar of friendship.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 63 / printed 62 -->

“Friendship is not made for laughter; when one goes too far,
it is to step forward and rebuke.”

When the `miguthikkan` spoken of in this Kural is understood as “straying from the good path”, may it not also be understood to mean that even the laughing, rejoicing and familiarity called “laughter” must not cross the limit?

One friend of mine was a good man—deeply affectionate too—a delightful friend. Likewise, I had a doctor friend who had known me from my youth and lived in another town. The friend I mentioned first and I had gone to that town. The doctor friend invited us to his house. We went. For a long time we laughed happily and talked about many things. Some prominent people who had come to the doctor for treatment sat with us, described their problems, underwent physical examination, obtained prescriptions and left. While the doctor was simultaneously attending to the illnesses of those who came and conversing with us, the delightful friend I mentioned slowly rose and went toward the doctor’s table. He picked up the doctor’s “instrument for detecting the heartbeat”—the stethoscope—from the table. My friend did not stop with merely picking it up.

In front of all those who had come and were sitting there for treatment, he placed that instrument on the doctor friend’s chest, put its earpieces to his ears, held the pulse at the wrist and began examining him.

I do not know how the doctor friend became so angry; saying, “Hey! Behave with some civility!” he snatched the instrument away and flung it sharply onto the table.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 64 / printed 63 -->

The prominent people who had come for treatment laughed, yet the incident left them speechless.

Then, somewhat angrily, I asked my delightful friend, “Why, sir? Should there not be a limit in everything? However close a friend he may be, can you behave like this? Is it proper to interfere in another person’s profession in this way?” At once he took the doctor’s hand, expressed regret, asked forgiveness and left with me.

That led me to think that Ayya Valluvar may have used `miguthikkan` specifically not only to say that friendship is not merely for laughter, but also to say that when even that crosses the boundary it must be rebuked and corrected.

Friends laughing, rejoicing, joking and playing too must remain within measure; if one person crosses the limit, the other must correct and restrain him. That is the mark of true friendship.

Some friends, thinking that they are behaving with the rights of intimacy, innocently do something or other and become the objects of needless dislike. As an example of that too, I must once again mention this same man who was my delightful friend.

There is even now in Chennai a film-production studio that has a special place in the hearts of the people of Tamil Nadu. Films for which I wrote story and dialogue, and films for which I wrote songs, were produced there. In those days I used to go there and enjoy conversations with film artists. That studio also had a music hall. A well-known music composer managed that hall. One day I went to that music hall with my delightful friend.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 65 / printed 64 -->

While the composer, the film artists and I were happily discussing many matters, the composer placed his fingers on the harmonium keys to set a tune for a song. Within a few minutes my dear friend sat beside him, pulled the “harmonium box” toward himself and, with great eagerness, began asking, “What `kattai`—what key—is mine?” It is generally an act capable of provoking anger in musicians. Very many among them regard music as “divine” and treat it with reverence. In that setting, with many artists seated in the hall, I could see from their faces that everyone had been pained by his pulling the harmonium toward himself and playfully asking, “What `kattai` is mine?” So, in the same playful spirit, I said, “For you—what `kattai`? Your wits are the block!” Everyone laughed at my reply and let my friend’s act pass without making much of it.

Had he stopped with merely asking, “What `kattai` is mine?” there would have been nothing to it. But pulling the harmonium away from the composer and then asking it—did that not become an example of the unwanted act called `miguthikkan`? Therefore, “crossing the limit is an unwanted act.”
