# A Thing Called Medicine!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 130 / unit marker only -->

Recently, on 3-9-2005, Honourable Indian Prime Minister Manmohan Singh inaugurated the National Siddha Medical Research Institute at Tambaram near Chennai. During my period in government, fifteen acres of land had been provided so that the institute could be established there, and I had laid its foundation stone at that function. For that reason, wishing to know what view the Indian Prime Minister held about Siddha medicine, I read closely the speech he delivered at the present inauguration.

At a time when people's interest in methods described as traditional medicine, such as Siddha medicine, is declining and a climate is arising in which confidence in them is difficult to establish, I may say that the Prime Minister's speech gave me considerable comfort.

A headache—and immediately a tablet. A feeling that fever may be coming—and at once call the doctor for an injection. In this way, traditional medicine is being left standing in the middle of the road while interest in “allopathy” alone continues to grow day by day among our people!

When we see that in many countries of the world, including India, population has increased threefold in about thirty years, we can understand that problems of pollution—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 131 / printed 130 -->

control and inadequacies in methods of disease prevention have naturally arisen.

The condition has changed from population increasing merely by thousands or lakhs; when the number of people grows by crores, illnesses too multiply. To prevent and cure those illnesses, there are many kinds of treatment and medicines.

In the developing scientific world, surgery and allopathic methods are, for the most part, welcomed for curing disease. Yet when doctors confidently say that at times suitable remedies can also be found against illnesses through leaves and herbs used in methods such as Siddha medicine, patients do not simply believe them one hundred per cent.

Giving the patient confidence first is regarded as an important medicine. Whatever traditional medicine may be, it is very important that the standard of the way in which it is administered should be modernised according to the times. A patient may wrinkle the face on seeing freshly plucked leaves and herbal foliage given just as they are; yet when those same leaves or herbs are processed and given as a tablet or placed in a tiny tube, can we not see the patient accept and use them without pulling a face!

There are circumstances in which the need for some method that cannot simply discard either of the two—an allopathic method or a traditional medical method—becomes unavoidable.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 132 / printed 131 -->

Let us suppose that the allopathic method is the “lift” that carries one to the fifth floor of a hospital. Those who cannot walk because of pain in the legs may use it.

After passing the required floors and reaching the long corridor on the fifth floor, if the leg still hurts, do we not use a wheelchair to cross that long corridor? The other medical systems that are not allopathy—Siddha and the like—are like that wheelchair!

In his speech at the inauguration of the National Siddha Medical Research Institute, the Indian Prime Minister compared the allopathic and Siddha systems and praised both. That is something practitioners of both systems can accept.

An important part of the Prime Minister's speech was:

“Allopathic medicines generally cure diseases. Our traditional medicines such as Siddha not only cure diseases; they also help prevent diseases from arising. Modern methods for preparing them must be further improved.”

In the “allopathic” system alone, at present, surgery and investigative instruments for diagnosing diseases in detail are used. In non-allopathic traditional medical methods, everyday illnesses that arise can be treated simply without causing side effects. We also know from our own experience that fruits, vegetables and greens can serve like medicines—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 133 / printed 132 -->

for protecting ourselves so that such illnesses do not arise at all.

I still clearly remember a traditional physician from a village called Karugudi near my own village of Thirukkuvalai telling me when I was young that if the nine grains—paddy, wheat, green gram, pigeon pea, chickpea, field bean, black gram, sesame and horse gram—are cleaned well, ground into flour and made into powder, and if each day two teaspoons of it are taken, an equal quantity of sugar added, mixed with milk, boiled and drunk morning and evening, all the nutrients needed for bodily health will be obtained; hunger will arise well; digestive power will increase; strength to resist disease will develop; and the body will gain good strength.

“Medicine is not needed for the body
if, after what was eaten has passed,
one eats with due care.”

Thus Ayya Valluvar—the humane physician greater than all physicians even in that age—says in the Thirukkural that “if, after giving the food already consumed the time needed for digestion, one eats again, that person will need no thing called medicine.” It is something to observe closely and follow.
