# Strength and Radiance!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 66 / unit marker only -->

On the wall, board or screen-cloth directly opposite the tip of the nose there should be a circle of light. It may appear as a full moon in the midst of darkness, or as the rising sun. One may sit before it either on the floor with the legs folded or on a chair with the legs hanging down. What matters most in the practice is that the gaze be directed steadily in one direction, without wavering or moving, and that one keep looking at that circle of light without taking one’s eyes off it. This can also be done silently, with the mouth closed.

The instructor Mr. T.K.V. Desikachar, who says that this practice helps steady the mind and is a form of meditation, remarked that to make the meditation still more refined it would be good to open the mouth and utter “Narayana Namaha!” I asked whether, instead of the state of worshipping the “Sun” by saying “Namaha” after “Narayana”, which begins with a long letter, I could reshape it as “Nyayiru Potruthum”—“Let us praise the Sun”—beginning with the long letter `ஞ`, and obtain the same result. The instructor accepted that too. Therefore, every day in his presence, I follow the meditation practice in both ways: first silently, and then by loudly uttering the `Silappathikaram` line, “Nyayiru Potruthum! Nyayiru Potruthum!”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 67 / printed 66 -->

Some years ago, Tamil scholar T.P. Meenakshisundaranar contacted me through Professor Anbazhaganar and spoke to me especially about this meditation practice. He stressed that if, every day for at least five minutes, one closed one’s eyes without allowing thought to scatter and meditated with just one thought held in the heart—a thought of God, or otherwise of Periyar or Anna, but in any case one single thought—it would greatly help bring peace of mind.

I did not follow that advice or undertake the practice then. I had an excuse as though I had no time—but now, without missing a day, along with walking and yoga practice I undertake meditation too, with the hymn “Nyayiru Potruthum”.

At times, when speaking in a rush, the tongue stumbles, does it not? Even to overcome that, the hard consonant `ற` and its sound are useful. Yes; if the sound of `ற` is produced by curling the tip of the tongue inward, a forceful and precise `றற` sound emerges. If this is practised many times—beginning with one utterance each time and gradually increasing to five—the stumbling of the tongue will slowly diminish; that is the experience of many people.

More important than all these practices is the practice of “rising at dawn”. We can see expressions such as “He sleeps until one-thirty in the morning” and “For him the day dawns only at ten o’clock” becoming sentences of modern civilisation. On some particular day, if one has had no sleep the previous night and therefore sleeps exhausted the next day, there is nothing wrong in that; it is understandable. But if that itself—

becomes the daily habit, and such people can see the “rising sun” only as one among the candidate symbols placed at the polling booth, then that is certainly not suited to bodily health.

Therefore, reducing body weight chiefly through walking; making the lungs function properly through breathing exercises; regulating blood circulation and thereby creating protection for the heart; strengthening bones and nerves through yoga; finding peace of mind through meditation—realising that things such as these are indispensable to life, the purpose of this article is that we, who read books about so many fields, should also acquire an interest in reading books that explain the field of health.

A society made up of people free from disease—people with strength—can itself become a protective fortress for the nation. If this article serves at least as an effort that takes a step toward that, I will have gained the benefit of writing it. There are many good practices not mentioned in this article that add strength and radiance to life. Let this be an encouragement to seek them out and make use of them too.
