# The Way of Democracy

<!-- Tamil source: scan 36 / unit marker only -->

Today, throughout India, the method followed in general elections and by-elections is for voters to press buttons on electronic machines and cast their votes for the symbol printed before the name of the party or individual candidate they prefer. In the present day, elections are held on the general basis that everyone above a specified age has the right to vote. Yet we cannot forget that, some years ago, the franchise was restricted: at one time only the educated could vote, and at another only graduates could vote. In the period still spoken of today in connection with the Uthiramerur charter, qualifications of conduct and fitness were prescribed for those who would serve as village leaders and members of village governing committees; in the public hall, villagers wrote names on palm leaves, placed them inside a pot, drew them out, counted them and announced the names of those selected. This was the “kudavolai” method.

Whatever the method, the accepted electoral principle was the opinion of the majority. The differences lay only in the form through which that opinion was obtained; the underlying basis of learning the majority view did not change.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 37 / printed 36 -->

The representation given to those who could vote in the electoral rolls—and likewise the list of those considered qualified to receive those votes—was once divided according to whether people owned land or did not, were educated or unlettered, were graduates or not. Only the names of those granted the franchise under such distinctions found a place there.

That is to say, one may say that until 1952 India continued under a system in which only certain people could be chosen as representatives for the great majority of citizens.

Only after India was declared a republic in 1950 were rules framed so that all people could choose their representatives on an equal footing—and, especially as a result of the voice raised by Annal Ambedkar, so that oppressed people would have a separate-constituency mode of election.

From remote antiquity until now, people have felt that they need a leader to guide them, speak for them and protect them. When that leader was a king, monarchy existed under his leadership. When that history came to an end, a system of republican rule in which the people themselves choose the government emerged. In India, this finally became a lasting change.

When we see that the principles laid down for monarchy in the time of Valluvar or in the Sangam age are, for the most part, applicable even to today’s republican rule, we can understand how extensively the approaches and ethical norms that should govern the relationship between rulers and the ruled were discussed.

So, from whatever angle we look at it, there are two sides: those who wish to rule and those who permit them to do so. In today’s terms,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 38 / printed 37 -->

the two are the candidate and the voter who has the authority to decide whether to support that candidate’s desire or not. It is in the hands of these two groups that the philosophy of people’s rule called “democracy” must be protected.

If we think about whether our esteemed voters act with that understanding—or whether they are even allowed to act with it—we can see that, little by little, some poisonous drug is being injected by a needle into the arteries and nerves of democracy: through the power of authority, the might of wealth, the frenzy of violence and the force of corruption. The philosophy of people’s rule, and the people who place their faith in it, are being deceived! We can understand it and ache inwardly—but are unable to do anything else!

People’s rule is for whom? For the people!

But if the right to constitute that rule is being snatched from those very people, cunningly or brazenly, should not the people prepare themselves for a great revolution against those who would seize it, so that it cannot be taken away?

If the philosophy of people’s rule should, by mischance, fall into the hands of religious fanatics crazed by sectarian hatred or into the hands of those possessed by the frenzy of dictatorship, then every citizen must understand this truth: “Democracy has no freedom” until it is liberated from those cruel people. Only then can democracy remain alive! Otherwise we shall have to stand and watch while its body is left to rot.

7-5-2005
