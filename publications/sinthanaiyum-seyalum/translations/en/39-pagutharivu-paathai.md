# The Path of Rationalism!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 167 / unit marker only -->

I shall first recall the ideas I presented under the title “Science That Had Grown Even Then,” as the ninety-third flower in my `Tolkappiya Poonga`, of which twelve editions have appeared, and then share my thoughts for today’s “Thought and Action.”

When we take pride in saying that the `Tolkappiyam` is a treasury reminding us that it is a chest of ancient Tamil history from two thousand years ago—with many different natural characteristics and ancient ways of life set out particularly and distinctly, a fundamental structure that deserves admiration and following even today, and also a few positions that cannot accord with the customs of the modern new world—our astonishment rises still higher when we see, as the summit of all this, the following sutra in `Marabiyal`:

“To know by one is to know by touch;
to know by two is that, with the tongue;
to know by three is those, with the nose;
to know by four is those, with the eye;
to know by five is those, with the ear;

<!-- Tamil source: scan 168 / printed 167 -->

to know by six is those, with the mind;
those who understood rightly laid down the order.”

(`Marabiyal` — Sutra 27)

Its meaning may be explained thus:

A life-form possessing one sense is able to know through the body. A life-form possessing two senses can know through the body and the mouth. A life-form possessing three senses can know through the body, mouth and nose. A four-sensed life-form can know through body, mouth, nose and eye. A five-sensed life-form can know through body, mouth, nose, eye and ear. In addition to these, one that can know through the mind as a sixth faculty is said to possess six senses. Thus living beings are divided into six kinds.

Not only that: when `Marabiyal` also sets out a list showing which living beings possess from one sense up to six senses, we cannot help being amazed at how, in very ancient times itself, a grammar of life was formulated on a scientific basis.

Long ago, subtle observers studied all living beings in the world by taking the five sense-organs in their bodies together with the mind, the inner instrument that draws in and understands them, and revealed to us how those beings rise step by step in knowledge from one sense to six. Through the five faculties—body, mouth, nose, eye and ear—and through the inner faculty of the mind, living beings receive respectively the perceptions of touch, taste, smell, light and sound, and the power of inference. Having discerned closely how they grew in knowledge in this way, the ancient Tamils classified them into six kinds of living beings; this classification is a tradition of `porul` grammar that they perceived and explained.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 169 / printed 168 -->

“Grass and tree are of one sense;
there are others too born of that branch.”

(`Marabiyal` — Sutra 28)

This sutra states that the two kinds of plants—grass and tree—are living beings possessing one sense, able to know through the body. By saying “others too,” it also indicates beings other than these two; they too possess the single faculty of knowing through the body. What further evidence is needed that `Tolkappiyam` is a treasury of the scientific knowledge Tamils possessed about plants two thousand years ago?

After the list of one-sensed beings that can perceive through the body—worms, grasses, trees, creepers and plants—comes:

“Conch and snail are of two senses;
there are others too born of that branch.”

(`Marabiyal` — Sutra 29)

Conch, snail, shellfish and the like are said to be two-sensed beings. The feeling that knows when struck, and the oral sense that comes through taking food, make two faculties.

“Termite and ant are of three senses;
there are others too born of that branch.”

(`Marabiyal` — Sutra 30)

Winged termite, ant, white ant and other beings like them possess three senses.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 170 / printed 169 -->

These are beings able to know through body, mouth and nose.

`Marabiyal` further explains that crab and dragonfly possess four senses, and animals and birds five.

Human beings are those who possess six senses—this grammar is not responsible if one or two out of the six are missing, or if they remain permanently missing!

— `Tolkappiya Poonga` (Flower 93)

It is precisely the sixth faculty—the faculty of discriminating and reasoning—that we call `pagutharivu`, rationalism.

At present, some traditionalists, thinking that rationalism means condemning everything old, condemn rationalists; and, in the same way, a few rationalists imagine that rationalism consists merely in condemning and belittling the old. That has become part of the conduct of today’s world.

We must welcome and rejoice in the new while remembering what the late yet ever-living Thavathiru Kundrakudi Adigalar said: “The example showing that novelty arises from the old is this: rubbish heaps and decayed plants and creepers in the fields become manure and help create a new crop. Thus novelty does not suddenly appear somewhere in the open sky; it emerges by using the manure called the old.” And that old material too must be good manure—not fertiliser mixed with poison that would destroy the seed itself.

To put rationalism into practice does not mean only removing superstitions from human society;

<!-- Tamil source: scan 171 / printed 170 -->

nor only that caste and religious differences must be removed;

nor only that blind acts conducted in the name of God must be rejected outright;

nor only explaining that running to temples and sacred tanks to be cured of illnesses and gain health is the act of those without clarity;

nor only reforming ordinary people who suffer by believing in mantra, tantra, yajna, yoga, spirits, ghosts, goblins and demons;

nor only stripping bare the monastery illusion that deceives people by saying that swamis can sleep only after bathing in rosewater, drinking fruit juice and lying in a bedchamber surrounded by maidens—and that this itself is pure asceticism;

nor only increasing still further the knowledge of the future society in this ever-growing new age of science.

It is not enough if rationalism is used only for all these. Along with them there is one more indispensable matter to think about: Who should administer our village? Who is qualified to bear responsibility for the administration of a town? Who has the competence and clarity to administer a great city, a State, a country—who has the capacity to provide security, peace, prosperity and growth, together with firm strength of principle? Who can administer without confusion, crookedness, deceit, departure from fairness, or the one-sided rule of “one justice for you and another for me”? Rationalism must help us determine who possesses the discipline to govern; it must help us question where wrongdoing is found and stop it.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 172 / printed 171 -->

It is because the sixth faculty shown by `Tolkappiyam` exists that we have a duty to remind those who possess that sixth faculty that rationalism must be used for this purpose too. Therefore, today’s “Thought and Action” is meant to make clear that **“the path of rationalism is needed in every direction.”**
