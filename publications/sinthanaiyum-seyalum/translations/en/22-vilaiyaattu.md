# Sport

<!-- Tamil source: scan 98 / unit marker only -->

Taking it as mere play, Rama, in his youth, is said to have struck the hunchbacked woman on the back with balls of clay in the palace garden at Ayodhya. Whether or not it hurt her, the story says that the anger she developed towards Rama was what later caused even his coronation to be stopped through Kaikeyi. At the end of the tale, students are advised: “See how true the saying ‘play can turn into trouble’ became in Rama’s case.”

In other words, that proverb used in the Ramayana against a playful act has become almost a phrase for frightening young people in every village of India; and news that one of our sporting teams has won abroad—won an award—or that a foreign team has come here, lost and returned has become as rare as a horse’s horn.

When countries such as America, Russia and China carry away twenty medals, thirty medals, in Olympic competitive events, do we not keep heaving sighs of longing and ask when such a happy state will come to India?

Whether it is football, volleyball, tennis, hockey, or a long-distance race—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 99 / printed 98 -->

even in world-class competitions other than cricket, which is not included in the Olympics, the measure of our victories does not give us encouragement. Patriotic young people crowd the arenas with Indian national flags to watch each contest and its result. When our national teams return defeated, their faces and flags drooping as they leave the spectators’ stands, we who watch it on television are left writhing, unable to control the shame that tears at and devours us and the pain that claws at our hearts until blood seems to ooze.

Since the “modern Olympics” began at Athens in 1896, have we been able to take pride in winning even enough gold, silver, or medals of still lower rank to count on all the fingers of our hands?

Countries with populations below ten crores—below two crores—why, even only a few lakhs—declare that victory is assured whether they compete abroad or face foreign teams at home, and then prove it in practice. If our teams are no less in issuing challenges to foreigners, but in the end are also no less in sitting inside a room and crying their hearts out, are we to accept such a pitiable condition? In this vast country of a hundred crore people, can we not select teams of strength and skill in many kinds of sport as one brings pearls and coral up from the sea?

<!-- Tamil source: scan 100 / printed 99 -->

Can it be done today? Can it be done tomorrow? Can it be done within this year? Instead of standing bewildered by such questions, if we act with the determination that it must certainly be achieved one day, anything can be achieved! If it is a necessary “good”, it must be made possible!

Parents must not think their duty ends with raising the child in their house and sending that child to school. They must come forward not only to produce good scholars, but also to prepare capable sportsmen and sportswomen and give them to the country. When those athletes are selected and teams formed, importance must not be given to only one part of the country; the opportunity must be shared among all regions. For those entrusted with this responsibility, and for the committees responsible, patriotism alone must stand in the heart as a lighthouse casting light in all four directions—not as lamps shut inside pots, with the narrow vision that it is enough to raise only oneself!

Parents who are government employees and teachers should first give attention to this, giving due place to cleanliness whether their children play in village streets and open grounds or on city playing fields. They should encourage other parents to follow them; village elders and city notables should come forward without grumbling to provide every necessary facility. If that happens, there can be no doubt that our country too will advance by winning victories in the field of sport!

9 - 8 - 2005
