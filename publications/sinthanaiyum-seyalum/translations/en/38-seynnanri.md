# Gratitude for Help Received

<!-- Tamil source: scan 163 / unit marker only -->

Among the great list of three-letter Tamil words such as love, knowledge, affection, valour and victory—and many more besides—if there is one word that has become something we use frequently in everyday life without attaching any special distinction to it, we may say it is the word `nandri`—“thanks.”

We ask someone wearing a watch what time it is. If he says “four,” we say, “Thanks.”

At an eatery, when an attendant brings and places before us the food or drink we asked for, we look at him and say, “Thanks.”

After eating, as we leave the establishment, we hand money to the cashier seated in front. Saying “Thanks,” he receives it and gives us the balance after deducting the bill; saying “Thanks,” we receive that change.

From there we come straight to the bus stop. When we say “Anna Nagar,” the conductor tears off and hands us a ticket. Instead of giving the fare as one coin, we count out the change. The conductor—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 164 / printed 163 -->

accepts it saying “Thanks” and moves on to the next passenger. We look for a place to sit in the bus. Someone shifts and makes room for us. Saying “Thanks,” we sit down there.

We get down from the bus and walk home. Asking, “Why are you sweating so much like this!” my wife switches on the electric fan. Saying “Thanks,” I undo the buttons of my shirt and recline in the easy chair to enjoy the breeze!

I ask my dear son to bring me the nearby book of commentary on the Thirukkural. He brings it; thanking him, I turn the pages. My eyes fall on the very page I had read and marked before leaving for the office that morning.

In the chapter `Knowing Gratitude for Help Received`, there is a Kural whose meaning is: “The greatness of help is not determined by the measure in which it is rendered; its measure is assessed according to the character of the one who receives that help”:

“Help is not bounded by the measure of the help;
it is bounded by the worth of the one helped.”

When we read this Kural again and again, how deeply it makes us think that it appears in the chapter called `Knowing Gratitude for Help Received`!

In daily life, when we utter the frequently used word “thanks”—whether in English, in some other foreign language, or indeed in our mother Tamil—or when we place it at the end of letters we write, almost like a concluding formula, the word carries neither life nor impact.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 165 / printed 164 -->

We give the sound “thanks” about as much importance as we give the sound of a cough, a clearing of the throat, or a sneeze. No, no—if we say that we use the word wholeheartedly, with inner affection, then that is merely an act of deceiving ourselves and deceiving others too.

If we like, we may take saying “thanks” as a mark of culture. But we must not forget one thing: there is a difference between **saying** gratitude and **showing** gratitude. At the same time, we must not expect that those who receive our help will take that help exactly as capital, run a great enterprise with it, and return it to us with the proper interest added.

Yet Ayya Valluvar says in one Kural that “help rendered out of love without calculating what benefit will come from it is greater than the sea”; and in the very next Kural he says that those who understand benefit will regard even a millet-seed’s measure of good done to them as a palmyra’s measure.

“If the worth of help done without weighing its return is weighed,
its goodness is greater than the sea.”

“Even if good of a millet-seed’s measure is done,
those who know its worth will take it as a palmyra’s measure.”

As I sit marvelling at and reflecting on the depth of thought in these two Kurals,

my wife’s voice is heard: “Aren’t you coming to eat? Today it’s the `vathal kuzhambu` you like!”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 166 / printed 165 -->

“Is that so? Vathal kuzhambu? Many ‘thanks!’” I say, put the Kural book back where it was, and get up to go inside!

“Wash your hands first”—my wife gives me a vessel of water.

Showing “thanks” with my face, I take the vessel, wash my hands, and set to with gusto on the meal with vathal kuzhambu—

“How is the vathal kuzhambu?”

“Wonderful! Thanks!”

All these words of “thanks” are merely words that come from the mouth!

All these thanks are things that are **said**—

it is gratitude that is **shown**

that will stand firm through time—

like letters carved in stone,

unable to be erased even by sea cataclysms.
