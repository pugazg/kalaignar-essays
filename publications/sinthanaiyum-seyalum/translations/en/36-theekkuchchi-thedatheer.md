# Don't Look for a Matchstick!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 155 / unit marker only -->

In the language struggle waged against Hindi domination to protect the Tamil language and establish the rights of Tamils, Singath Thamizhan Chinnasamy died by setting himself on fire in the city of Tiruchi. Even while explaining that a method of destroying one's own life in that way is itself akin to extreme violence, the leaders of the Dravidian movement struggled desperately to stop the cruelty by which, during that language struggle and later, unable to bear the repression and imprisonment inflicted on leaders, no fewer than a hundred Tamil youths embraced fire or drank poison and sacrificed their lives. There was pride in declaring that this sacred land would never forget the honoured names of those flames of sacrifice; at the same time, advice and loving appeals were issued saying that those lives were needed to perform many more good deeds in the world, and therefore they must not seek to destroy themselves.

I think back on those days surrounded by sorrow. Seeing those who entered the fire and died from it, I too have writhed like a worm caught in flame.

Even now that field of fire seems to rise around us like a long, high wall. Unable to bear the sight—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 156 / printed 155 -->

even when I close my eyes, a sound that startles us is heard:

“It is good to enter the fire.”

Yes; these words form a line in a literary work and fill us with dread.

Her husband, dear as life itself, has died; there is no life for her after this—thus in the Silappathikaram we see Kopperunthevi fall upon the breast of Nedunchezhiyan Pandiyan, who had given up his life for justice, and give up her own life.

We see too the queen of Bhootha Pandiyan,
crying and writhing at the thought
of enduring the cruelty of widowhood
after losing her husband,
and resolving that destroying
her own life was the best end—

from ancient times to this new age,
ascending the husband's pyre and dying
has, for various reasons,
become a familiar practice.
Yet here are the words “It is good to enter the fire.”
`Nanmanikkadigai` makes us understand
in what circumstance they took shape.

Yesterday, arranging three rare ideas—classifying them as dry ginger, pepper and long pepper—I explained and wrote about a poem from the work `Thirikadugam`.

Today I wish to cite a line from a poem in `Nanmanikkadigai`, another work in the same series of Pathinenkeezhkanakku texts.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 157 / printed 156 -->

`Nanmanikkadigai` is a work of one hundred and six venpas composed by the great poet called Vilambinaganar because he was born in a place called Vilambi, in a period said to be earlier than two hundred years C.E.

Each venpa sets out ideas as though four gems had been strung together; that is why it became `Nanmanikkadigai`!

Vilambinaganar himself asks a question: “Which is better than what?” And it is as an answer to that question that he says, “It is good to enter the fire.”

When there is no yaazh producing sweet, ordered music, the loud sound of the parai is better; compared with men who lack greatness, a community of women of good conduct is better; rather than eating food that has gone bad, enduring the suffering of hunger is better. After setting out these three ideas, why does he also say, “It is good to enter the fire”? Do you know?

“Rather than parting
from one who loves you,
it is good to enter the fire.”

That is: “Rather than leave and live apart from one who showed you love, desired you and cherished you, it is better to destroy one's life in fire,” he says.

Yes; the poet gives such emphasis to the grief of separation that arises between people of great love, saying that it cannot be resolved except by falling into the fire and giving up life—

when the sound of a melodious yaazh is absent,
he can even say that the sound of the parai
is good—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 158 / printed 157 -->

the greatness absent in men
can be made good by the greatness of women—

rather than eat spoiled food,
one can bear the suffering of hunger;

but

the grief of separation arising
between those bound by great love,
he says, is resolved only by entering the fire.

We must take this as his way of
making us understand the greatness
of friendship that does not part,
and resolve to remain without parting;
we must not immediately go searching
for a matchstick and kerosene.

* * *

“The parai is better than a yaazh without melody—
a woman of fullness is better
than men without distinction—
hunger is better than eating spoiled food—
rather than parting from one who loves you,
it is good to enter the fire.”

(Poem 15 —
`Nanmanikkadigai`)
