# The Anklet and the Gem!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 159 / unit marker only -->

In order to insist that desires must be renounced—and that, among them, desire for women must be hated far more strongly than desire for land or gold—detached ascetics have composed poems that degrade a woman, limb by limb, to a considerable extent. In particular, Jain poets occupy a large place in the list of those who gave us the poems of the `Naladiyar`. Those poems show that they adopted that path in order to insist that one must not be deluded by the beauty of the perishable body and stray from the path of righteousness. In the `Naladiyar` collection of four hundred venpas sung by four hundred poets, here are two poems as evidence for what I say.

“Entrails and fat, blood and bone,
skin joined with sinews—and between them,
flesh and grease placed together: among these,
in what respect is she a cool-garlanded maiden?”

It is a poem carrying this irritated question: looking at one made of intestines, fat, blood, bone, skin and nerves, with flesh and bodily grease between them, what taste or connoisseurship is there in fondly calling her—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 160 / printed 159 -->

“O maiden wearing a cool garland”?

Here is another poem:

“A pot with nine wound-like openings, seeping foulness,
a vessel of churning broth—the foolish,
dazzled by the dark skin spread over it, call her
broad-shouldered, bangled maiden.”

Seeing the body as a pot with nine openings from which a seeping, stinking mixture of filth emerges, people of little understanding are captivated by attraction and babble that she is a broad-shouldered woman, a graceful creeper adorned with bangles.

Like these two poems we find in the `Naladiyar`, the great Jain poets composed many such verses.

And amid those very traditions come `Silappathikaram` and `Civaka Cintamani`, works included in the line of the Five Great Epics.

The great poet Ilango Adigal, who composed `Silappathikaram`, and the distinguished Tiruttakkatevar, who gave us `Civaka Cintamani`, were both adherents of the Jain religion.

The poems composed by these Jain elders are not arranged to the extent of saying that women—women in particular—must be hated and cast aside, as in the works of the Jain elders who composed the `Naladiyar`; more than that, they are filled with alluring imagination.

“Women of delicate waists, gleaming
as though lightning had been dissolved and painted,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 161 / printed 160 -->

sank into the pallor upon their lovely bodies,
as though gold had been dissolved and painted there;
seeing mountains hide away, unable to equal
Civakan’s shoulders stained with kumkum,
they melted and stood like cows that cry
when their calves are taken away.”

Women of slender waists, shining as though lightning had been dissolved and sprinkled to fashion their limbs, sank into the pallor that appeared on their radiant bodies as though gold had been dissolved and painted upon them. Seeing mountains themselves hide because they could not equal Civakan’s shoulders, they stood melting like cows that cry when their calves are separated from them.

And do you know with what delighted feeling Ilango Adigal, the Jain poet who gave us `Silappathikaram`, explains Kannagi and Kovalan studying the epic of the bridal chamber?

“Thinking of the impermanence of life upon the earth, as though they had resolved that very day to renounce everything, they experienced every pleasure too numerous even to list—and how? Like frenzied snakes entwining and embracing one another, Kannagi and Kovalan joined hands as loving spouses.”

Thus this venpa of `Silappathikaram` reaches the very summit of imagination!

“As though two radiant serpents joined and mingled,
they became one;
as loving spouses they joined hands—all pleasures
whose names do not fade they drew close to, as though
seeing life on earth impermanent, they would renounce it today.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 162 / printed 161 -->

It seems that Tiruttakkatevar and Ilango Adigal composed `Civaka Cintamani` and `Silappathikaram` respectively, letting the excellence of `akam`—the inner world of love—shine forth, as splendid answers to the opinion expressed by the great poets of those times that Jains might be skilled at singing of ascetic renunciation but would not know how to compose love poetry overflowing with pleasure!

Meanings:

`thadiyum vazhumbum` = flesh and bodily grease

`panigal` = snakes
