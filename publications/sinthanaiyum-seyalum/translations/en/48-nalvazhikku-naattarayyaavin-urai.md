# Nattarayya's Commentary on Nalvazhi!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 210 / unit marker only -->

The elder Mr. N. M. Venkatasamy Nattar—affectionately called `Nattar Ayya` by lovers of Tamil, a name fragrant with good Tamil—lived not only as a guardian of Tamil; he also carried an unending concern that the Tamil people should not be ruined by divisions of caste. He wrote essays and books insisting upon that idea. The rare commentaries he wrote for Avvai Pirattiyar’s `Nalvazhi` and Athiveerarama Pandiyar’s `Narunthogai` have been praised not merely as simple, but as beautiful.

While reading his commentary on `Nalvazhi`, I came upon a poem beginning “By the thirtieth year, free of the three...” I read the whole poem and eagerly wanted to know what commentary Nattarayya had written for it.

“By the thirtieth year, freed of the three,
if one unfailingly gains not the One within oneself—
one’s learning will be learning alone,
as in old age a lovely woman’s breasts will be breasts alone.”

For this poem, the commentary he gives is as follows:

<!-- Tamil source: scan 211 / printed 210 -->

“By the age of thirty, having rid oneself of the three faults—lust, anger and delusion—if one fails unfailingly to attain within oneself God, the `kevala porul`, then one will merely possess the learning one studies, just as beautiful women in old age merely possess breasts without enjoying pleasure in union with a husband.”

The commentator N. M. V. Nattarayya says that its general meaning is: “Before old age arrives, one must remove the three faults called lust, anger and delusion and strive to attain the true substance and rejoice in it.” To make it still simpler and clearer, he gives this meaning: “Merely learning education without acquiring the awareness called God is like a woman possessing breasts alone without experiencing pleasure with her husband!”

Nattarayya expands `free of the three` as the state in which lust, anger and delusion—the three—are absent.

Next, when commenting on “if one fails to gain the One within oneself,” he writes, “if one fails unfailingly to attain God within oneself.” But rather than simply saying God directly, he says, “God, the `kevala porul`.”

At first everyone is bound to be astonished—what is this, calling God a `kevala porul`? Would Avvaiyar, who sang `Nalvazhi`, really have sung like that? People will be startled!

I too began turning the pages of a Tamil dictionary. For `kevalam` I found many meanings: triviality, disgrace, low state, certainty, order, unequalled;

<!-- Tamil source: scan 212 / printed 211 -->

and for `kevalappaduthuthal`, the meaning “to insult.” Seeing these, the question of how Nattarayya could have used this word for God swelled in my heart, and I looked more sharply at the dictionary. I removed my doubt. For `kevala porul`, the dictionary gives one single meaning: **Parabrahmam**. Yes; I say this with the `Kazhaga Tamil Dictionary`, published by the Tirunelveli South India Saiva Siddhanta Publishing Society, Chennai, as my evidence.

My purpose in using this `Nalvazhi` poem and Nattarayya’s commentary upon it is only to demonstrate, through the dictionary, that Tamil words can acquire meanings that differ from, vary from and even contradict one another according to the place in which the words occur. I have not the slightest other purpose.

For example, in the Tamil dictionary, `kaanavar` may mean “people of the Kurinji land,” “people of the Mullai land,” “chiefs of the Paalai land,” or “hunters.”

Change `kaanavar` to `kaanavan` and search for the meaning, and the same dictionary also says “monkey.”

For `makkal`, the dictionary says “human beings.” But if legs seem to sprout on that same word and it becomes `maakkal`, it can mean “those without rational understanding,” or people of five senses—and why, the same dictionary does not fail to say that `maakkal` can also mean “human beings”!

`El` can mean “to accept”; it can also mean “to differ.” `Ichchai` can mean “desire”; the dictionary also gives “falsehood” as a meaning.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 213 / printed 212 -->

`Il` can mean “house.” It can also mean “absence”!

What a wonder! Did you see how one `kevala porul` has been useful in showing how many Tamil words possess how many kinds of meanings?

And was it not `Nalvazhi`, together with Nattarayya’s commentary upon it, that gave both you and me the opportunity to explain this and to learn these details!
