# Joy and Sorrow!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 69 / unit marker only -->

When we say, “the town laughed”, it means that someone—or some people—was subjected to some humiliation, and that the people of that town spoke of them contemptuously!

On television I saw a scene in which an entire country laughed. And when that country laughed, I also saw another country weep and lament. If I put it in a style that confuses you and your friends,

one country laughed,

one country cried,

the whole world sank into sorrow!

That can be stated in three sentences. Has the confusion not cleared? Is it increasing? Here—I shall put a full stop to it.

The world-famous “Olympic Games” are held once every four years. During the period of the “Olympics”, we have all felt the excitement among sports enthusiasts in every country as they await the results of the competitions and the list of medal-winning nations.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 70 / printed 69 -->

The Olympics were held in Sydney in 2000 and in Athens in 2004; after they were held in Beijing, China, in 2008, the selection of where the 2012 Olympics should be held took place in Singapore, and it was decided that the 2012 Olympics would be in London.

As soon as it was announced that 2012 would be in London, English men, women and children who had been sitting in a park waiting for the news leapt into the air, embraced one another, danced about and expressed their joy; it made those who watched them thrill.

This was in England—in the city of London!

At the same time, we saw people sitting in parks in France, storing their eagerness in their hearts and on their faces and keeping their ears and eyes fixed in the direction from which the news would come, wilt when the Olympic opportunity did not come to their country, treating it as though grief had arrived at their own homes.

It was to show how much eagerness and concern those people have that their country should gain distinction even in a sporting contest, and that it should get the opportunity for it, that I said “one country laughed! one country cried”, placing England and France respectively before you.

The homeland of these Olympic games, begun in 776 B.C., is Greece! After the independence of that Greek country was taken away by the Romans, the Olympic games were banned in A.D. 393.

After a long interval, through the efforts of the Frenchman Pierre de Coubertin, it was only in the year 1896 that—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 71 / printed 70 -->

the modern Olympic games were revived and began to be held in the city of Athens! The “Oxford Dictionary” describes this as: “(1) An ancient Greek festival held at Olympia every four years, with athletic, literary, and musical competitions. (2) A modern international revival of this as a sports festival held every four years since 1896 in different venues”.

The Olympic festival was first held at Olympia in Greece, a place rich in natural resources. It was conducted at Olympia once every four years as a religious festival. During the period of this festival, an agreement to stop war would be in force among the Greek cities. At that time no king could launch an invasion against another king.

At first, the competitions held were mainly running races. That was because, in those days, warriors had to run and carry information about an enemy country’s invasion; as preparation for this, importance was given to running races. Those who won in this competition received the title of victor. In addition, the winners were crowned with an olive branch cut with a knife made of gold.

The Olympic contests of the early period were conducted in ways that can astonish us. A competitor could take part only naked, without wearing clothes. Footwear could not be worn. There were restrictions such as only Greeks being allowed to participate. In later times Romans were permitted to participate. Women were forbidden not merely to take part in the contests but even to watch them. If any woman violated that prohibition and watched, the punishment for her was death.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 72 / printed 71 -->

In the modern Olympic games, in the year they began, 14 countries participated. There were 241 competitors. All of them were men.

When one thinks of India in the Olympics, hockey is what immediately comes to mind. Hockey was introduced in 1908. From 1928 to 1956 India achieved the feat of winning six gold medals. It later won gold in 1964 and 1980, making a total of 8 gold medals. Silver and bronze: 2.

In Olympic history, India, with a population of more than 100 crore, has so far won only 4 medals in individual events. They too are only silver and bronze medals! In individual events, K.D. Jadhav in wrestling in 1952, Leander Paes in tennis singles in 1996, and Karnam Malleswari in weightlifting at the 2000 Sydney Olympics won bronze medals.

When the weightlifting events at the 2000 Sydney Olympics were under way, Karnam Malleswari, competing for India, lifted 240 kilograms and won bronze. At the competition venue, only her coach was there with Malleswari. The other Indian athletes and the officials who had accompanied them were not there. The reason was the thought: where are we going to get a medal in this event?

In the 2004 Olympics, the United States took first place with a total of 103 medals, including 35 gold, 39 silver and 29 bronze. China won 63 medals, including 32 gold, 17 silver and 14 bronze; Russia won 92 medals, including 27 gold, 27 silver and 38 bronze. India received only one silver—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 73 / printed 72 -->

medal and therefore reached 65th place in the medal table.

In the shooting event held on 17-8-2004 at the 2004 Olympics, Rajyavardhan Singh Rathore won a silver medal for India.

When we look at the medal list our Indian nation has obtained in the Olympics, we can only grieve that “we are in the forefront of the rear ranks”. For this condition to change, and for us to shine without equal not only in the Olympics but in every sporting competition held in the world, the Union Government itself, with the cooperation of the State Governments, must undertake intensive efforts.

Even when we angrily scold someone we say, “Hey, don’t play games!” Has “play” become such an unwanted word for us! We must therefore understand that even in play we must not hate sport, and take the “lost victories” in many competitions as lessons. That alone will be the way to safeguard India’s honour.

I began by saying: one country laughed, one country cried, the whole world sank into sorrow—yes; just when the people of that country were in unbearable excitement on hearing that England had won the right to conduct the Olympic games in 2012, there came the incident in India in which militants entered Ayodhya and all of them were shot dead. Even though the militants were killed, we are left with the unending anxiety that the fire kindled by terrorism must not spread!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 74 / printed 73 -->

Even while they were rejoicing that they were going to conduct the Olympics in England, came the news that bombs had exploded at four to seven places in the city of London, that many people had died and that a thousand had been injured! And here in Tamil Nadu, at Vadasery market in Nagercoil, hundreds of shops have been reduced to ashes by fire and six people have died.

The Olympics on one side—

the town and the world

lying immersed

in unending pain on the other—

Not only is life itself a coming together of joy and sorrow; the history of many countries of the world too comes to be shaped in just that way. Just as we rejoice on hearing sweet news, let us also raise our shoulders to face sorrows with stout hearts.

9 - 7 - 2005
