# A Feast for Disease?

<!-- Tamil source: scan 94 / unit marker only -->

Some people cannot stop certain habits. When reading a book and turning a page to read the next one, instead of simply turning the sheet, they wet a finger on the tongue or lips and use the moisture of that saliva to touch the paper and turn the page.

Before that book was taken up for reading, it might have been lying in a library or on a household shelf amid a film of dust.

So there is a chance that dust—though invisible to the eye—may cling to the moist finger touching that dusty page.

The dust that clings in this way may, when the same finger turns the next page, stick to that sheet. The place where it sticks may even become a convenient vehicle on which the germs of infectious disease can easily climb and ride. Something unseen by the eye and unnoticed by the mind begins as an itch or irritation, slowly causes trouble, and later doctors will even give that disease a name.

There is reluctance to touch water with a finger for turning the sheet; it is easy instead to move quickly and wet the finger with saliva from the tongue.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 95 / printed 94 -->

But if that ends in harm, one has to suffer.

Some others put a finger or fingers into the armpit and gather dirt. They do it in an absent-minded state, almost as though in deep sleep. They roll the dirt gathered from the armpit between two fingers. Even then their expression is one of self-forgetting pleasure! They bring the rolled ball of dirt before the nose and smell its odour. When they finally throw that ball of dirt away depends on their own choice.

Remember: it is with those very dirt-smeared fingers that they stroke the tongue and use the saliva to separate pages lightly stuck together in a book and read them. When that is so, is it not natural that a calamity should arise as though disease germs themselves had been invited to a feast!

Something still more harmful than wetting one’s fingers with saliva to turn book pages is this: sometimes invitations for a function, wedding cards or weekly newspapers have to be sent through the post, and all those postage stamps are licked for a moment on the gum at the back, moistened with the tongue, and then fixed directly onto their envelopes. What more poisonous kind of ‘infection’ could there be!

By running the tongue over and moistening the gum-coated backs of hundreds of postage stamps, conditions can readily arise for any kind of poisonous disease to take hold. And not only the gum on the backs of postage stamps: some people also—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 96 / printed 95 -->

moisten the adhesive part of envelopes with the tongue with such enthusiasm that, watching them, it can even look as though they are playing the `morsing` instrument.

What is the reason for all this? Even if there is concern for cleanliness, one would have to bring water to moisten the stamp—one would need a cup—one would have to go to where the water is. The laziness produced by that thought is the principal reason—

It is our own body. But can we therefore rub and rub our fingers in the armpit, gather and roll up the dirt, and, as Mother Parvati Devi is said to have made a figure of Vinayagar and seated it as a guardian deity at the entrance to her chamber, seat such a figure ourselves? Should we not understand the difference between ‘divine’ dirt and human dirt? Human dirt gives off a stench that pierces the nose! If this dirt mixes with saliva—with the lips—with the tongue—could infectious germs (`Infection`) not take hold to the point of damaging life itself? We must ask and gain clarity. There is no clear assessment at all concerning ‘divine’ dirt. Since what concerns us now is human health, we are under the necessity of saying only what people need—what is appropriate for them.

It is not only the habit of rubbing the armpit with a finger. Every human being may have some bodily mannerism: some bite their lips frequently; some blink their eyes far too much—excessively! repeatedly! All such people should certainly seek medical advice.

Some people, instead of immediately spitting aside phlegm cleared from the throat, keep rolling it about in the mouth while continuing to speak with others. On the faces of those listening—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 97 / printed 96 -->

it will look as though lamp oil is running down; those who are trapped there will ache with the hope that the person will soon take leave. Some bodily mannerisms may even be amusing to watch—but when what begins in the armpit develops into a dirt-storage factory, that is when danger begins, and we have to warn people of it.
