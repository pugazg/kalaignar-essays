# A Feminist Revolution!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 173 / unit marker only -->

Dr. N. Velusamy himself came in person and gave me the large book `Feminism in the Twenty-First Century`, which brings together more than a hundred research papers presented at a seminar conducted by the Tamil Department of Erode Arts College (Autonomous), with Dr. N. Velusamy as director. Yes—`feminism` is the great heading of the book.

Just as `punniyam` is a good word pleasing to devotees—

just as `kanniyam` is a good word befitting the worthy—

so too `penniyam`, feminism, is a word that has risen to insist upon all the rights of women.

This book reminded me of the work `Why Did Woman Become a Slave?`, containing the ideas Thanthai Periyar expressed many years ago and published by the `Periyar Self-Respect Propaganda Institution`. I took it out again now and read it through once more.

If the research papers presented at the Erode Arts College seminar are the halls and towers of a palace, then Periyar’s study `Why Did Woman Become a Slave?` must be called the crowning finial atop all those towers.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 174 / printed 173 -->

Among the culminating ideas in that book is this:

“As far as we are concerned, we hold that everything said for men applies to women as well; that they too must receive the same as men and conduct themselves accordingly; that in worldly life, society, law and religion, women must possess the same conveniences and rights as men; that only then can women be said to have obtained true freedom, and only then can they attain true, satisfying happiness and desire.”

The many ideas of Thanthai Periyar in that book appear to us like layer upon layer of petals in a flower, expanding upon this passage.

Mr. Velusamy has given me the seminar volume on `feminism` with great affection. Yet when I see that it also contains studies of the poems of my beloved daughter Kanimozhi against the background of feminism, I remember that, trained in Periyar’s camp with Anna as teacher, even in my student days in the 1940s I wrote an article carrying this very idea under the title `Ilamai Pali`—`The Sacrifice of Youth`—in the second or third issue of Anna’s `Dravida Nadu`. It was a written portrait drawn with emotion of how a woman’s youth is sacrificed!

My beloved younger brother Veeramani wrote a foreword as early as 1987 to the sixteenth edition of `Why Did Woman Become a Slave?`, comparing Periyar’s thoughts on women’s rights with the thoughts of Bertrand Russell.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 175 / printed 174 -->

Thus, from generation to generation, those who come in Periyar’s path have dedicated themselves to labouring, in every way possible, to develop and strengthen the women’s-liberation movement begun by Periyar from then until today. It is as evidence of that continuity that we raise our voice loudly, demanding that all parties represented in the Indian Parliament agree that reservation for women must be guaranteed by law.

Among those who find their due place in the stories, essays and poems examined and interpreted in the seminar papers in the `Feminism` volume are many like me who came in the path of Ayya and Anna.

From the time I wrote `Ilamai Pali` as my first article, portraying the sorrow of a young woman, until today—whether story, epic, screen portrait, drama or novel—the point of my pen has always served as the point of the sword in the army fighting for women’s rights.

Even while writing commentary on the Kurals of Ayya Valluvar, I brought out women’s rights—not by forcing them into the text, but naturally.

“She worships no god; she rises worshipping her husband;
if she says ‘Rain,’ the rain will fall.”

From Parimelazhagar onward, many scholars explained this as meaning that if a wife who rises worshipping her husband says “Rain,” rain will fall.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 176 / printed 175 -->

Paavendhar Bharathidasan, however, explained it as: “That wife is like rain that falls at the moment one says, ‘Fall.’”

But the commentary I gave was: “A wife who worships her husband as God will think of herself as a slave, like rain that trembles in fear and falls the instant it is commanded, ‘Fall.’”

In the film `Manthiri Kumari`, Amuthavalli rolls the deceitful king down the mountain. I declared even then that I had taken the root of that feminism from the literary work `Kundalakesi`!

In `Devaki` too—and in the films that followed one after another, `Thirumbippaar`, `Parasakthi`, `Neethikku Thandanai`—one can know without the help of a research paper that characters were created as representatives of feminism!

In both the play and film `Manimakudam`, is it not the intelligent woman Alli who stands as leader of the revolutionary group!

“Like a figure rolling
within the eye,
know that a heart too
belongs to a woman.”

These are lines of poetry I wrote before 1945.

None of this is self-advertisement; it is an explanation that the flame of the Self-Respect movement has, from those days onward, also remained a new light for feminism.

In the seminar collection given to me, Mr. V. Marimuthu has written that “in the twenty-first century, an effort has recently been made to give men and women equal shares in property rights.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 177 / printed 176 -->

From this it appears that some people like him do not show complete attention to the history of the Dravidian movement.

Only now has a law giving women property rights come at the all-India level.

But I do not understand how they forgot that as early as 1989, during the government under my leadership, Tamil Nadu had already enacted a law giving women an equal share in property rights.

Without treating these little criticisms from the point of my pen as spear-points directed at the volume, the more I think about this collection of studies on feminism, the more I am filled with wonder and delight.

7 - 10 - 2005
