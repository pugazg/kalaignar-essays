# Thirikadugam

<!-- Tamil source: scan 152 / unit marker only -->

Am I not one accustomed to getting up before I can fall? So when I awoke, my eyes first had to fall on the faces of the books nearby. On top of them was a small book called `Thirikadugam`, containing one hundred venpas, which Chinnakuthusi gave me yesterday. There are those who have heard of Pathuppaattu, Ettuthogai and Pathinenkeezhkanakku. There are also those who have read them many times.

Pathinenkeezhkanakku consists of eighteen works. `Thirikadugam`, it seems, is one among those eighteen.

Just as dry ginger, pepper and long pepper—the three ingredients—are said to be useful both for curing disease and preventing it before it comes, so each poem in `Thirikadugam` is said to contain three ideas arranged together, capable of bringing benefit to body and mind.

The poet who composed all one hundred poems in this work was Nallathanar.

Although the aim of the poems is to recommend good qualities and teach good conduct, the background of old-time ideas does not fail to show its head in some poems of this work!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 153 / printed 152 -->

Even so, noble ideas like stars flashing between clouds do not fail to attract us.

Here is a poem that seems to stab a finger at the condition of today's Tamil Nadu:

“A town without a Kanakkayar who teaches,
an assembly without elders who can end disputes,
and living beside one who will not share out food—
these three do not produce good.”

Its meaning is as follows:

A town without a teacher who provides education,

an assembly where even the elder who should
settle a problem lacks maturity of understanding,

and a condition in which one cannot sit beside
a person who shares out food before eating—

these are things that do not produce welfare;
they can produce harm.

Is it enough merely to sink into amazement at the way Nallathanar explains how a town, the character of those sitting beside us when we eat, and an assembly of townspeople must be constituted if they are to produce good—and how, if they are not so constituted, they will produce only harm?

We must labour and make efforts so that the people of a town are educated—or at least have learned something. If a condition is to arise in which there is no one illiterate in any town, must there not be a sufficient number of teachers called `Kanakkayar`, those gods who teach letters—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 154 / printed 153 -->

and literacy?

In the same way, following Ayya Valluvar, who gave the foremost among all the ideas compiled by learned people on the culture of “sharing one's food and protecting many lives,” should we not first distribute among others the food, clothing and things that pass through our hands and then take what belongs to us, rather than taking and hiding everything away for ourselves?

As yet another idea, in this poem Nallathanar says that an assembly must contain “elders who can end disputes,” and emphatically points out that an assembly without people of such maturity cannot be one that produces good.

We need not wander in search of evidence for this. In foreign countries, uproar and disorder in assemblies may occur once in some distant while. But when we see such things occurring every day in our own Parliament, Legislative Assemblies, municipal corporations, municipalities and local bodies,

does not the truth conveyed by Nallathanar through `Thirikadugam`—that an assembly without people mature in understanding will bring harm to the country and its people—seem an acceptable truth indeed?
