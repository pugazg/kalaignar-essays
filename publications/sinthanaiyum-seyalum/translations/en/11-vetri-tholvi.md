# Victory and Defeat!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 56 / unit marker only -->

In the flower-gardens of Sangam literature and in the pages of history, I have seen and read of the heroic Tamil who swung the sword and piled up victories on the battlefront, and of the honourable Tamil who lost the chance of victory. Of those two, the one who has captivated me most is the Tamil who did not wilt in defeat and guarded his honour.

He lost his land, lost his palace and found himself caught in prison. Yet because water to quench his parched mouth was not brought to him in prison at the proper time, he felt that his honour itself had been lost and died—the Tamil named Cheraman Kanaikkal Irumporai. Why do we still remember that difficult-to-pronounce name and honour him? Is it not because defeat did not make him wilt, and because he valued honour above life itself?

When a battalion of warriors moved toward the field with the discipline of a flock of goats, his son stood in the ranks as one warrior among all the others. Yet the king gave that son alone a little more of the drink called `theral`. A mother laments that her son is still alive instead of lying on the bier among those who gave their lives in that battle, receiving the king’s praise—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 57 / printed 56 -->

and poet-mother Avvai sheds tears. Even now, when I think of that heroic mother, a film of tears covers my eyes.

Rather than seeing her son return victorious from the cruel killing field wearing the garland of victory, that great Tamil woman would far prefer him to return in a funeral procession as one of those who died in battle bearing an honour-wound. Do we not feel moved to bow before her?

“Like the ram among white goats,  
though young men like himself were there,  
the `theral` extended past many to my boy—  
they have not laid him upon the legless cot  
and covered him with pure white cloth.”

`Purananuru` — (Avvaiyar)

(`seccai` = ram  
`mandai` = the liquor called `theral`  
`kaal-kazhi kattil` = a cot without legs, a bier)

Even if one were born merely as a lump of flesh without human limbs and organs—legs, hands, eyes, nose or ears—the heroic tradition laid down the rule that the body must bear a wound received in battle; a wound would therefore be cut into it with a sword, and only then would it be sent to the earth. The self-respecting heroic spirit shown by this Tamil tradition makes our bodies thrill!

Because Tamil kings had been spoken of with contempt, Cheran Senguttuvan marched his army as far as the Himalayas, defeated the Aryan kings called Kanaka Visayar at the place called Kuyilaluvam, made them bear upon their heads the stone for Kannagi’s statue, and returned beating the drum of victory. Yet, more than him—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 58 / printed 57 -->

King Cheraman Kanaikkal Irumporai, who did not wilt in defeat and guarded his honour, takes a higher place in our hearts.

Because a mother believes that her son must gain a place even higher than his, she remembers that, although many warriors stood in the battle line, the king himself came forward and gave her son the `theral` drink with his own hand; and old Avvai laments that the son has still not come forward to receive an honour-wound on the battlefield, die and guard the glory of the soil. That lament becomes a `Purananuru` poem!

A mother wishes her son to attain a warrior’s death merely because he received a drink from the hand of the ruler. If that son had received benefits and positions from the king as well, what more might she have expected of him?

Therefore, not only in the literature and histories of that day, but even today, whoever takes part in political, social or public life must not search for shortcuts in order somehow to achieve the goal. A defeat suffered without allowing the sense of honour to fade—and even a fall suffered after receiving an honour-wound—must be placed on the list of victories. That too is an undeniable truth! One day that truth will rise high; it certainly will!
