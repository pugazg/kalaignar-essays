# Meditation???

<!-- Tamil source: scan 117 / unit marker only -->

From the very beginnings of the Self-Respect Movement, one of the journals that appeared carrying many explanatory articles which identified the superstitions that were ruining society and sought to uproot them was `Pagutharivu`. The volcanic writings of progressive thinkers such as Swami Kaivalyam and Chinthanai Sirpi Singaravelar would appear in that journal as articles scattering sparks. Just as that `Pagutharivu` journal carried Thanthai Periyar's rationalist thoughts, today an intellectual treasury called `Unmai`, edited by dear younger brother Mr. Veeramani, moves among us.

Though I have many tasks without interruption, I have a special interest in reading `Unmai`.

`Unmai` has been offering worthy ideas like a clear stream. In an article published in its issue dated September 1, however, some ideas appearing here and there have left us in confusion. Considering it my duty to point this out, I wish to indicate them.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 118 / printed 117 -->

The very title of that `Unmai` article—“Is What the Swamis Teach Meditation?”—appears to accept meditation itself. After saying that because swamis teach it, “the methods people are following in the name of meditation are wrong,” the article's author, Mr. M. Manoharan, writes: “If meditation had truly been practised in the proper manner, our country should at least have advanced somewhat in every respect. Production should have increased and income should have risen. Famine and hunger should have disappeared.”

Whether it is meditation or physical exercise, it concerns an individual's bodily health. Contrary to what the author says, I see no connection between that and economic problems such as a country's income, famine and hunger. Nor can such a connection be made.

The author, who says meditation must be practised in the proper way, also says: “Swamis deceive people by calling it meditation.”

Except perhaps for one or two, a great many swamis are indeed deceiving people. And some among the people are indeed being deceived. It is not only in the name of meditation; people deceive and are deceived in the name of God as well. Therefore, as evidence that the author does not reject “meditation” itself, he says at one point in his own article that “there is meditation in silambam training.”

They say, “Meditation means bringing the mind to a single point.” Swamis say, “For that purpose, think of a god—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 119 / printed 118 -->

or stare at a single point, and the mind will become one-pointed.”

This too is an idea set out by that author. Does he accept or reject the bringing of the mind to one point? Here is his own answer:

“In martial-arts training, if attention is fixed on the movements of the silambam staffs, the mind becomes concentrated on them.”

The author's difference, then, is over the method by which the mind is to be steadied; he does not reject the steadying of the mind itself.

“At one place they say that if you stare at a point the mind will become one-pointed. If you do that, only boredom will result; the mind will not become one-pointed. You may keep looking at a point for one day, two days or a few days. If you keep looking at the same thing throughout the year, you will only go mad.”

The author writes this at one point. No trainer speaking about meditation says that one must sit looking at the same point throughout an entire year or throughout one's life. So the question of anyone going mad from that does not arise.

“To steady the mind, the body must be given work grounded in understanding.”

“If you tell people to sit cross-legged without giving the body any work, many thoughts will merely surge like waves in the mind; the mind certainly will not become one-pointed.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 120 / printed 119 -->

These views too belong to the author. From them it becomes clear that his difference lies in the method of bringing the mind to one point; he does not reject the practice of steadying the mind.

When I asked an expert—a practitioner of “meditation training” and a teacher of “yoga”—for an explanation, that expert (certainly not a swami) explained it as follows:

“There are many kinds of meditation exercises for bringing the mind to one point. They may be modified according to each person's age, physical condition, mental state, goal and belief. Whichever form of practice is followed, in the end the mind becomes calm and comes to rest in one state.”

About thirty years ago, the Tamil scholar T. P. Meenakshisundaranar strongly urged a suggestion upon me. Accepting it, from then onward I have practised yoga as well as meditation for concentrating the mind. There may have been lapses of a few years here and there in that practice; even so, from experience its benefit can still be felt!

I have also had the practice of walking for a long time. But what does the author in `Unmai` say about walking?

“Walking is not physical exercise at all. It is work that everyone must do every day. To call it exercise is ignorance.”

We must not listen to this claim of the author and abandon walking. If body weight increases, heart trouble and blood pressure may arise. Well-known doctors all advise people to take up walking and reduce body weight—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 121 / printed 120 -->

so walking should be undertaken every day at a fixed time in a broad, well-ventilated open space.

The author says that “karate, kung fu, kuthuvarisai and silambam are all martial arts,” and then says that “yoga is only like learning `A, Aa`; that is not enough for a human being.” I am not opposed to this view of the author. Just as his view can be accepted, would I not also consider yoga and meditation to be practices that can be useful to everyone?

Martial arts too must be learnt only after taking age, circumstances and physical condition into account. The author categorically says, “Yoga is the foundation of martial arts,” and praises martial-arts training by saying, “If one learns it, there will be no need at all to go to a hospital.” That may be accepted without obstruction. In the same way, we accept that meditation and yoga too are excellent exercises for bodily health. The author himself agrees that just as meditation and yoga contain breathing exercises, martial arts also contain them. Breathing exercises help expand and cleanse the lungs.

“If martial-arts practice is continued regularly, one can live healthily up to the age of 100,” says the author.

It is likewise said that those who practise meditation will live a long life.

The famous wrestler “Gama,” a master of martial arts, looked like a mountain—yet he was struck by a bone-wasting disease, became thin as a splinter and died; and Ramana Munivar, trained in the art of meditation, died of a severe `pilavai` disease. Though these may be exceptions—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 122 / printed 121 -->

meditation based on breathing exercises and martial-arts practice based on yoga are both capable of benefiting a person according to that person's age, physical condition, mental state, goal and belief.

On the basis of age, physical condition, mental state, goal and belief, each person may choose one of the two practices according to their own capacity. There is no need for a quarrel over which is superior. The practice may be chosen according to age and other circumstances. The person who gives the training may even be a swami. But he must not be a fake swami. Apart from these points, the author's other views are acceptable.
