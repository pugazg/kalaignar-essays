# Think, O Mind

<!-- Tamil source: scan 221 / unit marker only -->

“I have heard many people say that a snake and a mongoose fight fiercely and that, though the snake finally dies, the snake’s venom will have spread through the mongoose’s body; and that, to remove the poisonous effect, the mongoose falls upon and rolls over a herb known only to itself, rubbing its body against it and thus removing the power of the venom. I do not know how true that is. But because I am one who continues to work in our rationalist movement with a feeling for the dignity of our people, arrows of many kinds fly at me from many directions. To remove the poisonous effect of the wounds they cause, I regard Thanthai Periyar himself as the herb available to me—like the herb said to be available to the mongoose.”

I spoke these words about the “herb” that removes the poison of the “mongoose-snake fight” at a function held at Periyar Thidal on 15 August 1971. I was then serving as Chief Minister. It was at that event that Periyar issued an affectionate command to Annai Maniammaiyar and my beloved younger brother Veeramani that a statue should be erected for me, and himself began the fund for it with his own hand.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 222 / printed 221 -->

Is Periyar merely a herb? Was he not the first matchstick struck to ignite a flame in the sulphur warehouse where sacks upon sacks of superstition had been piled? Was he not the mighty leader of rationalism who could roll away even volcanoes rubbed into eruption by the wind of the deluge as though they were footballs!

Born on 17 September 1879, and from his twelfth year itself, around 1891–92, Periyar repeatedly said:

“From my childhood I had neither caste nor religion—that is, I did not practise them. In places where there was compulsion, however, I may have outwardly pretended. Likewise, regarding God too, I never had belief or fear in my mind. I have never refrained from anything I believed I ought to do because I thought God would be angry or punish me. Nor have I done anything because I thought God would be pleased or give me a reward.

“I still think about whether, at any time in my life, I ever truly believed in caste, religion or God; I have thought about it many times before as well. I have thought and thought about when exactly I ceased to have faith in these things. I have never been able to find it.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 223 / printed 222 -->

Periyar, who made these observations many times, was an adherent of the Congress movement from 1907 and became a Congress member in 1919. He shaped his public life through many struggles and imprisonments, found joy in swimming against the current, and while still in the Congress became the Hero of Vaikom, raised his voice against caste fanaticism at the Cheranmadevi Gurukulam, fought for communal representation, and, when his resolution on communal rights was not accepted, left the Congress in 1925 and began the Self-Respect movement.

The struggle for women’s rights—the struggle against Hindi domination—the struggle for the abolition of caste—the agitation against superstition—the cry for equality and egalitarianism: because in all these he stood forth as **Periyar**, he was honoured with the title Periyar at the women’s conference held in Chennai in 1938.

## Who Am I?

When Periyar spoke of himself, he said:

“I, E. V. Ramasamy, am one who has undertaken the service of reforming Dravidian society and making it a society with honour and knowledge like the other societies of the world, and I remain engaged in that work.

“Whether or not I possess the ‘qualification’ to perform that service, because no one else in this country has come forward to do that work, I have taken it upon myself and continue to serve.

“Apart from this I have no other attachment; and because I formulate my principles and plans with rationalism alone as their foundation,

<!-- Tamil source: scan 224 / printed 223 -->

I consider myself qualified for that service.

“I believe this is enough for one who performs social service.”

Even today, these words can be seen at the Periyar memorial. That “testament,” together with his handwriting, can also be found in the great and rare work `Periyar E.V.Ra. Sinthanaigal`, published with friend Anaimuthu as editor.

When Arignar Anna fell ill and went to America for treatment, he wrote to Periyar from a New York hospital:

“Most beloved Periyar,

“Greetings. My health is improving well. The sight of you at the Chennai hospital and at the airport, filled with anxiety and distress, still appears before me. That is why I am informing you that the condition that gave cause for worry has completely passed. My thanks for your affection. I was deeply troubled when I saw that, in an article for your birthday issue, you had written in discouragement that you might become an ascetic and withdraw. Your work has given society a great awakening. A new path has become available to the people. As far as I know, no other social reformer has achieved such a great victory—especially in our country. Therefore there is not the slightest need for you to feel weariness or anxiety.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 225 / printed 224 -->

Though the year 1949 separated the father and the son, their attachment to the principle of self-respect—and their firm goal that Tamil Nadu should flourish as a land of rationalism, a cultured land—brought the two together again in 1967.

Though neither is with us today, they dwell in our hearts in the form of their principles. Merely thinking of them gives us, of itself, the ability to find a taste even within whatever burden may lie upon our hearts.

Let us act with this thought.
