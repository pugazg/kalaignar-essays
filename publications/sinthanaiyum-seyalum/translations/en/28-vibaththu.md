# Accident

<!-- Tamil source: scan 123 / unit marker only -->

Accidents take place every day. I do not wish to make politics out of this. Nor should it be done. Even if they cannot be eliminated altogether, government departments and officials can at least take an interest in reducing the number of accidents to some extent.

Most accidents occur during journeys. It can be clearly understood that among their causes are the lack of forethought on the part of those driving the vehicles and needless haste.

Those travelling to party conferences, festivals and meetings; those going to temples, temple tanks and festivals to worship gods and fulfil prayers; or brides and bridegrooms travelling for a wedding, along with children going joyfully, leaping and playing beside them—when, in a single second, yes, in just one single second, they meet with an accident and are laid out in rows as corpses, and newspapers and television programmes show us those scenes, we close our eyes without even realising it. We are shaken.

“What use is there in travelling with all possible caution? When fate is like that, can it be changed?” Such familiar consolations from olden times—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 124 / printed 123 -->

coated with a Sanatana varnish—never fail to hide in the background of every accident!

When there are ways to remain alert, act alertly and avoid accidents, in what way can it be called wisdom if, through negligence or disregard, we expose ourselves to harm?

A large share of accidents take place at night, affecting poor and simple farmers and working people travelling crammed together in lorries.

Middle-class people travelling in buses become victims of accidents because of the carelessness of the drivers who operate those vehicles.

Even cars carrying four or five people overturn or collide and are smashed, taking away their lives. Even as I write this, the news being reported in the daily papers and on television is this: the family of Coimbatore industrialist Arulraj, including two young children aged four years and eight months respectively, were returning after visiting the Velankanni temple when, at about one in the night near Tiruchi, their car crashed terribly into a stationary lorry. Except for the four-year-old boy, for everyone in that car it became their final journey.

The accident occurred at one in the night. Whoever may be travelling out of town by car, when they come to take leave of me I always say, gently but firmly: “Please leave only after daybreak.”

<!-- Tamil source: scan 125 / printed 124 -->

Those driving cars must not forget the danger of taking up driving after eating a meal, or trying to drive cheerfully after drinking spirits. And however cautiously car drivers themselves may travel, buses and lorries coming from the opposite direction may regard themselves as elephants and the approaching cars as cats, caring nothing for any traffic-control rules.

Even if a long-distance journey is made during the day, there should be two drivers, including an alternate driver.

In foreign countries, traffic police remain highly watchful along the roadsides. They are present in sufficient numbers. If an accident happens somewhere on the road, an “ambulance” will arrive at the spot within the next few seconds.

There, vehicles are driven fast while accidents are avoided; here, our condition is such that accidents can be avoided only if vehicles go slowly.

Only now are four-lane and six-lane roads gradually beginning to be laid.

Government departments will attend to them. In the meantime, it is we who must remain alert in order to save ourselves from accidents.

Acting with prudence is among the most important ways of avoiding an accident.

6 - 9 - 2005
