# Spirit of Service

<!-- Tamil source: scan 145 / unit marker only -->

How does a volunteer become a leader? The answer to this question is this: because a volunteer's spirit of service continues, he is able to become a leader.

Under number 32 in “Thought and Action,” I pointed to Nelson Mandela's youthful spirit of service, desire for public welfare, awakening of racial consciousness and disposition toward sacrifice. None of these arose from a goal of becoming a leader. I pointed them out precisely to show that his natural disposition to serve and his acts of sacrifice were what made Mandela a great leader!

People with such a spirit of service have risen as leaders both from distant foreign countries and from states close to us within our own country—from neighbouring states too.

Even after rising, they did not abandon that spirit of service; they radiated light and showed the way.

Young people must read and understand the events of their youthful lives and learn to walk along that path. Those heroes of history may today—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 146 / printed 145 -->

no longer be alive; they may have become pictures, statues and memorials. But the history of courage, sacrifice and intellectual revolution they left behind is like the scars seen on a coconut tree: things that must be carefully used by us—by our young people.

When a young coconut tree is planted, one or two fronds appear; later they fall one by one. As fronds keep falling, the tree appears taller; as mature fronds fall one by one, the tree's height increases. The mature fronds that fall along the way become palm-leaf strips; they become shelter for poor people's homes. Green fronds too are sometimes cut for customary and other occasions. Is it not all of public benefit?

Even though a frond has disappeared, so long as the tree itself has not vanished, the scars it left will not vanish. More than that: to climb to the crown of the coconut tree to pluck tender coconuts or coconuts, those scars become steps on which the feet can be planted. Just as scars become steps, elders who have completed their lives—martyrs, heroes—become historical foundations for young people engaged in constructive work. Just as scars are made into steps, young people must make their histories into guides.

Whether or not I who write this shine as a leader, from my youth I have understood the uses given by coconut fronds that have not fallen, the uses given by fallen fronds, and the scars left by fronds that have disappeared. I am one who has not lost the spirit of service. I know that without `Maanamigu`—honour and self-respect—`Maanbumigu`—Honourable—has no value.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 147 / printed 146 -->

It is Periyar whom we can praise as a fruit ripened through service. Trying to ripen while still unripe would become greed—and then disappointment too, would it not? It is because that fruit of rationalism served through every stage, waiting to become flower, tender fruit, unripe fruit and then ripe fruit, that it serves humanity both as a feast of many tastes and as useful medicine. It has also created successors.

Celebrating his birthday, and celebrating the birthday of Arignar Anna—who said every day he worked together with him was among the finest days of his own life, and who turned young people into spear-points—is not a political demonstration. It is a proclamation of ideals bursting forth like a swelling spring from the very foundation of the heart!

A spirit of service acquired in youth creates idealists in just this way—as leaders, as presidents of countries, as guides!

Therefore, young man! Keep blowing like the wind. As a breeze, you will give comfort to the people! Become a storm as well, and knock down and cast away evils!

15 - 9 - 2005
