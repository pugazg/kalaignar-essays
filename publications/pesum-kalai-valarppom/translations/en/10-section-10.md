# 10

<!-- Tamil source: scan 45 / printed 43; folio suppressed/inferred -->

Just as a speaker must pay attention to the subject and to the style of speaking, he must also take great care that the words he pronounces are free of error.

Merely because both “ல” and “ள” belong to the idaiyinam class, however passionately one asks “அவர்கள் என்ன சொன்னார்கள்?” if it comes out as “அவாகள் என்ன சொன்னார்கல்?”, it will sound like an off-note among the people. There are many speakers who interchange the “ல” and “ள” sounds in this way.

“கண்ணனைக் காணச் சென்ற குசேலன், கையில் அவள் கொண்டு சென்றான்.”

Did you notice how many errors there are? When “அவல்” becomes “அவள்”, what a distortion results!

Speakers must come forward to correct the major fault of pronouncing “ல” as “ள”, “ள” as “ல”, “ன” as “ண”, and “ண” as “ன”. Do we not see people with a stammer, through tireless effort, acquiring the ability to present good ideas in an orderly way? Therefore, if speakers persist in trying to correct their pronunciation errors, success will not fail to come!

Some speakers make a serious mistake because they imagine they are stating what they want to say with force and precision.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 46 / printed 44 -->

“If I were to speak about our language, heat and flavour rise through all our veins and nerves.”

In this, they put more pressure than necessary on the “ச்” in “பற்றிச்”. While speaking they unnecessarily insert the “ச்” in “சொல்ல வேண்டுமென்றுச் சொன்னால்”. They pronounce “நாடி நரம்பு” as “நாடி நறம்பு”, gritting their teeth, and heavily stress “நறம்புகளில்”. All these make for defective speeches.

I want to mention an amusing incident. It was in 1957, when I had won the Kulithalai constituency and was sitting in the Legislative Assembly as a member under the leadership of Arignar Anna! If a member uses words contrary to the traditions of the House, another member may raise a point of order and bring it to the Speaker's attention. The Speaker decides whether it is a point of order or not, and may advise the member who spoke contrary to convention, or order those remarks removed from the Assembly proceedings.

1957—it was a time of fierce conflict between the Congress and the D.M.K. There was one Congress member who would speak very rapidly in the Assembly condemning the D.M.K. I was very angry with him. Not personally! Only because of the way he spoke! One day I kept waiting for a chance to trap him in the House. He was called to speak. He had precisely this ல/ள pronunciation problem.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 47 / printed 45 -->

“Take the D.M.K.! Take what they say! For example, take ‘திருவல்லுவர்’.”

He kept speaking like this! I immediately interrupted: “Honourable Chair! A point of order!” The Speaker then was Dr. Krishnarao—a golden-hearted man! He spoke childlike Tamil. “What point of order?” he asked.

“The member who is speaking keeps saying ... ‘kollungal! kollungal!’—using such violent words! Is that proper?”

I asked this and sat down. At once Speaker Krishnarao looked at the member and warned him, “Mr. ...! Do not speak violently!” Everyone, including the ministers seated opposite me, burst out laughing. The member was unbearably embarrassed! I considered it a great victory for myself.

Tamil's distinctive “ழ” sound too suffers terribly in the mouths of some speakers!

They even pronounce “Tamil” as “Tamish”! Intending to say “வாழு! வாழவிடு”, they say “வாலை! வாலைவிடு”.

As the saying goes, “Drawing comes from practice of the hand—Senthamizh from practice of the tongue.” With effort, such faulty pronunciation can be removed. Those who wish to earn a name as speakers must pay special attention to pronunciation.
