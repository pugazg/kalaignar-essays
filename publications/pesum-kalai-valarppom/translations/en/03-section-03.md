# 3

<!-- Tamil source: scan 16 / printed 14 -->

Our forebears divided Tamil into three: iyal—music—koothu. Countless years ago, in that age when human beings roamed naked and later tied leaves and foliage around themselves, covering only the organs below the waist, it could not possibly have been that iyal came first, music second, and koothu third! Iyal is based on<!-- Tamil source: scan 17 / printed 15 --> words; music is based on sound! Koothu is based on movement!

In the beginning, human beings had no knowledge of language at all. When a person saw a stone or a tree, a mountain or a river, he gave none of them any name. So there was no need for words to arise. It was only through gestures that people began to make things understood to one another. Waving their hands, nodding their heads, they lived like mute people.

After that, they made one kind of sound to call someone far away and another kind of sound to make someone nearby turn toward them. Through sound-signals such as “ஏ” “ஓ” “ஈ”, they communicated with one another. Only as a third stage did human society begin using words suited to the respective regions in which it lived!

Even though iyal now stands first among iyal, music and koothu, in the earliest period, after humankind had appeared but before it had grown and undergone many changes, people lived through movements—gestures, and so koothu occupied first place then. Music, based on sound, was then—and is now—in the middle. Iyal, which had been third, came and sat in first place because it operates on words—and those words became necessary for music and likewise necessary for koothu.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 18 / printed 16 -->

Only by choosing and using those words can one skilfully handle both the art of writing and the art of speaking.

Choosing words does not mean keeping a dictionary, memorising words, and trying to work out how to fit them into our speech!

By reading many books, newspapers, weeklies and monthlies, new words become familiar to us. When we speak, they should come of their own accord and fall into place. We should not climb onto a stage and stand there searching for suitable words sentence by sentence. To some extent, we ourselves must be a dictionary. Even if we cannot be a great dictionary, we must know many words.

A speaker does not need to understand enough words to write a commentary on a work of Sangam literature. I will not say that a speaker must know all those words for that. If speakers know them too, they can shine as still more powerful speakers. Not only that: in the expanding world of knowledge, new words are being born every day. It is the duty of a speaker to know them all.

In earlier days, however great the speakers or leaders, once they mounted the stage they would spend a few minutes on a preamble before speaking.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 19 / printed 17 -->

“There may be faults and shortcomings in what this humble one is about to say. If so, I ask you, members of the audience: just as the swan, when milk and water are mixed, drinks only the milk and leaves the water, take only the merits in my speech and leave the shortcomings.

Speeches in the old days used to be like this. When I first began speaking on stage, I too used this same preamble with another comparison.

“Members of the audience! Just as, if sugar and sand are mixed, the ant leaves the sand and eats only the sugar—and just as, if iron filings and wood dust are mixed, the magnet draws only the iron filings—I ask you likewise to cast aside the faults in my speech and accept its merits.”

Back then, I thought I had done something novel simply by replacing the swan and milk in this way!

Even now, when young speakers mount the stage to speak, after greeting the chair and the audience, if they begin, “I, who am not mature in science and experience, ask you to forgive me if there are faults and shortcomings in what I say,” the audience will certainly feel affection and fondness for that young person.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 20 / printed 18 -->

Many young people mount the stage after memorising a speech. If even a small interruption occurs while they recite what they have learned in a fluster, they stand bewildered and try to begin their memorised speech all over again from the start! Do not load their brains with weighty matters—place in them only the matters they know from current affairs. Only then will they themselves understand what they are speaking about.

“Like Socrates, the wise elder of Athens—like Karl Marx, who sowed the seed of thought for the garden of communism—like Ingersoll, who found light in darkness—the lion of self-respect, Thanthai Periyar, shone.”

For a young speaker who memorises what has been written out like this and speaks on a stage, should he not know who Socrates is? Should that young speaker not understand Marx and Ingersoll as well?

Therefore, a young speaker speaking at a Periyar function should be taught only what can be made to stick in his mind.

“Periyar laboured to abolish castes! Periyar rejected the claim that human beings were born as one caste from Brahma’s face, one caste from his shoulder, one caste from his thigh, and one caste from his feet, and thundered that all are one clan—the human clan.”

Only when a young speaker is trained in this simple way, in a simple style, can the words his lips<!-- Tamil source: scan 21 / printed 19 --> utter be connected to his heart. Only when that connection exists can he express those ideas with feeling.

A victorious Legislative Assembly member once took me to a felicitation function in his constituency. Speaking at the first event, he said: “People of the constituency! During the election I had given you a big promise. Namely, that if you made me win, I would bring Kalaignar! Now I have brought him and fulfilled the promise!”

Next, when I spoke, I said, “The promise that I would be brought here is not such a big one! It is only a cheap promise! I am always meeting the people.”

Later, another meeting at another place in the same constituency! The Legislative Assembly member welcomed me and spoke there too. Do you know what he said?

“People of the constituency! During the election I had given you a cheap promise. That was the promise that, if you made me win, I would bring Kalaignar!”

Those near me were startled as soon as he said that. I kept smiling. That was what came of the member thinking that he too should use the very word I had used with a sense of<!-- Tamil source: scan 22 / printed 20 --> humility at the first meeting in that constituency!

He did not even understand the meaning of the word ‘cheap’! What was in the mind was one thing! What came out on the lips was another!

From this, is it not clear that two things are essential for speakers—knowing many words, and keeping a connection between the heart and the words they utter?
