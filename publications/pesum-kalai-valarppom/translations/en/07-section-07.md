# 7

<!-- Tamil source: scan 34 / printed 32 -->

A speech must suit one's appearance and one's age—that too is not an idea to be neglected! I want to mention here, for two different points, two plays staged about thirty years ago and advertised as being held under the leadership of Arignar Anna and in my presence.<!-- Tamil source: scan 35 / printed 33 -->

One was a play in North Chennai, produced and staged by party comrades. A man who works for the poor raises the flag of revolt against the head of the local mutt. With the poor following him, this ally of the poor enters the mutt and angrily addresses the head of the mutt seated on a golden throne:

“Look at our working class, worn to the bone by labouring and labouring; must our sweat run like a river so that heads of mutts like you, eating, puffed up and bloated, may grow fat?”

He delivers the speech like this. At the very point when the audience should have been moved and applauded, everyone gathered in the hall to watch the play burst into mocking laughter. Anna looked at me with a smile. I struggled to hold back my laughter.

Do you know why lines that ought to have won applause drew ridicule instead?

The actor playing the head of the mutt at whom the ally of the poor said, “You lie there eating—puffed up—bloated!” had a body like a cluster bean. He appeared stooped and shrunken, all skin and bone. But the man who thundered, “We have laboured and laboured until we are worn to the bone,” looked hefty, with a paunch and belly, and appeared to weigh about three hundred pounds. Either the lines should have been changed, or at least the actors should have been changed to suit the roles.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 36 / printed 34 -->

Such mistakes occur on speaking platforms too. If a stout man, speaking about poor labourers, includes himself among them and says:

“Look at our famine-stricken eyes. Look at our stomachs, shrunk and groaning with hunger. Look at our dry lips.

Come! Let us find a way to end the poverty that further torments our withered bodies!”

then those in the gathering will forget the feeling in the speech and begin commenting on the speaker's body.

One should speak as an advocate for the poor and simple; even if the speaker is not wealthy, including himself among those with famine-stricken eyes will not produce the proper effect.

Speakers whose bodies are so frail that a blade of grass could trip them may captivate a gathered crowd by describing Alexander's power—Napoleon's fearless heart—the heroic history of the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas. But those speakers must string their words so as to display the firmness of their hearts; if instead they exaggerate their own physical strength, the people will only take it as a joke and will not become one with the speaker's feelings.

As with appearance, so with age! Speech must suit one's age. People may praise speech beyond one's years merely for form's sake, but they will not give those ideas the respect they deserve.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 37 / printed 35 -->

“Grandmother Avvai composed the Aathichudi. We must not forget Avvaiyar's counsels such as ‘Desire to do virtue’ and ‘Let anger cool,’ not only in our youth but in the future as well.”

When a little girl or boy speaks like this, it sounds natural. But if the same child says:

“There was not just one person called Avvaiyar! There were others too. The Avvai who sang the poems found in the Purananuru was different!—the Avvai who sang the Aathichudi was different! We must not confuse the two Avvaiyars and think they were one!”

and then recites a Purananuru poem as well, it will sound neither natural nor as though the child understands what is being said.

When young people speak what only adults can speak—and when adults too overstep their bounds and speak what only the aged can speak—such speeches will not be pleasing!

“Getting married itself is foolishness! If a man and a woman like each other, let them simply live together properly. If they don't like it, cancel it and let them go their ways. What sanctity is there in this thing called marriage—vengaayam!”

In Tamil Nadu, only Periyar could speak like this before thousands of people—with the bride and groom, their relatives and friends sitting right there on the stage!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 38 / printed 36 -->

His age—his labour—his sacrifice—his tireless service—and, beyond ideology, even the respect that theists had for him—all these stood by him. So when he spoke against marriage in the very house where a wedding was taking place, even those who did not accept the idea enjoyed it cheerfully without showing resentment. If anyone else, thinking he is speaking like Periyar, speaks on stages in that same ‘one cut, two pieces’ style, those seated before him will only frown.

Perunthalaivar Kamarajar did not have the habit of arranging words into elegant Tamil sentence structures. But the way he approached the people's problems through his speech was distinctive. People listened eagerly to his long speeches because he was a leader speaking for them. His age, capacity for action, sacrifice, labour and influence—all these laid the foundation for his speech. Therefore, the people did not consider even the great simplicity of his speech a fault; they accepted what he said with satisfaction. If any speaker complains, “I spoke like Perunthalaivar Kamarajar; why didn't the people listen eagerly?” our answer is simply this: that speaker has not grown into Kamarajar!
