# 18

<!-- Tamil source: scan 75 / printed 73; folio suppressed/inferred -->

The distinctive manner or individual style called “mannerism” can take hold of speakers too. As they speak, distinctive bodily gestures and unavoidable habits begin without their even realising it; later, those very things become characteristics peculiar to those speakers.

Some stand on the stage without dancing or moving, pouring out ideas like a waterfall.

When Arignar Anna spoke, he would turn his body to the right and left. His hands would rise and fall slightly! He had the habit of taking snuff. One hand would be inside the side pocket of his shirt. When the people, immersed in the flavour of his speech, applauded and cheered, he would, without anyone noticing, take snuff from the box in his shirt pocket and push it into his nose, then wipe with his upper cloth as though he were wiping sweat, and continue speaking.

Thanthai Periyar, in his later years, spoke only while seated! I was one of those who had gone to meetings with him during the period when he spoke standing. He would wrap his body in a shawl—sandal-coloured, or coffee-coloured, sometimes a mix of white and yellow. While speaking, he would pull the shawl tight around himself.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 76 / printed 74 -->

Thalapathi Alagirisami, tall in stature with an upright gaze, would swing his arm again and again like a sword raised against an enemy on the battlefield, and words would fall in rhythm with it, making the people float in emotion.

When Jeeva, with his somewhat stout body, large moustache and loud voice, spoke, the stage would tremble. There was great speed in his bodily movements. Only when he spoke with vigorous body movement would words fall from him without hindrance.

Frequently jerking his neck to one side and brushing aside his thick moustache were signs that the speech of Silambu Selvar Ma. Po. Si. had begun to heat up.

Rajaji had the skill of arranging argument upon argument while repeatedly extending his hand toward the people as though bestowing grace.

Raising his heels, shaking his shoulders, speaking as though a sea-sluice had been opened, and then suddenly drawing out certain words in a thin voice to win the people's response—this was the distinctive style of Navalar Nedunchezhiyan.

Standing before the microphone with his head held high—never lowering it even once—and raising his hands toward the sky while raining down words may be called a distinctive manner seen in Professor Anbazhagan.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 77 / printed 75 -->

Such artificial styles that have arisen naturally in each person reach a stage where even they themselves cannot give them up!

There is no need for us to practise some bodily gesture or antic merely because we think we must have one when we speak. For some people, bodily antics become such an obstruction that the people fail to pay attention to the speech itself.

Some stand before the microphone scratching their heads as they speak—twisting a hand as they speak. Some scrape dirt from their neck with their fingers as they speak, roll the collected dirt into a little ball, sniff it at their nose, and then drop it down. All this happens while they are still speaking.

Speakers must never, even by mistake, learn such styles or bodily habits.

As far as possible, it is better to speak on stage without excessive movement, keeping bodily antics under control.

Chewing words—biting them and spitting them out—falls gratingly on the ears of listeners.

Thinking they are saying “The police beat and drove away the protesters” with force, precision and anger, some speakers bite and fling out the words “The police beat and drove away the protesters.” Such speech too will not be enjoyable.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 78 / printed 76 -->

Among heads of mutts who used foreign-language words and Vedas and puranas in delivering upanyasams, Kundrakudi Adigalar was one who displayed skill in platform speaking suited to the time and occasion, using good Tamil and quoting literature gracefully.

When Tamil-thendral Thiru. Vi. Ka. thundered from platforms for the freedom struggle, for literary renaissance, for Tamil rights and for spreading religious principles, he made it a habit to repeat certain important words three times with force and precision.

Maraimalai Adigalar, who worked to spread Tamil principles and opposed Hindi domination, used only pure-Tamil words in his speeches.

In the speeches of Navalar Somasundara Bharathiar—who had the appearance of a fighting bull and tirelessly took part in the struggle against Hindi domination—the scolding expression “Ada saniyane!” would frequently come falling out.

Even after pouring forth words at countless meetings, Muthamizh Kaavalar Ki. Aa. Pe. Viswanatham still writes small notes in sequence, keeps them in hand, and with their help remains a speaker who captivates the people.

Pasumpon Muthuramalinga Thevar, who looked utterly gentle at first sight, would become a lion's roar when he began speaking in anger.

Chinna Annamalai made people laugh simply by telling short little stories.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 79 / printed 77 -->

The late Tamilvaanan, a good writer, also possessed the skill to bring a gathering under his sway.

Though they did not have the habit of speaking frequently at meetings, some writers like Jayakanthan would say something new in their speech and create a lively stir among those assembled. Quaid-e-Millath, who presented ideas in carefully chosen, lucid words, and Abdul Samad, who learned by staying close to him and delivered speeches sweeter than honey, earned renowned places on the speaking platform.

Those who wish to shine as speakers must closely observe how people from so many different fields spoke—and how they speak.
