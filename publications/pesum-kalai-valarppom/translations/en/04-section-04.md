# 4

<!-- Tamil source: scan 22 / printed 20 -->

If the heart suffers from a famine of words, stage speech cannot captivate the people. Good ideas arise in the minds of some, but they struggle without the right words to express them. Others may have both excellent ideas and the words to express them filling their minds. Yet they may still lack the power of speech needed to make use of such an opportunity.

Many senior scholars who have researched several languages and earned degrees have failed because they could not deliver a lecture in a way that drew in the audience seated before them. A speaker may deliberately practise at first how to raise and lower the voice to suit a speech, and what kinds of words to use;<!-- Tamil source: scan 23 / printed 21 --> as time goes on, these habits will come naturally.

A beginner fails on the very stage where he thinks he must speak at length! He should leave the audience or the general public thinking, “Goodness! Couldn't he have spoken a little longer?”

The moment one learns to speak beautifully for five minutes, one must not nurture a desire to speak at a conference where fifty thousand people have gathered. At such conferences, one should closely observe what the leaders and the front-ranking members of those parties say, and how they attract the people.

More important than how many hours a speaker spoke is what he spoke about.

When I was a young speaker, I once spoke at a meeting in Kudanthai. As I remember, the meeting was chaired by the elder K. K. Neelamegam of Kudanthai. Arignar Anna had come to deliver an address. I too was asked to speak. I spoke for only a very short time and won the applause and enthusiastic cheers of the people.

“They say Chinna Annamalai of the Congress Party spoke at a meeting! He, they say, is a malai—a mountain! Anna, they say, is a durai—a lord! Only if rain falls on the malai will it come to the durai, they say! So Annamalai is above Annadurai! To Annamalai, who spoke like this,<!-- Tamil source: scan 24 / printed 22 --> I would say: here with us is the megam—the cloud—needed for the rain that must fall on that malai! That is K. K. Neelamegam!”

As soon as I said this—applause! Cries of joy! I stopped my speech right there and earned a “Sabash.”

At meetings, people sometimes clap in appreciation! Sometimes they clap to tell the speaker to finish! I want to point here to an occasion when the K. K. Neelamegam I just mentioned delivered the welcome address at a conference held in Kudanthai.

The elder Neelamegam, with a fine physique, an upright gaze and firmness of principle, had written out his welcome address and read it. People do not listen with interest to long speeches that are read from writing. And that day his speech was very, very long! The afternoon meal hour was approaching too. In his stack of pages, after page 25 he skipped page 26 and read page 27. The thread of the speech broke; mocking laughter and applause rose in the conference pavilion. K. K. N. became furious!

“Whether anyone listens or not! As long as even one person remains in this pavilion, I will read my speech to the finish.”

After he roared like this, silence settled over the pavilion. I recalled this incident only as an example to show that even speeches read from writing<!-- Tamil source: scan 25 / printed 23 --> must be read carefully once or twice beforehand, their page numbers marked correctly and the sheets arranged in order, and then delivered with the voice raised in a way that attracts the people.

Many years ago, an actor who had gained some fame in the world of drama and cinema came with me to Arani to speak at a public meeting. There was a good reception when he was announced as a speaker. At the beginning he poured out words as though a sea-sluice had been opened. His mind must have been somewhere else! He had not become one with the ideas he was placing before the people. As he went on speaking, he suddenly stopped, bewildered. He stood like that for more than a minute. Abandoning the thread of the subject he had been speaking about, he said, “With this I conclude my speech,” and sat down.

What do we understand from this? Only if a speaker has truly merged inseparably with the ideas he is trying to tell the people can he speak without stumbling or obstruction! If he lets his mind wander somewhere else while merely moving his lips before the gathering, it will not become speech filled with feeling!

Anna deserves the credit for creating an age when people came to hear a speech as though they were coming to listen to music!

More than thirty years have passed; it had been announced that Anna would speak in the Chetpet area of Chennai.<!-- Tamil source: scan 26 / printed 24 --> I, poet Kannadasan, Arangannal and Mullai Sakthi set out in a car toward the place. It must have been around eight at night. We did not know exactly where the meeting was being held. We stopped the car by a betel-and-areca-nut shop and asked, “Ayya! Where is the meeting being held here?” A man standing at the shop entrance peeling and eating a banana looked at us and replied, “That one? The Annathurai kachcheri, isn't it? Turn this way to the left and go!” He showed us the way.

They thought of a speech itself as a musical kachcheri. In 1953, when I was in Trichy prison after taking part in the Kallakudi agitation, an ordinary prisoner there looked at me, folded his hands and said, “Namaskaaranga!” I asked him, “Do you know me?” “Oh! I know you very well! Three months ago you came to Manapparai for a kachcheri, didn't you?” said that prisoner! Is it not a joy even to think how far Tamil Nadu has advanced from that stage when meetings themselves were thought of as kachcheris? Those who wish to become speakers must understand that, to excel in this art of speaking that has advanced so far, they must labour hard.

This is an age in which the address “Agraasanadhipathi avargale!” has changed and the Tamil words “Avai-thalaivar avargale!” ring out! This is an age in which “Sriman avargal!” has changed into “Thiruvalar avargal!” Were writers alone<!-- Tamil source: scan 27 / printed 25 --> responsible for these changes? Were not speakers with love for Tamil responsible too? Is it not because they spoke and spoke at meetings, planting those words in the hearts of the people, that the words became familiar! By reading many Tamil books, and by unfailingly reading good Tamil journals, a Tamil speaker can gain the ability to use Tamil words without stumbling or obstruction.

If speakers merely graze the tips of the grass, at every meeting they will keep grinding the same flour that has already been ground. They do not even pick up and look at the new items of news appearing each day in the journals.

Some speakers prepare one speech and go around for a whole year with it. After that, they prepare another speech and set out again. There is nothing wrong in stating at another place an idea used in one place, or in using a comparison used elsewhere! But only if there are changes in the angle from which they are presented will the speech gain lustre! If the same words—the same sentence structure—are used again and again like a recorded tape, those hearing that speaker for the second or third time will grow tired.
