# 5

<!-- Tamil source: scan 27 / printed 25 -->

Kalaivaanar N. S. Krishnan would conduct a wedding in Chennai every year for one of the boys or<!-- Tamil source: scan 28 / printed 26 --> girls in his family. The wedding celebration would be held on a grand scale. Periyar, Anna and Jeeva would unfailingly attend those weddings. Some poets would attend too.

One poet—I do not wish to mention his name—would find a place each time among those who blessed the couple. Though he called himself a man of progressive ideas, in the name of humour he would mock educated women merely to make the wedding guests laugh.

He would say that when educated women entered the kitchen, they would first wash the brinjals with soap so that the brinjal fry would be clean. He would say that, because germs might cling to the knives used to cut vegetables, they would wash those knives with “Dettol.” The poet had repeated this very joke three times at weddings held in that house.

Once, director Bhimsingh and I were getting ready to leave for the fourth wedding held at Kalaivaanar's house. Bhimsingh was a little late.

“What, Bheem! The wedding must be over by now! Anna will have begun speaking and be ready to leave! Come quickly!” I hurried him.

“Don't rush, sir! Only now that ... poet will be putting soap on the brinjal,” he said mockingly!

<!-- Tamil source: scan 29 / printed 27 -->

This is an example of how undesirable it becomes to say what one has already said—to say it again in the same town—and, worse still, at a wedding in the same house—or at a function!

Some others too embrace failure by repeating what certain speakers have said, hoping to win the same praise from the people by speaking like them!

When attacking the Congress rulers at meetings, I used to describe with a great flood of sorrow the incident in which a man named Punniyakodi, driven by poverty, fell into a well with his family and committed suicide. The people listening before me would shed tears!

Not Punniyakodi and his wife alone: beginning with their eldest daughter, thirteen-year-old Manimekalai, down to a tender one-year-old child, all six children and their parents—eight people in all—became victims of poverty under this rule, I would say, speaking with emotion overflowing. My friend Dr. Ramayya, who attended meetings with me, was eager to speak on stages—and a good friend too!

In the 1962 Legislative Assembly election he contested as a Kazhagam-backed candidate. At an election campaign meeting in his constituency, he seems to have decided to tell the Punniyakodi story and melt the people.

A huge crowd had gathered; it was announced that the candidate would speak! It is always better for candidates to speak briefly, ask the voters for their support,<!-- Tamil source: scan 30 / printed 28 --> and quickly bring the speech to an end! But when my doctor friend saw the crowd, he wanted to speak at length and began the Punniyakodi story.

He piled up the family's poverty—the hardship—the cruelty that drove them to resolve on suicide—and brought them all the way to the well-side. He named the first child Manimekalai, but instead of stopping there he said she was five years old, and described the parents lifting her and throwing her into the well.

He still had to give the ages of five more children! He said the second child was three! The third was two! The fourth was one! When he came to the fifth child ... he faltered, unable to give an age!

Only if he had begun by saying that Manimekalai was thirteen would the arithmetic have worked out. The moment he stumbled over the ages, the people who ought to have been grieving began clapping and cheering.

Not only that: if such incidents are narrated merely as though one were reporting news, the feeling and the awakening one hopes to create through them certainly cannot be created among the people.

Speakers must develop a style of their own. Once people begin comparing them—“he speaks like that man; he speaks like this man”—they will no longer pay attention to the<!-- Tamil source: scan 31 / printed 29 --> speech; they will pay attention to the style being imitated.

“Just as one taps a pot and knows from its sound whether it is cracked or not—one can know from a man's speech whether he is intelligent or not.”

Speakers should fix in their minds this saying of Demosthenes.
