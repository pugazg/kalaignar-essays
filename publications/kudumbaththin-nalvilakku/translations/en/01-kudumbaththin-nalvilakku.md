# The Family's Lamp of Well-Being

<!-- Tamil source: scan 4 / printed page unnumbered -->

I do not think I need to explain in detail how much faith Muslim brothers, or those belonging to other religions, have in the family welfare programme, or how much concern they have for it. Whether they are minorities or followers of other religions, I ask everyone in every religion to rise above religious prejudices, go beyond all such divisions, and attend to this as a national problem.

When Pakistan invaded India, those in Pakistan were Muslims; the Muslims in India, the Muslims in Pakistan—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 5 / printed page 2 -->

without even the slightest thought arising in their hearts that they ought to stand on the side of the Muslims in Pakistan, stood against the Pakistani soldiers with the feeling that India was our country and showed such astonishing valour as to drive Pakistan back. I wish to point this out with a deep sense of gratitude: those who showed that valour were the Muslim people of India. Just as, when the Pakistani invasion came upon India, you set religion aside and laboured with the nation foremost, so too—indeed, what we face today is more frightening than that invasion. Family life is being thrown into disorder, families are multiplying, the population is increasing, and crores of people are born in India every year; their number keeps rising. If that is so, then more than a Pakistani invasion—more than a Chinese invasion—we are creating for ourselves a condition of insecurity within our own country. Therefore, just as much as we care about protecting our country from the troubles caused by invasions, we must likewise accept the great responsibility of ensuring that the country is not damaged by the invasions called ‘births’ that arrive every year in our homes. We must accept that responsibility across religious divisions. I ask everyone to think and act in that spirit.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 6 / printed page 3 -->

Even in Hinduism, many people once regarded practising family planning as sinful. To say even more, the revered Gandhi himself said that such methods should not be used. He said that devices should not be used for family planning—that treatment methods should not be adopted—that mental self-restraint alone was necessary. Even though Mahatma Gandhi himself said this, neither Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, nor Lal Bahadur Shastri, nor Mrs Indira Gandhi, who is Prime Minister today, nor the many people holding governing responsibility who had been Gandhi's followers and had accepted many of his principles, treated what Gandhi said as sufficient in itself.

If India's population growth is to be reduced, we must adopt treatment methods. We must use modern methods—modern techniques. It is in recognition of this necessity that the Government of India and the State Governments have assumed responsibility today and are carrying this work forward rapidly.

So many things were once regarded even in the Hindu faith as “sins.” Yet, in the course of time, things that had been dismissed as sins—however much they were explained at the time, with the one who spoke being reviled, the one who put them forward being ridiculed, the ideas themselves being rejected and thrown into a

<!-- Tamil source: scan 7 / printed page 4 -->

corner—we now see many such ideas accepted within that same Hindu faith and put to use for the good of the country. “Prevent children from being born? Is that something for us to do? Is it not God who gives them? If we prevent that, is it not a betrayal of God Himself?” That is what all our elders used to say. If such elders existed even in towns, even in places touched by a little of the light of civilisation, you can understand how fiercely people in the villages must have opposed this principle—the principle that family planning is necessary.

Many things were considered sins. I myself spoke about one such matter at a meeting, and our friends have even brought it out in book form.* My father, at one time, was afraid even to have his photograph taken. One day a photographer came to my village. When he came and said that he would photograph everyone, many people gathered around him. They warned that having one's photograph taken would shorten one's life—that one should not be photographed. But those in my household who had travelled a little to towns and learnt that civilisation—my relatives—said that there was no danger in being photographed and

* “Social Duty”, a publication of the Tamil Nadu Family Welfare Department.

<!-- Tamil source: scan 8 / printed page 5 -->

held my father, brought him over and seated him, settled him in a chair, and, fearing that he might get up and run away even then, took every precaution around him. They made my mother and me stand beside him and took a photograph. That was the first photograph he ever had taken—and the last. After that he would not agree to another photograph at all. Yet after that photograph was taken he lived for another twenty or twenty-five years, and even longer, before he died. So there was even a belief in the villages that taking a photograph was a sin; that when a photograph was taken, half of our soul went away and hid in that photograph, and that therefore one would die young. We have freed ourselves from all those beliefs and are walking in the civilised world. We must therefore think about the security of the country.

However vigorously we may now implement the family welfare programme in various places, in various States—and in the various districts of our own State, with one district competing against another—and however successfully we may carry it out, the three crores we had have now become four crores! Three crores have become four crores, and

<!-- Tamil source: scan 9 / printed page 6 -->

when that has happened, some may think; some may ask: why should the number of our people's representatives in Parliament fall, when the population itself has increased? But today that number in Parliament is apportioned by looking at the higher percentage. Seen in that way, if our percentage falls by even half a point or one point, even these 39 may be in danger. One or two may be reduced from that as well. We once had forty-one members in Parliament representing Tamil Nadu. Today, the reason 41 has fallen to 39 is that the family welfare programme was implemented here, the population declined, and two seats were reduced in Parliament. Today all of us are troubled by the fear that the number may fall still further. It is not enough merely to remove that fear. On behalf of the Tamil Nadu Government, we are pressing the Delhi Government to restore the 41 seats we had before. We are asking that the number of members in Parliament be apportioned on the basis of the 1951—52 population census. The Central Government must agree to that; it must listen to this united voice of the people of Tamil Nadu, I would say. At the same time as we raise our rightful voice that our representation must be protected from reduction, please let no one forget that we must make still greater progress in family welfare in our own lives—and in the family welfare programme—

<!-- Tamil source: scan 10 / printed page 7 -->

and move forward even more successfully.

If the schemes implemented by a government are to produce successful results—if everyone is to share in the benefit of those schemes—population growth must be dammed back so that people can share in those benefits.

I have said this many times. If sixteen items are divided among eight people, each person can be given two. If a situation arises in which sixteen items have to be divided among 32 people, no one should forget that each person will receive only half. Today the Tamil Nadu Government prepares a scheme for four crores of people and examines the results of schemes in different places. If, by the time a scheme designed for four crores is completed, four crores have become five crores or six crores, everyone will not be able to share in the proper benefit of the scheme. The proportion each person receives will be smaller. The country will therefore be unable to obtain the full benefit. It is with all these calculations in mind that I ask that this family welfare programme be made a success.

The home is the country's first foundation. Only if the lamp of well-being burns in homes—if homes are bright—if there is peace in homes—if those in the home are immersed in happiness—will that be reflected in the country. Today, homes are not like that. Even if there is good employment, even if a family has means and opportunities, it is only when that family has a limited number of children—only when it has two or three children—that the mother and father can have peace of mind; we can see this in every family. If that peace is to exist, the family must be of a measured size. When even families with means and opportunities grow weary at having more than two or three children, if in ordinary families—in the families of common people—in the families of the poor and the simple—the population keeps growing, if children continue to be born in that household year after year, the first child will receive good attention when it is born! When the next child is born, the first child will be left without attention! When the child after that is born, the second child will be left without attention. If there are four or five children, all four or five will be left without attention!

In a poor man's home—in the home of the humble—in the home of a person with an ordinary income—if there are one or two children, both can be properly educated. Both can be given proper nutritious food. Both can be protected from hunger. If their number grows, the children will be

<!-- Tamil source: scan 12 / printed page 9 -->

half-starved, while the mother and father will be fully starved—that is how those families will have to live. Therefore there is no kind of sin in this family planning.

Arignar Anna, speaking at a family-planning function, once said something very beautifully. He said that even God Himself had observed family planning most properly: even Lord Shiva, who is said to be our great god, had only two children—one Pillaiyar and the other Murugan. That is what Anna said. Shiva at least had two children. Pillaiyar had no children. There is no mythology saying that Murugan had children. So when even the gods themselves have carried out family planning properly, it is wrong for us—human beings—to think that we must not carry it out—that carrying it out is a sin. Mythology itself tells us what happened to all the gods who did not practise family planning. What happened to Kuchelan, the father of 21 children? We read in mythology that in the end he went in search of Krishna Paramatma and became Kubera. I ask: will every Kuchelan who arises in every village find a Krishna Paramatma to eat the flattened rice he brings and build palaces and mansions for him!

Likewise, the great sage Narada assumed a woman's form and had sixty children. It was because he had them that he had to lift his tambura and

<!-- Tamil source: scan 13 / printed page unnumbered -->

wander all over the land, we read in mythology; we do not read that he lived properly with those sixty children. So mythology itself gives us a good example. Those who had many children were driven to run about in distress. Parama Sivan, who had only two children, lives a most exalted life in Kailaiyangiri, we read. Therefore I ask those in the Hindu religion to follow our gods at least and involve yourselves vigorously in this family welfare programme.

In matters of national security, just as you have taken a position that rises above religion, I ask all those of the other religions to cooperate with the measures of this Government. Through the earnest efforts of Professor Anbazhagan, who has responsibility for this department, the family welfare programme is proceeding very rapidly and successfully in Tamil Nadu. In that spirit, I ask everyone to extend their cooperation.
